One story worth more time this morning: Nvidia's quarter, and the part of the business that's quietly being rewritten.

What happened. The numbers were enormous. About a hundred and sixteen billion dollars of revenue, a hundred and seven point six billion of it from artificial intelligence chips. Colette Kress, the interim chief executive, also raised the company's growth outlook for twenty twenty-eight, from seventy percent to seventy-five.

But the room was about leadership. Hours before the call, Jensen Huang said in his first public statement that he'd be honored to return if the board asked. On the call, the chairman, Harvey Jones, said the board does not intend to reinstate him. And on the same day, the Journal reported that the Securities and Exchange Commission is investigating the company. That's the Journal's reporting so far. Huang is due to testify to Congress on December first about export controls.

Now the mechanism. Nvidia doesn't only sell chips. Over the past two years it has also financed the companies that buy them: equity stakes in startups and cloud providers, guarantees, commitments to rent back capacity it sold. Critics call it circular. Nvidia's money helps a customer raise more money, the customer buys Nvidia chips, and the revenue shows up on Nvidia's books. It works while demand is real, and ties the vendor to its weakest customers if it isn't.

What changed is the tone. Kress told investors the company will be, in her words, disciplined and selective about supporting other artificial intelligence companies. Finimize reports she had questioned, as finance chief, whether those investments paid off, and that Huang himself had started to trim some commitments. Analysts it cites estimate a deeper retreat could pull fifty billion dollars or more out of the industry's financing.

Who's under pressure. The smaller cloud providers that have leaned on Nvidia's backing to raise debt. And Nvidia itself, which now faces a cheaper rival: Alphabet sells its own chips to outside customers, by one estimate about thirty percent cheaper for some comparable work, and Apple, Meta, Oracle and Anthropic are buying.

What the numbers mean. The company has lost roughly half a trillion dollars of market value since Huang resigned, while the business has never been bigger. The market isn't doubting this quarter. It's pricing the customer who can't raise money without Nvidia's help.

What would have to happen next. Watch the financing market rather than the earnings. If deals like CoreWeave's planned investment-grade bond and the SpaceX chip lease clear easily without Nvidia standing behind them, the retreat is painless. If they start to stall, the support Kress is pulling back turns out to have been holding something up.

One idea before we finish.

In nineteen eighty-three, Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman published a paper built around a woman named Linda. She's thirty-one, outspoken, very bright, a philosophy major who cared deeply about social justice. Which is more probable: that Linda is a bank teller, or that Linda is a bank teller and active in the feminist movement? The second can't be more likely than the first; every feminist bank teller is a bank teller. Yet in several versions of the study, roughly eighty-five percent of people chose it, including graduate students trained in statistics. Tversky and Kahneman called it the conjunction fallacy.

The circumstance is the funny part. They'd expected the effect to vanish once the logic was put in plain sight, so they kept making the question easier, stripping it down until only the two options were left. The error barely moved. Kahneman later recalled the paleontologist Stephen Jay Gould saying that even knowing the right answer, a little man in his head kept jumping up and down, shouting that she couldn't just be a bank teller.

The complication is Gerd Gigerenzer, the German psychologist who spent years arguing they'd misread their own result. In ordinary speech, probable doesn't mean mathematically probable; it means plausible, or fitting the evidence, and people may be answering that question sensibly. And when the same problem is posed in counts, out of a hundred women like Linda, how many are bank teller, the error rate falls sharply. Kahneman replied that the effect survives in many forms. They argued in the journals through the nineties, and neither side conceded.
