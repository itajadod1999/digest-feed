One story worth more time this morning. Nvidia and the five hundred billion dollars.

Here is what was announced. Six firms, Apollo, Blackstone, BlackRock, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs and KKR, are partnering with Nvidia to source five hundred billion dollars of financing for artificial intelligence infrastructure. Jensen Huang said in an interview that he approached only those six, and that none of them turned him down.

The purpose is to create dedicated pools of capital, at scale and at attractive rates, for Nvidia's customers. Note who is being financed. Not Nvidia. Nvidia's buyers. This is seller financing, at the scale of a mid-sized economy.

The mechanism worth understanding is the piece Huang mentioned almost in passing. In some cases, Nvidia may provide residual-value support for up to twenty-five percent of a deal. Residual value is what the asset is assumed to be worth at the end of the financing term. It is the number a lender is least sure about, and with chips it is least sure by a wide margin, because nobody knows what a graphics processor from this generation fetches in five years. By standing behind a quarter of that, Nvidia moves the most uncertain part of the credit off the lender and onto its own balance sheet, which is investment grade and very large. Lower uncertainty, lower cost of capital, more data centers built with Nvidia chips in them.

Which is why the sharpest read on this came from Ben Thompson, who called it a price cut in disguise. Nvidia is not lowering the sticker price. It is lowering the cost of financing the purchase, and paying for that with its own credit. Companies do this when they are protecting a position rather than expanding one, and the position Nvidia is protecting is real. Its biggest customers are all designing their own chips.

Now the part that gets less attention. Look at where the money is coming from. Those six firms are the largest private capital managers in the world, and they are collectively preparing to route trillions of dollars of insurance, retirement and institutional money into this build-out. Two other things landed the same week: the Securities and Exchange Commission exempted data-center bonds from key securitization rules, and Bank of America earmarked two hundred fifty billion dollars for infrastructure lending including data centers. Three separate decisions, one direction. The plumbing that carries ordinary savings into computing capacity is being widened, quickly, in several places at once.

What would have to happen for the headline to mean what it says. The announcement came with very few details on timing or structure, and executives acknowledged that deals already in the works will count toward the commitment. So the first thing to watch is whether five hundred billion is new money or a relabeling of what was happening anyway. The second is the residual-value guarantees, and how large they get. That is Nvidia underwriting the used value of its own product, and it only stays cheap while demand stays where it is.

The second story. Paramount is threatening to leave California, and the threat is really about a clock.

David Ellison told his senior leadership last week that he would begin relocating the company out of California if there was no progress toward settling by October. Georgia, Tennessee and Texas have dangled tax credits. California's attorney general, Rob Bonta, called it blackmail. The company's chief legal officer said you go where you're wanted, then added that Ellison intends to stay committed to California, which tells you how firm the threat is.

The underlying fight is Paramount's roughly hundred and ten billion dollar purchase of Warner Bros Discovery. California and eleven other states sued to block it, arguing it harms consumers. Last week the states won something valuable: the judge set a trial date for March.

That date is the whole story, because of a clause called a ticking fee. When a deal signs but takes a long time to close, the buyer often agrees to compensate the seller's shareholders for the wait. Paramount's ticking fee starts in October and runs at roughly six hundred fifty million dollars a quarter. It is payable in stock, and backstopped by Larry Ellison personally, so it does not drain Paramount's balance sheet directly. But it dilutes, and a March trial means at least two payments before anyone sees a courtroom.

So the exit threat is an attempt to buy leverage back. What California actually wants is structural remedies, which means keeping parts of the combined business separate permanently. What Paramount has offered is behavioral, things like committing to release thirty films in theaters each year. Those are not close to the same thing, and the gap is why there is no settlement yet.

Watch for whether Paramount puts a structural concession on the table. The theater chains want the deal, several Hollywood unions are pushing both sides to talk, and California's governor has reportedly expressed reservations about the suit. Everyone has a reason to settle. The company just has to offer the one thing it has been refusing to.
