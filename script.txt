A rules change worth knowing if you touch condominium lending. As of August third, Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac retired the limited review process for condominium mortgages. Limited review was the fast track, the version where a buyer with a large down payment could get financed without the association handing over a full documentation package. It's gone. Every conventional condominium loan now goes through full review.

And there is a second piece landing behind it. For loan applications dated January fourth, twenty twenty-seven and after, associations will need to put at least fifteen percent of annual budgeted assessment income into replacement reserves, up from the ten percent that has been the standard since twenty ten. Boards that don't get there risk their buildings being classified as non-warrantable, which takes the thirty-year conventional loan off the table for buyers. The Journal reports boards across the country are now facing pressure to raise dues or levy special assessments to comply.

One story worth more time this morning. Private credit found out what its liquidity is actually worth.

Start with the product. Over the last five years the industry took a strategy built for locked-up institutional money and put it in a wrapper aimed at individuals. Non-traded business development companies, interval funds, tender offer funds. The pitch was semi-liquid: you get private credit yields, and you can get out quarterly. By the end of last year that category held about five hundred thirty-four billion dollars, having added roughly one hundred billion in twelve months.

The mechanics matter, because the word semi is doing all the work. A typical non-traded business development company will repurchase up to five percent of its shares in a quarter, at net asset value. An interval fund is usually five percent quarterly as well, and unlike the business development companies it is contractually obligated to offer it. If requests come in above the cap, the board prorates. Everybody gets a slice of what they asked for, and the rest goes to the back of the line for next quarter.

That structure is fine as long as requests stay under the cap. They stopped staying under the cap.

In the fourth quarter of last year, redemptions across non-listed business development companies nearly tripled as a share of beginning-of-quarter net asset value, to about four point seven percent. Among the larger funds, those with more than a billion in net assets, requests jumped better than two hundred percent quarter over quarter. In February, Blue Owl Capital Corporation Two simply closed its quarterly repurchase program, switched to returning capital instead, and in March pushed out roughly thirty percent of net asset value to shareholders, funded by selling assets. Cliffwater's corporate lending fund, which runs north of thirty billion dollars, took requests on about fourteen percent of its shares in the first quarter against a seven percent cap. Blackstone's flagship credit fund, a roughly seventy-nine billion dollar vehicle and the biggest in the category, raised its own limit above the standard five percent to meet demand in the first quarter, and reporting since has described it capping and prorating as requests kept running at multiples of the cap.

Now, what is and isn't happening here. A gate is not a default. In most of these funds the loans are still paying. What broke is not the credit, it's the promise about timing, and the reason is more boring than a credit blowup: when the market started pricing rate cuts, floating rate private credit got less attractive next to public fixed income you can sell on any Tuesday you like. So people asked for their money. And the moment more than five percent of them asked at once, the wrapper did exactly what the documents said it would do, which turned out to be different from what a lot of buyers thought they were getting.

The thing to watch is whether proration becomes self-reinforcing. If you asked for a dollar and got twelve cents, the rational move next quarter is to ask for more than you actually want, because you know you'll be cut. That inflates the request number, which forces more proration, which teaches everyone to inflate again. The funds' defense is that the assets are performing and time solves it. The test is whether the requests level off from here or keep climbing into the fourth quarter.

The second story. Trepp's July delinquency numbers, and why they point the opposite direction from Friday's payoff numbers.

The headline: the commercial mortgage-backed securities delinquency rate rose fifty-one basis points in July, to seven point eight six percent. In dollars, loans thirty days or more late rose about eight percent on the month, to forty-seven and a half billion dollars, out of a universe Trepp tracks at just over six hundred four billion.

Multifamily was the worst of it, up forty-six basis points to seven point six nine percent. That is a number that would have sounded absurd three years ago.

But look at what actually drove the month. This was not a broad deterioration in payment behavior. It was a small number of very large loans changing status, mostly into non-performing matured balloon or into foreclosure. The five largest new delinquencies were a showroom and exhibition space portfolio split between North Carolina and Nevada, two Times Square properties here in New York, a Chicago office tower, and a Seattle office portfolio.

Non-performing matured balloon is the category that matters for how you read this. It doesn't mean the borrower stopped paying interest. It means the loan reached its maturity date and the balloon payment didn't happen. The loan is current on debt service and delinquent on principal at the same time. In a maturity wall, that is the failure mode you see most: not missed monthly payments, but loans arriving at the end of their term and finding that the refinancing proceeds available at today's rates and today's values don't cover the existing balance.

Now put that next to Friday's number from the same firm. The share of conduit loans that paid off on time at maturity in July rose to seventy-two percent, from about sixty-four in June, the best on-time payoff rate in four years.

Both are true, and together they say something specific. The ordinary loan hitting maturity is now, more often than not, refinancing successfully. Spreads have tightened, the lender base is deeper than it was, and the second-quarter origination figures back that up. What is left over is a residue of very large, mostly office, mostly older-vintage credits where the gap between the loan balance and what the asset supports is too wide to bridge with a rate adjustment or a small paydown. Those don't get better with time. They get resolved, one at a time, through modification or foreclosure, and each one is big enough to move a national percentage on its own.

So the aggregate delinquency rate is becoming a worse and worse summary of the market. It's increasingly a count of how many jumbo problem loans happened to change status that month. The number that tells you about lending conditions is the payoff rate, and the payoff rate is at a four-year high.
