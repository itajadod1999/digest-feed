Which brings us to the Gulf.

Start with the geography, because everything follows from it. The Strait of Hormuz is about twenty-one miles across at its narrowest. The shipping lanes inside it are narrower still: two miles inbound, two miles outbound, and a two-mile buffer between them. Before this war, something like a fifth of the oil the world burns each day went through that gap. Iran sits on the northern shore, with islands and a coastline that look straight down onto the lanes. Oman's Musandam peninsula, where Khasab is, sits on the southern tip.

There are ways around it, but not many. Saudi Arabia has a pipeline to the Red Sea, and the Emirates have one to Fujairah, outside the strait. Together they carry a fraction of what normally goes by sea. Kuwait, Qatar, Bahrain and Iraq's southern ports have no bypass at all.

It begins with a war that had nothing to do with shipping. In September nineteen eighty, Iraq invaded Iran. By nineteen eighty-four, the land war had bogged down into something closer to the Western Front, and Saddam Hussein went looking for a way to break it. He started attacking tankers that loaded at Iran's main export terminal, Kharg Island. The aim was partly to cut Iran's oil money and partly to provoke Iran into trying to close the strait, which would drag the great powers in.

Iran responded, but not by closing the strait. It went after the tankers of Kuwait and Saudi Arabia, which were bankrolling Iraq's war. Over the next four years, by the count of the naval analyst Ronald O'Rourke, four hundred and fifty-one ships were attacked in the Gulf, and well over a hundred sailors were killed. Most people remember it the other way round: Iraq attacked two hundred and eighty-three of them. Iran attacked a hundred and sixty-eight.

The first thing that moved was the price of insurance. War-risk cover is written separately from a ship's normal policy, it's quoted voyage by voyage, and underwriters reprice it as fast as the danger changes. A trade journal of the time reported that the Lloyd's market doubled its rates for Kuwait-bound ships in early nineteen eighty-seven. Owners kept sailing, and the cost went into the freight rate.

Then Kuwait made a move that changed the shape of it. In December nineteen eighty-six, it asked both superpowers for protection. The Soviet Union agreed to lease Kuwait some of its own tankers, and that was what concentrated minds in Washington. On the seventh of March nineteen eighty-seven, the Reagan administration agreed to put eleven Kuwaiti tankers under the American flag. Reflagged, they became American ships, and the Navy could escort them as such.

Two months later came a warning. On the seventeenth of May, an Iraqi jet fired two Exocet missiles into the American frigate Stark. Thirty-seven sailors were killed. Iraq called it an accident, and the United States accepted that. The country the Navy was in effect leaning toward had just done the most damage.

The first convoy sailed in late July. The first two reflagged tankers raised American flags on the twenty-first, and on the twenty-fourth, the lead tanker, the Bridgeton, struck a mine near Farsi Island in the northern Gulf. No one was hurt, and she made it to Kuwait under her own power. But the detail that stuck was what happened next. For the rest of the trip, the warships fell in behind the tanker, because a loaded supertanker can absorb a mine and a destroyer can't. The escorts were sheltering in the wake of the ship they were escorting.

The Bridgeton taught the Navy that its problem was mines, and it had almost no minesweepers in the Gulf. It spent the next year improvising, with European navies sending sweepers of their own. In September, American helicopters caught an Iranian ship, the Iran Ajr, laying mines at night, and the Navy seized it.

What they built was called Operation Earnest Will, and at its peak it was the largest naval convoy operation since the Second World War. A government study counts a hundred and twenty-seven escort missions. A Naval War College paper counts a hundred and eighty-eight transits by reflagged tankers alone.

It got bloodier before it ended. On the fourteenth of April nineteen eighty-eight, the frigate Samuel B. Roberts struck a mine about sixty-five miles east of Bahrain. The blast broke her keel. Ten sailors were injured, and the crew kept her afloat. Four days later, the Navy answered with Operation Praying Mantis. In one day it wrecked two Iranian oil platforms, sank the frigate Sahand, a missile boat and several small gunboats, and crippled a second frigate. Two American helicopter crewmen died when their Cobra went down.

And then the worst day. On the third of July nineteen eighty-eight, the cruiser Vincennes, in the middle of a surface fight with Iranian gunboats, shot down Iran Air Flight six fifty-five over the strait, believing it was an attacking fighter. All two hundred and ninety people aboard were killed. In nineteen ninety-six, the United States agreed to pay sixty-one point eight million dollars to the victims' families, without admitting legal liability.

Two weeks after that, Iran accepted the United Nations ceasefire resolution, five ninety-eight. Ayatollah Khomeini said accepting it was more deadly than drinking poison. The ceasefire took effect on the twentieth of August, and the last Earnest Will escort sailed at the end of September.

So did it work? The case for: the reflagged tankers kept sailing. Of the hundreds of ships attacked over four years, the great majority were damaged rather than sunk, and the share of total traffic that was hit was small. The oil price, which had collapsed in nineteen eighty-six, never seriously spiked. The strait was never closed.

The honest counterargument comes in three parts. The first is that the war ended on land. On the same two days as Praying Mantis, Iraq retook the Faw peninsula from Iran, and over the following months, Iraqi offensives, some of them using chemical weapons, broke Iran's army. Iran's leaders gave up because they were losing the war, and the convoys were one pressure among several.

The second is what escorting cost. It made the United States a belligerent in all but name, on the side of a regime that had just hit one of its ships, and it ended with an American cruiser shooting down a passenger jet. The third is that the threat in nineteen eighty-seven was limited. Iran was fighting a much bigger war next door and never committed to closing the strait, partly because it needed it for its own oil.

Which is the real difference with now. Since the war began in late February, with American and Israeli strikes, Iran has had little stake in keeping the strait open. Its own ports are under an American blockade. Its weapons are cheaper and more numerous: drones and anti-ship missiles rather than a handful of mine-laying boats. Back in English, and one idea before we finish.

For more than a century, economists used the lighthouse as their favorite example of something the market can't provide. John Stuart Mill used it in eighteen forty-eight, and Paul Samuelson put it in the best-selling economics textbook of the twentieth century. The logic is tidy: a lighthouse's beam helps every passing ship, and you can't stop a captain from looking at it, so no private owner could ever charge for it. In nineteen seventy-four, Ronald Coase, then at the University of Chicago, published a paper called The Lighthouse in Economics pointing out that nobody had checked.

The circumstance is that Coase went and read the history. In England and Wales, he found, many of the lights of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries had been built by private individuals. They got a patent from the Crown, built the tower, and collected light dues from ships when they called at port, based on the ship's tonnage and route. Some of those owners got very rich. Only in eighteen thirty-six did Parliament give the corporation of Trinity House the power to buy out the private lights, and it took years, and a great deal of money, to do it.

The complication is that Coase's private lighthouses weren't very private. Later historians, among them David Van Zandt in the nineteen nineties and Elodie Bertrand in two thousand six, pointed out that the Crown granted the monopoly, set the dues, and had them collected at ports by the state's own customs officers. A captain couldn't decline to pay. So was it the market providing a public good, or the state providing one through private contractors? Both sides read the same records, and they still haven't agreed what they show.
