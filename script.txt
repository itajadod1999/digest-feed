One idea before we finish.

In nineteen fifty-three, Bernard Kettlewell, a physician turned entomologist at Oxford, set out to test a story everyone half-believed. The peppered moth comes pale and speckled, or nearly black. The dark form was first recorded in Manchester in eighteen forty-eight, and within fifty years it was almost all you'd find there. The theory: soot had blackened the trees, so birds picked off the pale moths.

Kettlewell released thousands of marked moths in a sooty wood near Birmingham, then in clean woods in Dorset. Near Birmingham he recaptured dark moths at about twice the rate of pale ones; in Dorset, the reverse. It went into every textbook as natural selection caught in the act.

The complication came later. Critics noted he'd released moths in daylight, at unnatural densities, onto trunks where they rarely rest. The Cambridge geneticist Michael Majerus spent seven years rerunning it properly, with nearly five thousand moths, and found birds did take more of the conspicuous ones. He died before it was published. Some still argue predation was never the whole story, only the part easiest to see.
