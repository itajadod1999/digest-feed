One idea before we finish.

In May of eighteen forty-seven, Ignaz Semmelweis, a twenty-nine-year-old Hungarian doctor at Vienna General Hospital, put a basin of chlorinated lime at the door of a maternity ward and made the doctors and students wash their hands in it. Deaths from childbed fever, which had averaged around ten percent, fell to one or two.

The circumstance is what got him there. The hospital had two maternity clinics. The one staffed by doctors killed mothers at several times the rate of the one staffed by midwives, and women begged not to be admitted on the doctors' days. Then a colleague, Jakob Kolletschka, cut his finger during an autopsy and died of something that looked exactly like childbed fever. The doctors came from the dissecting room. The midwives didn't.

The complication is the usual telling, that the establishment rejected him out of pride. He didn't publish a full account for fourteen years. His theory named a single cause, matter from corpses, which couldn't explain cases with no autopsy involved, and it came well before anyone had a germ theory to make sense of it. Some contemporaries adopted the washing while rejecting his explanation, and historians still argue over how much of his neglect was other people's obstinacy and how much was his own failure to make the case.
