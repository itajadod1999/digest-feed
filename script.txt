One story worth more time this morning: Weil, Gotshal and Manges, and how a law firm dies.

What happened. The firm's leaders have told partners they plan to dissolve it, according to the Journal, the Times and the Economist. Weil ran the bankruptcies of Lehman, Enron and WorldCom. In a matter of weeks, merger talks with Willkie Farr collapsed, high-profile partners left, the incoming chair turned the job down, and the firm hired bankruptcy counsel of its own.

The mechanism. The Economist's Schumpeter column puts it in a sentence: a law firm's balance sheet is office chairs and brains. A partnership like this pays out nearly all its profit every year and keeps little capital. Its real assets are client relationships, which leave with the partners. But the costs stay put: long leases, associates' salaries, borrowing against fees not yet collected. So every departure raises the share of fixed costs on the partners who remain and lowers their pay, which makes the next departure more rational. It's a bank run where the deposits are people. Dewey and LeBoeuf went the same way in twenty twelve.

The players. Rival firms are recruiting Weil's partners in groups, and clients are deciding where their cases go. Sidley Austin has just hired a banker to lead an out-of-court restructuring practice, on the F T's reporting, which looks like a competitor moving into the gap. Schumpeter's larger claim is that Weil is the first great firm to suffer the death of elite collectivism, the idea that star partners stay loyal to a shared pot, and that it won't be the last.

What would make it matter more. Watch where the restructuring group lands, and whether it lands in one piece. And watch for partners elsewhere testing their own exits, because that is the part that spreads.

One idea before we finish.

In the early nineteen nineties, a young American lawyer named Michael Heller was in Moscow, advising on property reform as the Soviet system came apart. He noticed something odd. The shops along the main streets sat empty, while flimsy metal kiosks crowded the pavement in front of them, doing brisk trade.

The explanation, which he published in the Harvard Law Review in nineteen ninety-eight, was that each storefront had too many owners. One agency could sell, another lease, another occupy, another collect the revenue, and any of them could say no. When too many parties each hold a veto, a resource goes unused. He called it the tragedy of the anticommons.

The same year, with Rebecca Eisenberg, he argued in Science that biomedical research could suffer the same way, as patents on gene fragments piled up and every new drug needed a stack of licences.

That second claim has proved hard to show. A survey of academic biomedical researchers by John Walsh and Wesley Cohen, published in twenty oh five, found very few who said patents had actually stopped their work; most ignored them or worked around them. Heller's defenders reply that the projects most likely to be blocked are the ones nobody ever starts, which is exactly what a survey can't see.
