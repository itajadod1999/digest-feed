One idea before we finish.

On the twenty-ninth of March, nineteen hundred, a thirty-year-old named Louis Bachelier defended a thesis at the Sorbonne called the Theory of Speculation. He was trying to price options on French government bonds traded in Paris. To do it, he treated each day's price change as a random step, and worked out the mathematics of a price wandering that way. In the process he described what physicists would call Brownian motion, five years before Einstein's paper on it.

The circumstance is the strange part. Bachelier came to mathematics late, after his parents' deaths pulled him into the family business. His examiner was Henri Poincaré, whose report was polite, noted the subject was rather far from what candidates usually chose, and gave it a grade below the top one. That grade followed him. In nineteen twenty-six he was turned down for a post in Dijon after Paul Lévy flagged what he took to be an error in later work. The thesis sat largely unread until the nineteen fifties, when the statistician Jimmie Savage came across Bachelier's work and sent postcards to economists asking whether anyone had heard of him. Paul Samuelson had.

The complication is that the model is wrong in instructive ways. His prices could go negative, which Samuelson fixed in the nineteen sixties by making the steps proportional. And in nineteen sixty-three Benoit Mandelbrot, looking at decades of cotton prices, found far more violent jumps than a random walk allows. Finance built on Bachelier's idea has lived with that gap ever since, and nobody has settled how much it matters.
