One story worth more time this morning: what actually broke on Thursday.

What happened. The selling was not across the board, and the pattern is the story. The worst losses came in the companies that need the data center build-out to keep accelerating. Sandisk, the memory maker, fell twenty-two percent, the worst in the S and P. Nvidia fell nine. Tesla fell almost eleven. CoreWeave, the biggest of the so-called neoclouds that rent out computing power, had already dropped nearly ten percent on Wednesday. Meanwhile, the Economist notes, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta and Microsoft ended Thursday roughly where they were before the vote.

The mechanism. Data centers need local permission, and the people who give it just changed. DealBook counts forty of forty-one local ballot measures on data centers going against development. One developer told the paper to expect moratoriums from January and six to nine months of delays. A delay doesn't cancel a contract, but it moves the revenue. CoreWeave reported a hundred and four billion dollars of future contracts last quarter, against about thirty-five billion of debt. If construction slips, the backlog stretches and the debt doesn't.

The players. The neoclouds and the chipmakers need the spending to continue. The hyperscalers, the four giants that already own capacity, may not mind a pause; a slowdown makes what they've already built scarcer, and spending less frees up cash. Microsoft, Finimize reports, had already passed on an option to expand its contract with Nscale in Texas. OpenAI's rebrand is an attempt to win the towns back. What the numbers mean. The telling figure is the gap: chip stocks down twelve while the biggest platforms barely moved. Investors are betting on less building, not less artificial intelligence.

One idea before we finish.

On the nineteenth of October, nineteen eighty-seven, the Dow fell twenty-two point six percent in a single day. Ronald Reagan asked Nicholas Brady, a Wall Street banker who would soon become Treasury secretary, to find out why. His task force reported in January nineteen eighty-eight with a claim that sounded technical and was actually radical: the stock market and the futures market in Chicago were one market, joined by computers, and they had been regulated as if they were two.

The circumstance was a strategy called portfolio insurance. Big pension funds had been promised protection: as prices fell, a model would sell futures automatically. On the day, everyone's model said sell at once, the futures fell below the stocks, arbitrageurs sold stocks to close the gap, and the loop fed itself. Brady's best-known remedy was the circuit breaker, a pause that forces everyone to stop and look. The first market-wide halt in America came in October nineteen ninety-seven; Korea's tripped overnight.

The complication arrived with the first breakers. Merton Miller, the Chicago economist who led the futures exchange's own inquiry, argued the futures market was being blamed for a crash it hadn't caused. And later work, including a study of the Taiwan exchange, found what's called a magnet effect: as prices near the trigger, traders rush to sell before the doors close, which can pull the market toward the very halt meant to calm it. Whether a pause breaks a panic or concentrates it has never been settled.
