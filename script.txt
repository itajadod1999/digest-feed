One story worth more time this morning: a fight over old bonds in the biggest leveraged buyout ever.

What happened. In August, a group led by Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund, with Silver Lake and Jared Kushner's Affinity Partners, closed its roughly fifty-five billion dollar purchase of Electronic Arts, the video game maker. Left over from the old investment-grade company were about one and a half billion dollars of bonds: seven hundred and fifty million at one point eight five percent, due twenty thirty-one, and seven hundred and fifty million at two point nine five percent, due twenty fifty-one. Now the holders, who have signed a cooperation agreement and hired Akin Gump and Houlihan Lokey, say the company is in default. That's on Money Stuff's account.

The mechanism. Most investment-grade bonds carry a change-of-control clause. If the company is bought and its rating falls to junk, holders can demand their money back at a hundred and one cents on the dollar. These bonds were issued in twenty twenty-one when rates were very low, so in today's market they trade far below face value. The put at a hundred and one would hand holders a windfall of several tens of cents on the dollar.

The buyers didn't want to pay that. Their answer was defeasance, an old tool. The company sets aside enough Treasuries to cover every remaining coupon and the final principal, and in exchange it is released from the bonds' covenants, change of control included. Because those coupons are so low, buying the Treasuries to cover them costs much less than par. Money Stuff puts it at about sixty-five cents on the dollar.

The ratings are the other battlefield. The put needs a downgrade to trigger. Moody's withdrew its rating, S and P signaled high yield, Fitch declined to rate, and the company obtained an investment-grade rating from Egan-Jones, a smaller agency. The bondholders argue the defeasance doesn't work under the indenture, and that the put was triggered anyway.

What it means in context. Holders bought investment-grade paper partly for that hundred-and-one protection. If defeasance can neutralize it, every low-coupon bond from the cheap-money years is a little less safe in a takeover.

What would make it matter. Watch whether the holders sue, and whether a court accepts the indenture argument. Watch the Egan-Jones rating, since a lot rests on it. And watch whether other buyers of companies with cheap old debt copy the playbook. If it works once, it won't be the last time.

One idea before we finish.

In nineteen seventy, a young Berkeley economist named George Akerlof published a paper called The Market for Lemons. His argument was that when sellers know more than buyers, the market can unravel. A buyer of a used car can't tell a good one from a bad one, so he offers an average price. Owners of good cars won't sell at that price, so they withdraw. The average quality falls, the price falls with it, and in the limit only the lemons are left.

The circumstance is the best part. Akerlof was trying to explain why new car sales swing so violently over the business cycle. He also spent a year in India, at the Indian Statistical Institute, and used the local moneylenders, who charged enormous rates because they couldn't tell good borrowers from bad ones, as an example. Three journals rejected the paper. The American Economic Review found it trivial. So did the Review of Economic Studies. The Journal of Political Economy said that if it were correct, economics would be different. The Quarterly Journal of Economics took it, and in two thousand and one it won him a Nobel prize.

The complication is the used car market itself. In nineteen eighty-two, Eric Bond tested the theory on used pickup trucks and found no evidence that the ones sold second-hand needed more maintenance than those kept by their first owners. Warranties, dealers' reputations and inspections seem to do much of the work. Economists still argue over how often the unraveling actually happens, and how much the fixes cost.
