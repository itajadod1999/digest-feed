Good morning. Monday, August third. Here's what happened.

Starting with markets.

The dominant trade this morning is still Iran. President Trump said over the weekend that he wanted another attempt at talks with Tehran, that negotiations would resume today, and that the perimeters of a deal had been reached to reopen the Strait of Hormuz. He did not say who would participate. Iran has not confirmed any agreement on the strait, and an Iranian government spokesman said there are no direct talks with the United States at the moment.

Markets took the optimistic side anyway. Brent crude, the international oil benchmark, fell more than four percent to eighty-three dollars and sixty cents a barrel. That follows a climb of more than twenty percent over the course of July. Stock futures rallied and so did bonds. The yield on the thirty-year Treasury bond, which hit a multi-decade high last week, fell to five point two two seven percent. As of about six fifteen this morning, S and P five hundred futures were up half a percent at seven thousand five hundred fifty-seven, Dow futures up six tenths of a percent at fifty-two thousand nine hundred sixty-seven, and Nasdaq one hundred futures up half a percent at twenty-eight thousand five hundred fifty.

Veteran market watchers are split on how much of this to believe. Derek Halpenny, head of global markets research at the Japanese bank MUFG, told Bloomberg Television this morning that he thinks Trump wants a deal and that is why he has gone back to negotiations. Mohit Kumar, an economist at Jefferies, wrote to investors this morning that he expects an uneasy truce to drag on, with violations from Iran and sporadic attacks on ships, and more volatile swings as a result. On Friday, Exxon Mobil and Chevron both warned that fuel prices are likely to stay high for some time if the standoff keeps depleting reserves.

The other big move is the yen. Japan's finance ministry confirmed this morning that it conducted a coordinated yen-buying operation with the United States Treasury on Friday. It is the first time the United States has bought yen in a decade. The yen had weakened past one hundred sixty-three to the dollar earlier this month, its weakest in nearly four decades. It closed New York trading on Friday at one hundred fifty-seven point four zero, the strongest since early May. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said Friday's coordinated action countered disorderly yen movements, and that the United States will not hesitate to participate in further joint intervention.

The tell came before the announcement. At a cabinet meeting on Friday, a Reuters photographer captured a notepad in front of Bessent that read, To Do, Buy Japanese Yen, five to ten billion dollars. Currency desks spent the weekend on that photograph. Andrew Ross Sorkin's read this morning is that the framing of allied assistance is only half the story, and that the operation was a pre-emptive move to protect the United States Treasury market. Bessent, for what it is worth, made a large and profitable bet against the yen at Soros Fund Management more than a decade ago.

For the year, the S and P five hundred is up nine point four one percent, the Dow nine point two zero, and the Nasdaq nine point one seven, though the Nasdaq finished July down three point two percent. The ten-year Treasury yield is four point seven four five percent, up fifty-eight point two basis points on the year.

The July employment report lands Friday and it is the week's real event. June added only fifty-seven thousand jobs, and more Americans left the workforce entirely. Economists expect somewhere between eighty-eight and ninety thousand for July, with unemployment ticking up to four point three percent. The Fed left rates at three and a half to three and three quarters percent last week, with three officials dissenting in favor of a hike. Chair Kevin Warsh has described inflation as a choice that can be actively managed rather than simply endured. A hot number revives the September hike conversation. A weak one lands on a market that has just watched the artificial intelligence trade wobble.

Two things from last week worth carrying forward. The United States imposed new tariffs of ten to twelve and a half percent on roughly sixty trading partners, replacing an earlier round the Supreme Court struck down. The estimated cost is about nine hundred dollars per American household this year. And Nvidia shed nearly two hundred eighty billion dollars of market value in a single day, a record for any American company, after reports that it is in talks to guarantee up to two hundred fifty billion dollars of financing for a large new data center built for OpenAI. That is the circular financing worry in its purest form. The chipmaker helping fund the customer that buys its chips.

Moving to deals and credit.

AstraZeneca shares fell more than five percent in London today after the Financial Times reported that it held talks with Bristol Myers Squibb about combining. The combined company would be worth close to four hundred billion dollars, one of the largest pharmaceutical transactions ever attempted. Bristol Myers shares are up about forty-four percent over the past year, and oncology accounted for more than forty percent of its sales in the first six months of this year. The two companies' cancer immunotherapies compete directly, which is where the antitrust problem starts. Both declined to comment.

In Italy, Banco BPM withdrew its takeover bid for Banca Monte dei Paschi di Siena. That potentially clears a path for a rival offer from Intesa Sanpaolo, or for Monte dei Paschi to turn around and bid for Banco BPM.

Paul Grewal, the former chief legal officer at Coinbase, is joining the artificial intelligence start-up Cognition AI as its first chief legal and global affairs officer. Cognition is the company behind the coding agent Devin. It is valued at twenty-six billion dollars, counts Goldman Sachs and Mercedes-Benz as customers, and reached four hundred ninety-two million dollars in annual recurring revenue in May. OpenAI and Anthropic together spent three point one seven million dollars on federal lobbying last quarter, up twenty-three percent from the prior quarter.

Earnings are dense this week. Palantir and Snap report today. Palantir is down about thirty-one percent this year, at one hundred twenty-three dollars a share. SpaceX reports tomorrow for the first time as a public company, after dipping below its offering price last week, and its lock-up expires Thursday, potentially freeing more than nine hundred million shares. Advanced Micro Devices, Caterpillar, McDonald's and Pfizer also report tomorrow, then Eli Lilly, Novo Nordisk, Disney and Uber on Wednesday, and SoftBank Thursday.

Now real estate.

The largest New York financing of the past week is BXP's construction loan at three forty-three Madison Avenue. One point two billion dollars, on a forty-six story, roughly nine hundred thirty thousand square foot tower with direct access to the Madison concourse at Grand Central. Wells Fargo led, with BofA Securities, Bank of New York Mellon and JPMorgan Chase in the syndicate. The initial term is four years with a one-year extension option, priced at term SOFR plus two hundred fifty basis points, stepping down to plus two hundred twenty-five once certain leasing and construction milestones are hit. The building is about half pre-leased already, on a two billion dollar total project cost, with delivery expected in late twenty twenty-nine.

On the investment sales side, 60 Guilders and Sentry Realty bought fourteen forty-one Broadway for two hundred forty million dollars. That is a five hundred fifty thousand square foot, thirty-three story office building, about ninety percent leased and filled with below-market leases, which works out to four hundred thirty-six dollars a foot. The seller was the estate of L H Charney. Newmark's Adam Spies and Adam Doneger arranged the sale. It is the third acquisition in that immediate stretch of Midtown for the same buyer group, after thirteen seventy-five Broadway and thirteen seventy Broadway.

Elsewhere, Madison Realty Capital led a two hundred twenty-seven million dollar refinancing of Metro Parc, a new rental building in Miami-Dade. Goldman Sachs put one hundred sixteen million dollars into the overhaul of Parkside Commons, an affordable housing property in Syracuse. And Peachtree lent one hundred thirteen million dollars on the conversion of an apartment building into a Margaritaville hotel in Savannah.

Over to France.

Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu said the wildfires burning across the country are now under control, more than a week after the largest French wildfire since nineteen forty-nine flared up. The fires have burned roughly one hundred fifteen thousand hectares this year, and at their peak, they and the fires in Spain forced more than three hundred thousand people to evacuate. Finance Minister Roland Lescure described the damage to the local economy as a clap of thunder for a region that could have done without it.

And the Middle East.

Trump told reporters aboard Air Force One yesterday that he had called off what he described as the biggest attack since the Second World War, at the request of American allies in the region, including Qatar, Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates. He said they told him they would much prefer a deal to an attack, because you do not know where attacks lead. He had spoken with Saudi Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman on Saturday.

Iran's account is narrower. Foreign ministry spokesman Esmaeil Baghaei said Iran is not currently negotiating with the United States. It is negotiating with Oman, and has been for seven or eight days, on the technical management of a temporary safe shipping route through the Strait of Hormuz. Baghaei said progress has been made, but that an agreement with Oman alone would not be enough to reopen the strait while American operations continue.

Inside Iran, the executions continue. Two men were put to death on Monday morning after being convicted of spying and intelligence cooperation with Israel. Human rights organizations warned that more Iranian teenagers arrested during the January protests face execution. And the ILNA news agency reported that households are now selling personal belongings, from hair to perfume bottles, and renting basic work equipment such as laptops, to cover living costs.

A few other things.

Struggling American colleges are covering operating expenses out of restricted endowment funds, money that donors gave for other purposes, without their knowledge or consent. Nearly two hundred private colleges borrowed from restricted endowments in twenty twenty-five, up from one hundred thirty-one in twenty twenty-one, according to estimates from Perspective Data Science. Most of the money went to everyday expenses.

The California Democratic Party voted to endorse a one-time five percent tax on the state's roughly two hundred billionaires, which goes to voters in November. Both Governor Gavin Newsom and Xavier Becerra, the Democratic candidate to succeed him, oppose it.

Google pulled its new Google Earth image generation feature one day after launching it, after researchers demonstrated they could produce convincing fake satellite imagery of refugees at the Mexican border, a nuclear plant in Iran and a bombed hospital in Gaza.

And at the box office, Spider-Man: Brand New Day took an estimated nine hundred twenty-seven million dollars worldwide in its opening weekend, including three hundred fifty-five million domestically, the second-biggest debut ever. Together with The Odyssey, the weekend totaled four hundred thirty million dollars domestically, the biggest in box office history.

That's the morning. Have a good one.
