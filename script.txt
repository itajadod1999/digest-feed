One story worth more time this morning. The United Arab Emirates has cut off all trade with Iran, and that is a larger economic event than the headline suggests.

The sequence first. The Emirati defence ministry said its air defences detected two ballistic missiles fired from Iran toward maritime traffic, both falling into the sea, one inside Emirati territorial waters. An emergency alert went to residents' phones. Hours later the foreign ministry announced that all trade, commercial exchanges and financial transactions with Iran are halted until further notice. Iran's foreign ministry spokesman denies firing anything.

Here is why it matters more than a diplomatic rupture. For most of the past two decades the Emirates has been Iran's first or second largest trading partner, trading places with China. Emirati exports to Iran rose from under six billion dollars before the American withdrawal from the nuclear deal to more than twenty billion in recent years, and roughly a third of everything entering Iran, phones, electronics, car parts, cosmetics, clothing, has moved through the Emirates.

The mechanism is re-export. Goods arrive at Jebel Ali, clear into a free zone, and leave again on a different bill of lading, with Dubai's banks settling the payments. That is what let Iran keep importing through years of sanctions, because sanctions attach to the counterparty and on paper the counterparty is Emirati. Not smuggling. A legitimate logistics business sitting exactly where the sanctions regime has a seam.

Close that channel and you have not removed a trade partner. You have removed the plumbing. There is no substitute at that scale, because a substitute needs deep-water ports, free-zone law, correspondent banking and a willingness to be seen doing it.

One caveat worth holding. This happened once before, at the start of the war, and partially reversed in late June when hostilities eased, so the market will price it as reversible until it isn't.

What would make it concrete is customs data. The next Emirati non-oil trade release will show whether the announcement matched behaviour, or whether the trade routed around it, which is the thing this corridor has always been good at.

One thing to sit with this morning.

My experience is what I agree to attend to. Only those items which I notice shape my mind.

That is William James, in The Principles of Psychology, published in eighteen ninety after twelve years of work. James spent most of the eighteen seventies unable to work at all, in a depressive collapse he later described in the third person, and what he said pulled him out was deciding that his first act of free will would be to believe in free will. He wrote the book while teaching physiology at Harvard, complaining throughout about how long it took.

The observation I would make is that the sentence assumes something the same book spends chapters disproving. James separates voluntary attention from involuntary attention, and he is very good on the second: the loud noise, the moving object, the thing you did not agree to and cannot look away from. Agree to is doing a lot of work for a verb describing something that mostly happens to you.
