Now, what I got wrong.

First, last Saturday I called Anthropic's listing window a question rather than a date, and on Tuesday I passed along the word embattled. Same disclosure. Within two days the book was covered, and by Friday it was six times over. I gave one week's mood too much weight.

Second, on Tuesday I said oil believed the softer version of the Iran story. I didn't flag how quickly that version could reverse, and two days later it did.

Third, a number. On Tuesday I put Monday's Nvidia loss at about two hundred and thirty billion dollars. The Times's figure was a hundred and ninety-two billion, and I corrected it on Wednesday.

One story worth more time this morning: the week the ten-year crossed five percent. Nvidia had two deep dives already. This is the thread that moved most quietly, and further.

What happened. On Tuesday, October consumer prices rose four tenths of a percent, core prices three tenths, and core is running at about three point seven percent annualized over three months. The two-year had its biggest one-day jump since April of last year. Fed governor Christopher Waller had said beforehand that a core reading of three tenths or more might justify a half-point move. Chicago's Austan Goolsbee called the numbers distressing. Wednesday the ten-year sat at five. Thursday it jumped ten basis points. Friday's producer prices were a touch softer than expected, three tenths on the month, but six and a half percent on the year, the fastest since twenty twenty-two, and yields still went up.

The mechanism. The war has pushed energy into everything; heating oil bills alone are forecast up about seventy percent this winter. The Federal Reserve is now expected to raise rates in December, and futures fully price it. A rate increase in an economy that just added a hundred and sixty-five thousand jobs isn't a rescue, it's a brake. The long end is asking for more on top, because nobody can tell when inflation stops.

Where it lands first is housing. Existing-home sales fell to an annual pace of three point seven five million in October, the lowest since twenty ten. The thirty-year mortgage is at seven point one one percent, against six point two four a year ago. And yet the median price, about four hundred and sixteen thousand dollars, is still up on the year. Owners locked in below six percent aren't selling, so prices hold while volume collapses.

The context. DealBook points out the ten-year was just under four percent in late February, before the war. That's well over a full point in nine months.

The puzzle is that stocks rose two and a half percent anyway. The optimistic read is that the economy is strong enough to carry higher rates. The less comfortable one is that equities were relieved about a chip selloff that had gone too far, and haven't caught up with the bond market yet.

What would settle it is the Fed in December, and whether it moves a quarter or a half.

Back in English, and one idea before we finish.

In the winter of nineteen sixty-one, Edward Lorenz, a meteorologist at M I T, wanted to look again at a weather simulation he'd run on his Royal McBee computer. To save time, he restarted it halfway through, typing in numbers from a printout. Then he went for a coffee. When he came back, the new run had drifted completely away from the old one. The printout had rounded his numbers to three decimal places, from six. A difference of about one part in ten thousand had grown, in a couple of simulated months, into a different weather.

The circumstance is that he was trying to prove the opposite. In the nineteen fifties, many in the field believed that with enough data and big enough computers, weather could be forecast weeks or months ahead, and Lorenz had set out partly to test the statistical methods behind that hope. In nineteen sixty-three he published the result as Deterministic Nonperiodic Flow, a paper almost nobody outside meteorology read for a decade. The butterfly came later, from a talk title in nineteen seventy-two that a colleague, Philip Merilees, supplied when Lorenz hadn't given one.

The complication is where the limit actually sits. In nineteen sixty-nine Lorenz argued there was a hard ceiling, roughly two weeks, past which no improvement in data could help. Forecasting has since gained roughly a day of skill per decade, and a twenty nineteen study led by Fuqing Zhang at Penn State put the ceiling near two weeks, broadly backing him. Others argue some patterns, such as El Niño, stay predictable for months. Whether the two-week wall is a law of the atmosphere, or of the models we've built so far, is still argued.
