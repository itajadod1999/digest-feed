Now, what I got wrong.

First, on Wednesday I said the mood had flipped overnight after Nvidia's results, with Nasdaq futures up more than a percent. It lasted one session. The Nasdaq rose half a percent on Wednesday, then lost two and a quarter percent on Thursday and more than five on Friday.

Second, on Thursday I passed along Saudi Arabia's account that it had intercepted the Houthi missile aimed at Riyadh, with only debris falling. By Friday, witnesses were describing blown-out windows across several floors of the sovereign fund's tower. I should have flagged that as one government's claim.

Third, the Anthropic valuation. On Thursday I said one and a half trillion dollars without saying on what basis. On Friday I gave one point three on shares outstanding and one point seven fully diluted. DealBook's figures are one point two seven and nearly one point eight. Those are all defensible, but I should have named the basis every time, rather than letting the number drift from day to day.

Fourth, and the big one. Last Saturday's deep dive was the ten-year crossing five percent, and I framed it as the thread moving quietly and further. A week later the ten-year is back below five, even after Fed minutes in which almost every official expected to raise rates again. When stocks fall this fast, Treasuries get bought, and I didn't allow for how quickly that could swamp the inflation story.

One story worth more time this morning: why the technology trade cracked in the week it was supposed to be rescued.

What happened. Three things were meant to steady the market this week: Nvidia's results, a record listing, and a summit that might calm trade with China. All three happened more or less as hoped, and the S and P still fell on every single day.

The mechanism starts with financing. For two years, part of what kept artificial intelligence spending going was money circulating within the industry. Nvidia took stakes in customers and backed their borrowing; customers bought Nvidia chips. On Tuesday, Kress said that support will be more selective, and analysts Finimize cites think a deeper retreat could pull fifty billion dollars or more out. The Journal reported on Friday that the five hundred billion dollars Wall Street firms pledged in August to finance chip purchases has deployed nothing so far. And the money is reaching for odd places: Alphabet raised three billion dollars this week through tax-exempt prepaid energy bonds, a structure written for town gas utilities.

Then the politics. On Monday, Commerce issued a rule the Treasury wrote. On Thursday came the documents on chip sales to companies tied to China's military, and Taiwan Semiconductor told customers to pause advanced work for Chinese clients without a license. On Friday, at his news conference, the President threatened what he called a tremendous tax on American companies that build data centers abroad. The Times says it isn't clear how that would work.

And the regulators. California's attorney general is investigating Nvidia, and sued OpenAI on Monday. The accounting probe at Nvidia is reported but unconfirmed.

What the numbers mean. Morning Brew notes that, on FactSet's numbers, technology now trades at its lowest ratio of price to expected earnings since twenty twenty. So the market isn't saying the profits are gone. It's paying less for each dollar of them, because it can no longer tell how much was financed by the industry itself, and how much the government will let it sell. Finimize puts Anthropic at about forty times sales. That's the multiple being tested.

What would have to happen next. Watch the financing deals rather than the earnings: CoreWeave's planned secured bond, and the forty billion dollar SpaceX chip lease Goldman Sachs and Apollo are syndicating. If those clear, the selloff was a repricing. If they stall, it's something worse.

Back in English, and one idea before we finish.

In nineteen fifty-six, the social psychologist Leon Festinger and two colleagues, Henry Riecken and Stanley Schachter, published a book called When Prophecy Fails. Its claim was counterintuitive. When people have staked a great deal on a belief, and the world flatly disproves it, many of them don't abandon it. They hold it harder, and start trying to convert others, because more believers make the contradiction easier to live with.

The circumstance is the extraordinary part. In nineteen fifty-four, a woman in Oak Park, outside Chicago, called Marian Keech in the book, said she was receiving messages from beings on another planet warning that a flood would destroy much of the world before dawn on December twenty-first. A small group gathered around her; some quit jobs and gave away possessions. Festinger's team read about it in a newspaper and planted observers in the group, posing as believers. Midnight came, then the early hours, and nothing happened. Then, before dawn, a new message arrived: the group's faith had spread so much light that the world had been spared. A group that had avoided reporters started phoning newspapers.

The complication is that the study may have shaped what it observed. A handful of observers joining a group of a dozen or so was not a small intervention, and some later critics argue the newcomers' apparent conviction reinforced the members' own. And when later researchers looked at other groups whose prophecies failed, the surge in recruiting often didn't appear; some groups reinterpreted quietly, others simply fell apart. The Oak Park group itself scattered within weeks. Whether Festinger found a law of how belief works, or one vivid case that happened to fit, has never been settled.
