One story worth more time this morning.

Shein. The company opened its order book seeking about one point eight billion dollars, at a valuation that could reach twenty-seven billion. In twenty twenty-two it raised money privately at ninety-eight. A seventy-two percent haircut in four years, and the Economist's explanation is weak growth, rising costs including tariffs, and competition.

The middle item is the one that matters. Shein's economics rested on three things stacked together: small-batch manufacturing around Guangzhou that let it test a design in hundreds of units before committing; air freight; and the de minimis exemption, which let parcels under eight hundred dollars enter the United States with no duty and almost no inspection. Remove that third leg and every parcel becomes a customs entry with a tariff attached and a broker's fee behind it. That is a new cost line, charged per package, on a business whose whole proposition was the price of the package, and you cannot dilute it with volume, because volume is what you are being charged for. The venue is its own quiet story: this offering has been in preparation, in one city or another, for about three years, and Hong Kong is not where it started.

What makes it concrete is the range on the first of September. If the book covers at the top and the stock holds, twenty-seven billion becomes the real number. If it prices at the bottom and breaks, every late-stage private mark in Chinese consumer technology gets re-read in that light.

The second story. Kevin Warsh goes to Jackson Hole on Thursday, his first as chairman. Le Monde's Eric Albert makes the case that the Fed's credibility is what is actually on the table: inflation has run above the two percent target for five years, the President has spent the year attacking the institution's independence, and Warsh, who took the chair in May, has had a bumpy start.

The specific problem is that he has declined to use the main instrument a chairman has between meetings, which is expectations. A central bank moves markets far more often by saying what it intends than by doing anything, and Warsh has said almost nothing about where rates are headed. The Journal has resorted to digging through his old forecasts to work out how he thinks about inflation.

Silence is expensive just now. Last week's minutes showed the three dissents in favour of raising rates were serious, and that several officials now believe artificial intelligence investment is itself pushing up demand and therefore prices. That is a different diagnosis from tariffs washing through, and it implies a different path. Nobody outside the building knows which one the chairman holds. What would make Thursday matter is whether he puts a framework on the table at all. If he doesn't, the market keeps guessing, and guessing is what the term premium is for.

One thing to sit with this morning.

The human understanding when it has once adopted an opinion draws all things else to support and agree with it.

That is Francis Bacon, from the Novum Organum, aphorism forty-six of the first book, published in Latin in sixteen twenty. He was Lord Chancellor of England at the time, at the very top of the state. Sixteen months later he was convicted of taking bribes as a judge, fined forty thousand pounds, held briefly in the Tower, and barred from office for life.

The observation I would make is that the sentence is a diagnosis, and the book around it is meant to be the cure: an exhaustive method of tabulating instances, designed to make the mind's tilt irrelevant by never letting it choose what to look at. Nobody ever ran that method, Bacon included, and the tables in the book are unfinished. The diagnosis has outlived the cure by four hundred years, which is an odd result for a work arguing that noticing the problem gets you nothing.
