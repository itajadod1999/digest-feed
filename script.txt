Which brings us to the central bank, and to the question of who it answers to.

Start with what independence means, because it's narrower than it sounds. The Federal Reserve is not independent of the law. Congress created it, sets its goals, maximum employment and stable prices, and can rewrite its charter whenever it likes. The Fed's own phrase is independent within the government. What it has is independence of operation. Once its officials are in place, nobody can order them to raise or cut rates, and nobody has to approve the decision.

Three features make that work. The first is the terms. The seven governors serve fourteen years each, staggered so a seat comes open every two years, which means no single president should be able to appoint a majority in one term. The chair serves four years, and that term deliberately doesn't line up with the president's. The second is removal. The statute says a governor can be removed for cause, and never says what cause means. For most of the Fed's history that ambiguity was the protection. The third is money. The Fed pays for itself out of the interest on the securities it holds, and sends the surplus to the Treasury. It doesn't go to Congress for a budget, so Congress can't squeeze it through appropriations.

Then there's the committee that actually sets rates. The Federal Open Market Committee has twelve votes: the seven governors, the president of the New York Fed, and four of the other eleven regional bank presidents, on rotation. Those regional presidents aren't appointed by the White House at all. They're chosen by their banks' boards and approved by the governors in Washington. That's a structural oddity, and it matters. Even a president who filled every seat on the board wouldn't directly control five of the twelve votes.

None of this was there at the start. When Woodrow Wilson signed the Federal Reserve Act in December nineteen thirteen, the board in Washington included the Treasury secretary and the Comptroller of the Currency, and it met in the Treasury building. The modern design came in the Banking Act of nineteen thirty-five, pushed through by Marriner Eccles, a Utah banker Franklin Roosevelt had made chairman. It took the Treasury off the board, created the long terms, and gave Washington control of the rate committee. The irony is that Eccles wanted a stronger central bank partly so it could work with the New Deal, not stand apart from it.

And during the Second World War it didn't stand apart at all. The Fed agreed to hold Treasury borrowing costs fixed, three eighths of a percent on short bills and two and a half on long bonds, buying whatever it took to keep them there. The central bank was, in effect, a financing arm of the Treasury.

The break came in nineteen fifty-one, and it was messy. The Korean War had pushed inflation up sharply, and the Fed wanted out of the peg. Harry Truman wanted the peg kept. At the end of January he summoned the entire rate committee to the White House, and the next day his staff announced that the Fed had pledged to keep supporting the bond market. The Fed had pledged nothing of the kind. Eccles, by then an ordinary governor, released the Fed's own account of the meeting to the press. Within weeks, the Treasury and the Fed signed a short agreement, known as the Accord, freeing the Fed to set rates as it saw fit.

The man who negotiated it for the Treasury was William McChesney Martin. Truman then made him Fed chairman, presumably expecting a friend. Martin turned out to be the most committed defender of independence the Fed ever had, and the story goes that years later, passing Martin on a New York street, Truman said a single word to him: traitor. It was Martin who in nineteen fifty-five described the Fed's job as taking away the punch bowl just as the party gets going.

The pressure never stopped. In December nineteen sixty-five, after the Fed raised its discount rate, Lyndon Johnson called Martin to his Texas ranch and, by several accounts, physically shoved him against a wall. Richard Nixon did it more quietly. The White House tapes record him leaning on his own chairman, Arthur Burns, ahead of the nineteen seventy-two election, and the Fed kept money loose that year. Most economists count that as one ingredient in the inflation of the decade that followed.

Paul Volcker is the counterexample everyone cites. He raised rates to around twenty percent to break that inflation, through two recessions, and Ronald Reagan mostly let him. Mostly. In his memoir, Volcker describes a meeting in the summer of nineteen eighty-four in the White House library, where James Baker, with Reagan sitting there, told him the President was ordering him not to raise rates before the election. Volcker says he walked out without answering. He hadn't been planning to raise them anyway.

The case for all this rests on an argument and a chart. The argument is that politicians face elections on a short clock, and easy money pays off before an election while the inflation shows up after it. Give the decision to people who don't face voters, and the temptation goes away. The chart came in nineteen ninety-three, from Alberto Alesina and Lawrence Summers, who ranked rich countries by how independent their central banks were and found that the more independent ones had lower inflation, with no visible cost in growth.

Now the honest counterargument, because there's a serious one, in three parts.

The first is that the chart may be backwards. The economist Adam Posen argued that independent central banks don't create low inflation. Countries with a strong constituency against inflation, often a powerful financial sector, create independent central banks and then protect them. Germany's Bundesbank was independent because Germans remembered nineteen twenty-three, not the other way round.

The second is democratic. Since two thousand eight, the Fed has done far more than set one interest rate. It has bought trillions of dollars of bonds, lent to companies and cities in emergencies, and lifted asset prices in ways that help people who own assets more than people who don't. Those are choices with winners and losers, made by officials nobody elected. You don't have to be a populist to find that uncomfortable.

The third is arithmetic. In nineteen eighty-one Thomas Sargent and Neil Wallace showed that if a government keeps running deficits it can't otherwise finance, the central bank eventually has to accommodate them, however independent it is on paper. Independence, on that view, is only as strong as the fiscal policy around it.

And pressure isn't confined to one party. In twenty twenty-four, Elizabeth Warren and other Democrats publicly urged Jerome Powell to cut rates.

Which brings us to the last two years, when every one of those protections was tested at once.

In August of twenty twenty-five, the President moved to fire Lisa Cook, a governor appointed by Joe Biden, citing allegations of mortgage fraud that she denies. It was the first attempt to remove a sitting governor in the Fed's history. In January, Powell disclosed that the Justice Department had opened a criminal investigation tied to his testimony about the renovation of the Fed's headquarters, and called it a pretext. In May the Senate confirmed Kevin Warsh as chair, fifty-four to forty-five.

Then the courts. At the end of June, the Supreme Court handed down two decisions on the same day. By six to three, it held that commissioners of the Federal Trade Commission could be fired at will, gutting the ninety-year-old precedent that protected independent agencies. But by five to four, in an opinion by Chief Justice Roberts, it refused to let the President remove Cook while her case goes on. The Court has been signaling for some time that it sees the Fed as a special case, but the ruling was explicit that it didn't decide what for cause means. That question is still open.

And then the Fed did something odd for a central bank supposedly under the President's thumb. Under Warsh, it raised rates by a quarter point in September and held them at three and three quarters to four percent in October. Two officials dissented in October because they wanted another increase, and one of them was Lisa Cook. Markets now lean toward a December hike, at a meeting on the ninth.

So what did Tuesday change? Mainly the appointment lever. From January, anyone the President nominates to the board needs a Democratic Senate to confirm them, and Reuters expects Warren to chair the Banking Committee. A seat taken from Cook could no longer simply be handed to an ally. That's Timiraos's point.

What it doesn't change is the removal question, which belongs to the courts, or the money question, which belongs to Congress as a whole. And it does nothing about the softest protection of all, which is whether markets believe the Fed will do what it says. That one doesn't sit in any statute. It's earned at each meeting, and the next one is a month away.

Back in English, and one idea before we finish.

In two thousand five, Donald Shoup, a professor of urban planning at the University of California, Los Angeles, published a book of more than seven hundred pages called The High Cost of Free Parking. His argument was that the minimum parking requirements written into nearly every American zoning code, so many spaces per apartment, per restaurant seat, per square foot of office, were quietly one of the most expensive policies in the country. They made buildings costlier, pushed them apart, and hid the price of driving inside the price of everything else.

The circumstance is what makes it land. Shoup went looking for where the numbers came from, and found that planners mostly copied them from each other, or from a manual of trip counts taken largely at busy suburban sites, at peak hours. A required ratio could rest on a handful of observations. He also measured the other side, the curb, and estimated that drivers circling for cheap street parking in a small part of Westwood in Los Angeles drove close to a million extra miles a year. His fix was to scrap the minimums and price the curb so that a space or two was always free on each block.

The complication is that it worked on paper faster than in practice. Cities followed. Buffalo dropped its minimums in twenty seventeen, and New York lifted them for new housing across much of the city in twenty twenty-four. One study of Buffalo found developers building about half the parking the old rules would have demanded. But in many places they keep building it anyway, because lenders and tenants ask for it, and San Francisco's experiment with pricing the curb produced gains that researchers still argue about. Whether the rule was the cause, or just a symptom of how people already wanted to live, isn't settled.
