One story worth more time this morning: how you remove a founder from the most important company in the market.

Start with what's known. On Sunday, Jensen Huang emailed staff admitting an affair with Alice Huang, no relation, an executive at Megaspeed, a Singapore company and major Nvidia customer, whom American intelligence believes is a Chinese spy. On Wednesday the Times, citing five people who worked at Nvidia, reported a separate relationship with Kimberly Powell, the company's vice president of healthcare, who reports to him. Nvidia's code of conduct tells employees to avoid conflicts, personal relationships included.

Now the mechanism. Nvidia's bylaws let the board remove the chief executive with or without cause, by simple majority. The board has eleven members, ten counted as independent, and no chair. Removing Huang as chief executive would not remove him as a director; that takes shareholders. He is the largest individual shareholder, with about three and a half percent. He has run the company since nineteen ninety-three, with no named successor. The Economist reports speculation that the chief financial officer, Colette Kress, or a vice president, Ian Buck, could step in on an interim basis.

The precedents cut both ways. In nineteen eighty-five, Apple's board sided with John Sculley and stripped Steve Jobs of his operating role, and Jobs left within months. In twenty twenty-three, OpenAI's board fired Sam Altman and reinstated him within days, after most of the staff threatened to leave. A board can fire a founder. Whether it sticks depends on who follows him out the door.

The pressure runs both ways too. The Economist's editorial says Huang must resign. A Republican senator, Kevin Cramer, has said the same. Elon Musk and other Silicon Valley figures call it a personal lapse, not a security failure.

Why it reaches beyond one man: Nvidia controls an estimated ninety percent of the market for the chips behind the big artificial intelligence projects, and ten companies now make up roughly forty percent of the S and P. The Economist calls it a threefold concentration risk: a market that depends on one technology, which depends on one firm, which depends on one man.

What to watch: the board's announcement, which could come any hour; who's named interim; earnings on November seventeenth; and next week's meeting between the President and Xi Jinping, where chip sales to China just became a more awkward subject.

One idea before we finish.

In nineteen thirty-two, a Columbia law professor named Adolf Berle and an economist named Gardiner Means published The Modern Corporation and Private Property. Their claim was that the big American company was no longer controlled by the people who owned it. Shares had been spread among so many small holders that none could direct anything, and power had passed to salaried managers who owned almost nothing.

The circumstance gave it force. Means went through the two hundred largest nonfinancial corporations and classified each by who actually controlled it. By their count, about forty-four percent of the companies, holding fifty-eight percent of the wealth, were under management control. The book landed in the depths of the Depression, and Berle went on to join Franklin Roosevelt's Brain Trust.

The complication is that nobody agreed on what followed. Berle argued managers should be held strictly to shareholders' interests; a Harvard professor, Merrick Dodd, replied that managers were trustees for employees and the public too, and decades later Berle seemed to concede the point. Then in nineteen seventy-six Michael Jensen and William Meckling rebuilt the shareholder side as agency theory. And in nineteen ninety-nine, economists surveying firms worldwide found the diffuse ownership Berle and Means described was the exception, mostly American and British, while founders and families held control almost everywhere else. Index funds have since bunched American ownership back up in a handful of asset managers. Whether that answers Berle and Means, or just restates their problem, is still argued.
