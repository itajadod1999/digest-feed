One story worth more time this morning: where the distress in commercial mortgage bonds is moving, and why.

What happened. CRED iQ's October numbers show New York's distress rate improving, to eleven point two percent from twelve and a half a year ago. Elsewhere, it's getting worse. Los Angeles went to fourteen point six percent from nine point four, and its office loans to twenty-one and a half percent from under ten. Chicago is at twenty-three and a half percent, and nearly a third of its office loans are distressed. Houston's office rate went to thirty-one and a half percent from under twenty-two.

The mechanism. Ninety-three percent of October's new trouble came at or near maturity. These loans aren't failing because tenants stopped paying; they're failing because the building can't be refinanced at today's rates and values. The clearest example is in Philadelphia. Twenty-four hundred Market Street carries a two hundred and five million dollar loan, the building is ninety-nine and a half percent occupied, and the main tenant, Aramark, is signed through twenty thirty-four. It still went to the special servicer in September, ahead of a December maturity, and the servicer is working on a modification. A full building with a long lease isn't enough on its own.

And a shorter one: why the market punished both sides of the Marvell deal.

In an all-stock merger, the buyer pays in its own shares, so Lumentum's owners get a third of a company whose value depends on Marvell's stock. So when Marvell fell thirteen percent, the value of what Lumentum's owners were being offered fell with it, and Lumentum fell further still. The Journal's Spencer Jakab called it the complete opposite of what usually happens to a takeover target.

Why Marvell fell: the deal is worth more than half its own market value, both stocks had more than doubled this year, and it doubles Marvell's bet on data-centre spending just as investors question the payoff. What would change the picture is the next round of spending guidance from the big cloud companies. If they cut, Marvell will have paid in stock, at the top, for a supplier to a slowing market.

One idea before we finish.

In the mid-nineteen-nineties, William Nordhaus set out to measure something official statistics had never handled well: the price of light. The cost, in hours of work, of an hour of light at a given brightness.

The circumstance is the good part. He didn't just collect old price lists; he burned things and measured them with a light meter: firewood, candles, sesame oil in a Roman-style lamp, then kerosene, gas and bulbs. His paper, published in an economics volume in nineteen ninety-seven, concluded that the true cost of lighting had fallen so fast that conventional price indices had overstated its price increase since eighteen hundred by a factor of something like a thousand. His point was that when a good changes completely, from a candle to a bulb, the statistics keep pricing the candle.

The complication is where that logic leads. It arrived alongside the Senate's Boskin Commission, which argued the consumer price index was overstating inflation by more than a point a year. Run that kind of correction back across two centuries and you get nineteenth-century Americans implausibly poor, living on far less than anyone could survive on. Either the old statistics were badly wrong, or light was a special case. Economists still argue about how far his candles should be allowed to travel.
