Good morning. Saturday, October thirty-first, and a look back at the week. Nothing new this morning, only where the week's stories ended up, the numbers, and what I got wrong.

The short version: the week began with relief and ended with the worst two days for big technology in months. Bonds rallied the whole way through, oil kept climbing underneath, and Washington went into the final stretch before Tuesday's midterms with one eye on Iran.

Start with technology, because it ran the tape. Monday through Wednesday were quiet gains. Then the earnings arrived. Microsoft beat and held its spending in check, and was rewarded. Alphabet missed on search for the first time in four years, reported free cash flow of about minus nine billion dollars, and on Thursday had its worst day on record, down thirteen percent, with roughly five hundred billion dollars of value gone. And on Friday Intel, which had warned on fourth-quarter sales, fell about twenty-eight and a half percent, its worst day in roughly fifty years. The Nasdaq lost nearly three percent on Friday alone, and the Nasdaq one hundred fell into its third correction of the year.

Deals didn't slow down for any of it. DoorDash went from advanced talks on Wednesday to an agreed seventeen billion dollar purchase of Instacart on Thursday, though it doesn't expect to close before late twenty twenty-seven. That's a long engagement. E Q T agreed to buy Range Resources for thirteen billion in stock and Thrive Holdings, the offshoot of Josh Kushner's venture firm, is paying seven point six billion in cash for ADT. And Marvell's reported talks to buy Lumentum, at about ninety billion dollars, are still only that: reported, single-sourced, nothing announced.

Your shop, for what it's worth, had the private credit deal of the week. Ares Capital is buying MidCap Financial from Apollo, at a thirty percent premium to the market price and a fifteen percent discount to book, the kind of gap Matt Levine expects to drive more mergers among these funds.

And Weil, Gotshal and Manges went from wobbling on Monday to planning its own dissolution by Thursday: failed merger talks, departing partners, a chair-elect who turned the job down, and finally bankruptcy counsel of its own.

The week's genuinely new risk came from crypto. Binance said on Thursday that hackers had taken six point three billion dollars of coins, most of it in about twenty minutes. About three billion has been frozen. Analysts blame North Korea, and bitcoin ended the week below seventy-seven thousand dollars. Withdrawals are still suspended; the exchange says testing should finish this weekend.

Real estate had a week of prices being found. In Los Angeles, the two hundred and ninety million dollar loan on Wells Fargo Center South sold for a hundred and fifteen million, about forty cents on the dollar. In Boston, Rockpoint bought a hundred Summer Street at a foreclosure auction for two hundred and sixty million, against eight hundred and six million seven years ago. In Midtown, David Werner took forty-nine percent of six oh five Third Avenue after Fisher Brothers' bond sale in Israel found no takers. And by the trade press's count, only about thirteen percent of office loans maturing in commercial mortgage-backed deals in September paid off on time.

Against that, conversions came back: the sagging floors at the old Pfizer headquarters were jacked back up, and sixteen more conversions have been cleared since July.

The Middle East moved in two directions at once. On Monday, CNN reported that American forces could strike Iran within ninety-six hours of an order. By Wednesday, the Journal reported that Gulf governments had been told to expect a short, intense campaign after the midterms. Then on Thursday the Times and the Journal both reported that JD Vance had reached out to an Iranian hard-liner to revive talks, and on Friday Qatari mediators arrived in Tehran. All of it rests on unnamed officials.

Israel's election produced no government. Benjamin Netanyahu's bloc won fifty-three seats, the opposition bloc under Gadi Eisenkot forty-eight, and Arab parties a record nineteen. The arithmetic now goes to the president, Isaac Herzog.

At home, much of the run-up to Tuesday happened in courtrooms. James Comey was charged a third time, and a grand jury declined to indict Cassidy Hutchinson. An appeals court twice refused to set aside twenty thousand California mail-in ballots. And the Justice Department subpoenaed election officials in California and sent dozens of subpoenas in Alaska's Senate race, over a spoiler named Dan Sullivan whom the Democratic campaign denies recruiting.

The rest of the world's week. Brazil's market had one of its worst days in decades on Monday after Lula's win, the Ibovespa down about fourteen percent and the real about eight; by Thursday, Flávio Bolsonaro had defied a court order and landed in Miami. Two high-speed trains collided near Toledo, killing sixty-nine people, and officials now blame an error by traffic controllers. And Novaya Gazeta, Russia's last major independent paper, shut down, saying the Kremlin had orchestrated it.

Now the numbers, Friday close against Friday close.

The S and P five hundred finished around seventy-five eighty, down a little over half a percent on the week, after falling nearly two percent on Friday alone. The Nasdaq closed near twenty-five thousand four hundred and forty, down about two percent, its third losing week in a row. The Dow ended around fifty-one thousand seven hundred and seventy, down about eight tenths of a percent.

Bonds went the other way. The ten-year Treasury closed at about four point nine four percent, down roughly six basis points from five percent a week earlier. The two-year fell further, about thirteen basis points, to four point five six. So the curve steepened, the gap between the two widening from about thirty-one basis points to thirty-eight.

Oil climbed through all of it. The expiring December Brent contract finished Friday near a hundred and seventeen sixty, up a little over two percent on the day and about one percent on the week. The more traded January contract closed near a hundred and thirteen and a half. Brent rose about thirteen and a half percent in October, a fourth straight monthly gain.

Now, what I got wrong.

First, the plain error. On Thursday I said the United States had expelled Cuba's foreign minister. It expelled a Cuban diplomat. I corrected it on Friday, but it went out.

Second, oil. All week I quoted Brent without saying which contract. Last Saturday's hundred and sixteen sixty-five was the December contract. Some of my weekday figures, like Wednesday's hundred and fourteen, appear to have been the January contract, which trades several dollars lower. That made it sound as though oil was falling during the week when, like for like, it rose. From now on I'll name the contract.

Third, I twice passed along after-hours numbers as if they'd hold. Alphabet's seven to ten percent became thirteen, and Intel's twenty became twenty-eight and a half.

Fourth, a judgment call. Three of the week's deep dives were about financing the artificial intelligence build-out, and Tuesday's led with Anthropic's prospectus. Disclosure: Anthropic makes me. I gave my own maker the top slot the morning after Brazil's market crash, which was at least as consequential. You should know I made that call.

One story worth more time this morning: the week the artificial intelligence bill came due.

What happened. In about seventy-two hours, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta and Microsoft told investors they expect to spend more than seven hundred and twenty billion dollars this year on data centres and equipment, by DealBook's count. And the market drew a sharp line between them. Microsoft, whose software business throws off cash and whose spending came in under forecast, went up. Alphabet, whose cash flow went negative, had its worst day ever. Amazon, which by the Economist's estimate paid out about forty-two billion dollars more than came in, fell. And Intel, not one of the spenders at all, was hit hardest, partly because the build-out is soaking up parts that personal computers need.

The mechanism. Two things changed at once. The payoff got harder to see: Google's search queries are growing at their fastest in five years, but its artificial intelligence answers earn less per query than the old ads. And the costs started running upstream. Flash memory prices have roughly quintupled, the Journal reports. The biggest spenders are now paying for their suppliers' windfall.

The financing is where the week was most revealing. Meta's equity raise grew to nearly ninety-eight billion dollars, with three of its own chip suppliers buying in. Microsoft sold twenty-five billion of bonds the day after its results. Broadcom's chief executive assembled a sixty-three billion dollar vehicle in which banks buy Broadcom chips and lease them to Anthropic, and had to concede higher rates and default guarantees to get the banks to commit. Banks led by Morgan Stanley put off selling down part of a fifteen billion dollar loan for a Texas data centre tied to Anthropic, after the state paused new projects. One investor told DealBook it's a flight to quality, the big getting bigger.

What the bond market said. While all of this was going on, Treasuries rallied, and the two-year fell more than twice as far as the ten. One reading is that a slower build-out weakens the case for the Fed's next increase. The week's growth figure pulls the other way. The economy grew at an annualized three point four percent in the third quarter, with business investment accounting for more than half of it, and equipment spending up almost twenty-one percent. The data-centre boom is now holding up a meaningful share of American growth, while the Fed's preferred inflation gauge sits at three point six percent.

What would make it matter. The Anthropic listing, aimed at the week of November sixteenth, is the next test of whether public investors will pay for the spending rather than just tolerate it. And watch whether any of the spenders blinks. So far none has cut its plans, which is precisely what investors spent the week punishing.

Loose ends going into next week.

The midterms are on Tuesday. The Economist's poll with YouGov had Democrats ahead on the House ballot, forty-six to forty, and the President is rallying in Kansas and Iowa on Sunday, where Republicans unexpectedly look exposed. On Iran, the question is which comes first, the reported post-election window for strikes or the Vance and Qatari channel. Marvell and Lumentum are either a deal or a rumour. Binance's withdrawals are due back this weekend, by its own account. And Europe needs a new central banker, about which more in French.

Back in English, and one idea before we finish.

In nineteen thirty-one, Harry Beck, an engineering draughtsman who had been laid off from the London Underground's signals office, redrew the Tube map in his spare time. The maps of the day were faithful to geography, so the central stations crowded into an unreadable knot while the outer lines trailed off the edge of the paper. Beck threw the geography away. Every line ran horizontally, vertically or at forty-five degrees, the stations were spaced evenly, and the centre was blown up at the expense of the suburbs. It looked like an electrical circuit diagram.

The Underground's publicity office thought it too radical. A small trial printing went out in nineteen thirty-three, the public took to it immediately, and a descendant of it has hung on every Tube platform since.

The complication is that a map that's easy to read can also mislead. In twenty eleven, Zhan Guo, a transport researcher, studied how London riders chose their routes and found that many trusted the distances on the diagram over their own travel times, taking longer journeys because the map made them look shorter. New York tried Beck's approach in nineteen seventy-two, with Massimo Vignelli's subway diagram, and dropped it in nineteen seventy-nine, partly because riders complained it didn't match the streets above them. Whether a transit map should show the network or the city has never really been settled.
