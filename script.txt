One thread worth more time, and it sits underneath everything else this week.

The question the bond market spent the week arguing about is not really where the federal funds rate lands on the sixteenth. It is who is left to buy American government debt.

Supply is simple. Federal debt crossed forty trillion dollars this year, roughly a hundred twenty-five percent of the economy, and the Treasury has to sell it, roll it, and keep selling.

Demand is where the week's news actually lives. Norway's sovereign wealth fund, the largest in the world at two point three trillion dollars, has proposed cutting Treasuries from thirty-four percent of its bond portfolio to twenty-two, roughly eighty billion dollars. China has cut its holdings by nearly half from the twenty thirteen peak. Japan, still the largest foreign holder, has been selling to defend its currency. The Dutch central bank moved seventy-eight tonnes of gold from New York to London, citing geopolitical risk, and France quietly emptied its own vault at the New York Federal Reserve.

No one of those is a crisis. Together they describe a change in what the world's most conservative institutions think the safest asset is actually for.

And here is what makes it self-reinforcing. Foreign investors hold trillions in American stocks and bonds and have hedged only about forty-one percent of it against a falling dollar, the lowest share since twenty fifteen. That is a bet the dollar holds up when markets don't, and the dollar is down two point six percent this quarter. If those investors decide to hedge, the way they hedge is by selling dollars, which pushes the dollar down further, which makes the next one want to hedge. The unhedged position is the fuel.

Set that against the household picture in the same jobs report. Average hourly earnings rose three point one percent over the year against inflation at three point seven, so pay is shrinking in real terms, and the difference is coming out of savings. The personal saving rate is three percent, down from five point three two years ago.

What would make this more than background noise is a failed auction, which nothing this week resembled, or a Federal Reserve that hikes into a weakening consumer, which the sixteenth may produce. But the thing to watch has no headline attached, which is whether the long end keeps rising on days when nothing happens. A yield that climbs on news is a market pricing information. A yield that climbs on a quiet day is a market running out of buyers.

One thing to sit with this morning.

Could the young but realize how soon they will become mere walking bundles of habits, they would give more heed to their conduct while in the plastic state.

That's William James, in the chapter on habit in The Principles of Psychology, eighteen ninety. He had been writing it for twelve years and had come to loathe it, telling his publisher it was a loathsome, distended, tumefied, bloated, dropsical mass, and that the only two things it proved were that there is no such subject as psychology and that William James is incapable of writing it. He had also spent his late twenties in a depression severe enough that he considered killing himself, and dated his recovery to a day in eighteen seventy when he read Renouvier on free will and decided to believe in it. He was forty-eight when the book finally appeared.

The observation I'd make is that his own life is the counterexample to his sentence. He says the plastic state closes early and the habits set by thirty. He reorganized himself at twenty-eight by an act of will he called decisive, then wrote the chapter arguing that mostly you can't. And a few pages on he calls habit the enormous flywheel of society, the thing that keeps the fisherman at sea through the winter and the poor man from rising against the rich. He offers that as mechanics. It isn't.
