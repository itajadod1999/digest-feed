That ten-year yield is where today's piece begins.

At one o'clock Eastern on Wednesday afternoon, bids closed on thirty-nine billion dollars of ten-year Treasury notes. A couple of minutes later the result came out, and the bond market, which had spent the morning at its highest yields since two thousand and two, exhaled. A routine monthly sale, on a published calendar, moved the most important interest rate in the world.

So this morning: how the auction works, who built it that way, what the numbers coming out of it actually mean, and what would break it.

Start with the plumbing. The Treasury doesn't decide what to borrow week by week. Its Office of Debt Management sets the sizes of its regular notes and bonds every quarter, at what's called the quarterly refunding, after taking advice from a committee of dealers and investors. The guiding doctrine is regular and predictable. The Treasury wants to be boring. It announces each auction days in advance, and the moment it does, the new security starts trading on a when-issued basis, meaning people buy and sell it before it formally exists. That when-issued yield becomes the benchmark the auction gets judged against.

On the day itself, small buyers can put in noncompetitive bids, up to ten million dollars each, which say, in effect, I'll take whatever yield the auction produces. Everyone else bids competitively: this much, at this yield. No single bidder can be awarded more than thirty-five percent of the issue. The bids are ranked from the lowest yield upward, and the Treasury fills them until the whole amount is sold. The yield on the last bid accepted is called the high yield or the stop-out, and every winning bidder gets it, including the ones who bid lower.

That last detail is the important one, and it wasn't always this way.

For most of the twentieth century, the Treasury sold notes and bonds by subscription. It picked a coupon and a price and invited orders. Auctions for coupon securities only became routine in the nineteen seventies, and when they did, they were what economists call discriminatory, or multiple-price. You paid what you bid. Bid too aggressively and you overpaid everyone else. So bidders shaded their bids down, and the ones best placed to shade cleverly were the big dealers who could see the most order flow.

Milton Friedman had argued against that design as early as nineteen fifty-nine, in testimony to Congress. His case was that if everyone pays the same price, you can bid what the bond is genuinely worth to you without fear of being punished for it. More bidders come in, the playing field levels, and the Treasury ends up borrowing more cheaply. For three decades the idea stayed mostly in the journals.

What moved it was a scandal. In May of nineteen ninety-one, Salomon Brothers, then the most powerful bond house on Wall Street, ended up controlling roughly ninety-four percent of a two-year note auction, once its own bids and its customers' were added together. A trader named Paul Mozer had been submitting bids in customers' names without their knowledge to get around the thirty-five percent limit. When it came out that summer, Salomon's chairman, John Gutfreund, resigned, Warren Buffett stepped in to run the firm, and Salomon paid two hundred and ninety million dollars to settle.

The government's review afterwards reopened Friedman's idea. In September of nineteen ninety-two, the Treasury began selling two- and five-year notes by uniform price as an experiment. By late nineteen ninety-eight, every Treasury auction worked that way, and still does.

Now, who's bidding. The results sort winners into three groups.

Primary dealers are about two dozen big banks and broker-dealers that trade directly with the Federal Reserve. In return, they're expected to bid in every auction for a share of the issue, at reasonable prices. They're the backstop. Whatever nobody else wants, they absorb, and then they have to sell it on or finance it on their balance sheets.

Direct bidders are institutions bidding for their own account, without a dealer.

Indirect bidders place their bids through a dealer or through the Fed, and this group is usually read as a proxy for foreign demand, because foreign central banks bid through the New York Fed. That reading is only partly right.

Three numbers come out of every auction. The bid-to-cover ratio is the total bid divided by the amount sold. The tail is the gap between the stop-out yield and where the when-issued market was trading at one o'clock. If the auction clears at a higher yield than the market expected, it tailed, and buyers had to be paid extra. If it clears lower, it stopped through. And the dealer takedown is the share the backstop got stuck with. High dealer share means real money stayed home.

Here's Wednesday, from the Treasury's own data. The thirty-nine billion dollars sold at a high yield of five point three percent, the highest at a ten-year auction since November of two thousand. In September, the same auction cleared at four point eight three, so almost half a percentage point higher in a month.

Then the demand. The bid-to-cover was two point seven seven, the strongest of any ten-year auction in the past year, against an average of about two and a half. Indirect bidders took about eighty percent. Primary dealers were left with two and a half percent, against an average near nine and a half over the past year, and the smallest share of any ten-year sale in that stretch. And according to market trackers, the auction stopped through by about a basis point and a half.

To see what a bad one looks like, go back to the five-year note auction in late September. Bid-to-cover of two point two one, and dealers stuck with almost sixteen percent. Or the famous one, a seven-year auction in February of twenty twenty-one, when the bid-to-cover fell to a record low of about two point oh four, dealers absorbed around forty percent, and the stop-out came in roughly four basis points above the market.

Now, what breaks it.

The first thing to understand is that a Treasury auction can't fail in the way people imagine. There's no minimum price. The Treasury sells the full amount every time, at whatever yield clears it. Not everyone works that way. In November of twenty eleven, at the height of the euro crisis, Germany offered six billion euros of ten-year bonds and drew bids for less than four billion. The Bundesbank had to keep the rest. In America, failure shows up only as a price: a big tail, a high stop-out, dealers carrying paper they didn't want.

Which points to the real vulnerability: the dealers. Their balance sheets haven't grown anything like as fast as the debt they're expected to backstop, partly because of bank capital rules written after two thousand and eight. When everyone wants to sell Treasuries at once, as in March of twenty twenty, the dealers can't absorb it, and the Fed has to. So when people worry about Treasury demand, the precise worry is this: a run of auctions where the backstop is needed, at a moment when the backstop is full.

Second, the buyer base. As foreign central banks have bought a smaller share over the past decade, the gap has been filled by price-sensitive private investors, who come in only when the yield is high enough. The Treasury is still selling everything. It just has to pay more each time to find the buyers.

Then the honest counterargument, and it's a strong one: we read far too much into auction results. Yields are set every second in the secondary market, which trades hundreds of billions of dollars a day. The auction is one hour in that market. A stop-through often just means the market had already cheapened the bond going in. Yields had climbed for days into Wednesday, which is precisely what made five point three attractive. On this view, the auction didn't calm the market. The market had already done the work, and the auction confirmed it.

And the eighty percent indirect figure is less clean than it sounds. The indirect category includes foreign central banks, but also domestic funds that simply route their bids through a dealer. Not all of it is foreign.

The defence of the auction, though, is substantial. Through a pandemic, debt-ceiling standoffs that went to the wire and this year's war-driven inflation, every auction has sold the full amount and the market has cleared it. The uniform-price design Friedman argued for and the Salomon scandal forced through is one of the quieter successes of economic policy.

So what would tell you whether Wednesday meant anything? Three things. The inflation number on Wednesday, because the buyers at five point three are betting it won't climb further. The November refunding announcement, due in early November, which will say whether the Treasury plans to sell more longer-dated debt. And the next ten-year auction, also in November. If the bid-to-cover falls back toward two and a half and the dealers are carrying ten percent again, Wednesday was a good price, not a change of heart.

One idea before we finish.

In the summer of nineteen fifty-three, at Los Alamos, Enrico Fermi, John Pasta and Stanislaw Ulam set one of the first electronic computers, the MANIAC, a problem they thought they knew the answer to. Take a chain of masses joined by springs, add a slight nonlinearity to the springs, and put all the energy into the simplest vibration. Physics predicted the energy would gradually spread across every possible mode of vibration until it was shared evenly. They wanted to watch it happen.

It didn't. The energy spread a little, then flowed back, until almost all of it was sitting in the original mode again. The person who actually made the machine do it was a young programmer named Mary Tsingou, who wrote the code and ran the simulations. The nineteen fifty-five report carried only the three men's names. Fermi had died the year before. Her obituary ran in the Times this weekend, under a headline noting she waited fifty years for proper credit. Physicists now often call it the Fermi-Pasta-Ulam-Tsingou problem.

The complication is that nobody has fully closed it. In nineteen sixty-five, Norman Zabusky and Martin Kruskal traced the recurrence to stable travelling waves they named solitons, which opened an entire field. Soviet physicists showed that with enough energy in the chain, chaos does set in and the energy spreads after all. But whether a weakly nonlinear chain like the original one eventually shares its energy evenly if you simply wait long enough, and how long that would take, is still being worked on, more than seventy years after a computer gave four people an answer they didn't expect.
