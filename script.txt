Which brings us to tariffs.

Start with what happened on Wednesday. The trade representative, Jamieson Greer, proposed tariffs of fifteen to forty percent on products from fourteen economies, among them China, Japan and the European Union. His office says those countries have unreasonable excess manufacturing capacity that damages American industry. The Times reports some Chinese goods would face twenty percent, with room to go to forty. The rates are meant to take account of existing deals and other duties, and nothing takes effect until a public consultation is done. China says it reserves the right to respond.

If that sounds like a lot of procedure for a President who used to announce tariffs on a Friday afternoon, that's the story. To see why, you have to go back to who owns this power in the first place.

The Constitution gives it to Congress. Article One, section eight: Congress shall have power to lay and collect duties. For most of the nineteenth century, tariffs were the federal government's main source of money, and Congress wrote them itself, product by product, in enormous bills.

That system ended with its most famous product. In June of nineteen thirty, Herbert Hoover signed the Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act, which raised duties on thousands of goods. More than a thousand economists had signed a petition begging him to veto it. Trading partners retaliated, and world trade collapsed over the next three years. Most of that collapse was the Depression itself, not the tariff. But the lesson Congress drew is the one that matters here. It wasn't so much that tariffs were bad. It was that Congress was bad at setting them, because every member traded votes to protect the industries at home, and the bill grew line by line until nobody controlled it.

So in nineteen thirty-four, pushed by the Secretary of State, Cordell Hull, Congress passed the Reciprocal Trade Agreements Act. It let the President cut tariffs by up to half, in deals with other countries, without coming back to Congress each time. That's the template for everything since. Congress writes the conditions. The President walks through the door.

Over the next forty years, Congress built several more doors, and each one has its own toll.

The first is national security. Section two thirty-two of the Trade Expansion Act of nineteen sixty-two lets the President restrict imports that threaten national security, after the Commerce Department investigates. In nineteen seventy-six, the Supreme Court upheld Gerald Ford's use of it to put fees on imported oil. It sat mostly unused until twenty eighteen, when steel and aluminum got hit. Today it's the workhorse: steel, aluminum, cars, copper and more, product by product.

The Trade Act of nineteen seventy-four added three more. Section two-oh-one is the safeguard: if a surge of imports seriously injures an American industry, and the International Trade Commission agrees, the President can impose temporary relief, eight years at most. Washing machines and solar panels got that in twenty eighteen. Section one twenty-two is the balance-of-payments clause: a tariff of up to fifteen percent, on anyone, for a hundred and fifty days, and then it dies unless Congress extends it. And section three-oh-one lets the trade representative investigate a foreign country's practices, and if they're unreasonable or discriminatory and burden American commerce, retaliate.

Section three-oh-one was the weapon of the nineteen-eighties against Japan. In nineteen eighty-seven, Ronald Reagan put hundred-percent duties on about three hundred million dollars of Japanese electronics, accusing Japan of breaking a semiconductor agreement. In twenty eighteen and twenty nineteen, the first Trump administration used it to put tariffs on roughly three hundred and seventy billion dollars of Chinese goods. Thousands of importers sued, and the tariffs survived.

And then there's the emergency door, which is where this year's story really starts. On the fifteenth of August, nineteen seventy-one, Richard Nixon closed the gold window and, the same evening, slapped a ten percent surcharge on most imports. He used the Trading with the Enemy Act, a law from nineteen seventeen. The surcharge came off that December. An appeals court upheld it in nineteen seventy-five, and Congress, alarmed at how much power peacetime emergencies were handing presidents, replied in nineteen seventy-seven with the International Emergency Economic Powers Act. It was meant to narrow those powers. It lets a President regulate imports in a declared emergency. For nearly fifty years, no one read that as permission to tax them.

Until twenty twenty-five. In February of that year, citing fentanyl, the administration used the emergency powers act for tariffs on Canada, Mexico and China. In April came the so-called reciprocal tariffs, with a ten percent floor on almost everyone. The appeal was obvious: no investigation, no cap, no time limit, any country, any rate, changed by social media post.

A wine importer and a toy company were among the small businesses that sued. The trade court ruled against the tariffs in May of last year, and the appeals court agreed in August. In February of this year, the Supreme Court did too: the emergency powers act, the majority held, does not authorize tariffs.

What happened next is the system working exactly as built. The administration walked through the next door. It imposed a temporary global tariff under section one twenty-two, at fifteen percent, the legal maximum, and that clock ran out in the summer. In March, the trade representative opened section three-oh-one investigations into sixteen economies over what it called structural excess capacity, with written comments in April and a hearing in May. Wednesday's proposal is the result, and the list has shrunk to fourteen.

So think about the tolls. The emergency door was fast and unlimited, which is why it was used, and why it fell. The security door goes product by product and needs a finding. The balance-of-payments door has a cap and a clock. The unfair-practices door goes country by country and needs a record, but once you're through it, there's no rate cap and no expiry date, and the courts have historically deferred. Slower, but more durable. The practical effect is that tariffs stop being instant leverage, a deal by Friday or else, and start looking like a regulatory proceeding, with dockets, comment periods and appeals.

What could break it? Three things.

The first is Congress. It can take the power back whenever it likes, and a Democratic-led Congress arrives in January. But taking back a delegation means passing a law the President can veto, so in practice it needs two thirds of both chambers. That's the asymmetry at the heart of all this: Congress hands over power with a simple majority, and needs a supermajority to recover it.

The second is the courts. Section three-oh-one was written for specific foreign practices: a subsidy, a market closed to American goods. Critics argue that excess capacity is a condition of a whole economy, not a practice, and that stretching the statute to cover it invites the same kind of challenge that sank the emergency tariffs. Expect the lawsuits once the rates are final.

The third is the outside world, and that check is mostly gone. In twenty twenty, a World Trade Organization panel found that the twenty eighteen China tariffs broke global trading rules. The United States appealed, into a void: the body that hears appeals has had no members since December twenty nineteen, so the ruling never took effect. What's left is retaliation, which is what China is signaling.

Now the honest counterargument, because the case for this structure is better than it sounds. Delegation isn't an accident. Congress built it deliberately, so that a President could bargain, and bargaining needs a credible threat. Robert Lighthizer, who ran the China case in twenty eighteen, argues that section three-oh-one tariffs are what brought Beijing to the Phase One deal of twenty twenty. Hearings first, then the tariff, is arguably what Congress wanted all along.

The critics answer with the record. Chad Bown, at the Peterson Institute, tracked Phase One and found that China bought only about fifty-eight percent of the extra American goods it had promised. And economists who studied the twenty eighteen tariffs, including teams from the New York Fed, Princeton and Columbia, found that nearly all of the cost landed on American importers and consumers, not on foreign exporters.

And the Supreme Court decided only one question: who may raise the wall. Whether a wall of fifteen to forty percent is good policy is a separate argument, and the ruling didn't touch it.

What to watch from here. The length of the comment period, and how far the final rates fall from the proposals. Whether the product tariffs from the security door stack on top, or get credited against the new ones, as Greer's office suggested. The first lawsuit testing whether excess capacity counts as a practice. And whether Beijing responds before the rates are final or waits for them. In February the courts answered how much of Congress's power a President can borrow, for one statute. For the others, the question is wide open.

Back in English, and one idea before we finish.

In two thousand five, a Greek-American physician named John Ioannidis published an essay in the journal PLoS Medicine with a title designed to start a fight: Why Most Published Research Findings Are False. His argument was arithmetic, not anecdote. If a field tests many hypotheses that are unlikely to be true, with small samples, flexible methods and a cutoff of five percent for statistical significance, then most of the positive results it publishes will be false alarms, even if nobody cheats.

The circumstance is that he had the evidence in hand. The same summer, in the Journal of the American Medical Association, he looked at forty-five highly cited clinical studies that had claimed a treatment worked. Seven were later contradicted outright, and seven more found to have overstated the effect. Only twenty were cleanly replicated.

The complication is that nobody agrees on the number. In two thousand thirteen, the biostatisticians Leah Jager and Jeffrey Leek used the published results in top medical journals to estimate a false discovery rate of around fourteen percent, nowhere near most. Critics replied that the method trusted the very published numbers in question. Then, in two thousand fifteen, a large collaboration tried to repeat a hundred psychology studies, and only about a third came out significant the second time. Whether that means most findings are false, or that the fields simply differ, is still being argued.
