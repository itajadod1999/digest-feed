One story worth more time this morning: a Hong Kong developer asking its shareholders for money at an eighty percent discount.

What happened. On Sunday, New World Development, one of Hong Kong's biggest property groups, announced a rights issue of about twenty-four point nine billion Hong Kong dollars, roughly three point two billion American. For every share they own, holders can buy eleven new ones, at ninety Hong Kong cents each. That's about eighty-one percent below Thursday's close, the last before trading was halted. When the shares resumed this morning, they fell as much as forty-seven percent, to an all-time low.

Why so deep? Start with the mechanism. In a rights issue, existing holders get first claim on new shares, so a deep discount isn't a gift to outsiders, it's a lever on insiders. If you take up your rights, you keep your slice. If you don't, you're diluted at a terrible price. Once the new shares are out, the price should settle near what's called the theoretical ex-rights price, the blended value of old and new together. With eleven cheap shares for every old one, that's about a dollar twenty-three Hong Kong, and the new shares are still priced about a quarter below it. That's what makes the offer nearly impossible to fail. Shareholders are effectively told to pay up or be left behind.

Who's behind it. Chow Tai Fook Enterprises, the Cheng family's vehicle, owns about forty-five percent, and has committed to take up its full share and underwrite most of what other holders don't, up to about eighteen point seven billion Hong Kong dollars in all. HSBC and DBS are underwriting the remaining quarter of the issue. Bloomberg reported the move followed Bain Capital walking away.

Why it needs the money. New World lost about twenty-six point eight billion Hong Kong dollars in the year to June, much of it from writedowns tied to ending its 11 Skies project at Hong Kong's airport early. The company says the issue cuts net debt to equity from about sixty-eight percent to forty-eight. Analysts quoted by the South China Morning Post say adjusted gearing would still be above seventy, and that the cash alone won't end the liquidity strain.

What the numbers mean. A family putting up most of three billion dollars is a statement of commitment. The fall this morning matters. In theory, a share that still carries its rights should hold its value until the new shares are issued, because the rights are worth what the dilution takes away. A drop of nearly half says the market is repricing the company itself, not just doing the dilution sum.

What would have to happen next. Independent shareholders vote on January fifth. After that, the question is whether Hong Kong's property market stabilizes enough for New World to sell assets at prices that don't force another round. If it doesn't, this morning's raise starts to look like the first installment.

One idea before we finish.

In nineteen twenty-one, an economist named Frank Knight published Risk, Uncertainty and Profit, built from his doctoral thesis at Cornell. Its central claim is a distinction. Risk is when you don't know the outcome but do know the odds, like a roll of dice or a fire insurer's tables. Uncertainty is when you can't even assign the odds, because the situation has never happened in a form you could count. Knight argued profit exists only because of the second kind. Measurable risk gets insured and priced away; what's left over, for people who bet on judgment, is the return for facing what can't be measured.

The circumstance is that economics was then busy proving that competition drives profits to zero. Knight, an Illinois farm boy who'd nearly become a philosopher, wanted to explain why real businesses kept earning them anyway, and he found the gap in what could and couldn't be known.

The complication came from two directions. In nineteen fifty-four, Leonard Savage argued that any rational person acts as if they have probabilities for everything, so Knight's line dissolves. Then in nineteen sixty-one a young RAND analyst named Daniel Ellsberg, years before the Pentagon Papers, posed a simple choice between urns of colored balls. Most people he asked, prominent decision theorists included, preferred bets with known odds over bets with unknown ones, even when that made their choices inconsistent. Savage's rational chooser doesn't seem to exist. Whether that vindicates Knight, or just shows people are bad at probability, is still argued.
