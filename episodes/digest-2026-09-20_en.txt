Back in English, and to the morning's subject.

Warren Buffett stepped down as chairman of Berkshire Hathaway on Friday, in a letter to shareholders. He is ninety-six. He becomes chairman emeritus and stays on the board. Greg Abel has run the company as chief executive since the end of last year. The new chairman is Howard Buffett, who is seventy-one, prefers Howie, and is his son. Father Time always wins, the letter said. He has, however, been generous with me.

Start with what Berkshire actually is, because the popular version, a man who picks good stocks, has been wrong for about forty years.

Berkshire is an insurance company with things attached. In nineteen sixty-seven Buffett bought a small Omaha insurer called National Indemnity, and that purchase is the hinge of the whole story. Here is why. An insurer collects premiums today and pays claims later, sometimes decades later. The money sitting in between is called float. It is not the insurer's money. It is money the insurer holds and will eventually pay out. But in the meantime it can be invested, and whatever it earns, the insurer keeps.

Float behaves like a loan with two extraordinary properties. It does not mature, because as old policies pay out new ones are written, so the balance persists and usually grows. And if premiums collected exceed claims and expenses, the cost of that loan is negative. You are paid to borrow. At the thirtieth of June, Berkshire's float was about a hundred and seventy-seven and a half billion dollars.

That is the machine. Buy an insurer, underwrite carefully, take the float, buy operating businesses and shares with it, use the cash those throw off to buy more insurers. Run for sixty years. The result is a textile mill in New Bedford turned into a company worth a trillion dollars, with shares up on the order of five and a half million percent. The S and P five hundred returned thirty-nine thousand percent across the same stretch. Both are absurd numbers. Only one is in a different order of magnitude.

Now the problem the succession is actually about, because it is not sentiment.

Berkshire is sitting on roughly three hundred and sixty-five billion dollars in cash, down from a record three hundred and ninety-seven billion three months earlier. The decline is the point: Abel spent it. Berkshire was a net buyer of equities in the second quarter, close to twenty billion dollars' worth, after a long stretch of selling. Meanwhile the stock has lagged. Through the middle of this year Berkshire was up about three percent against roughly thirteen for the index, a gap of ten points or more.

That gap has a history. By one count it has opened nine times since nineteen ninety, and in seven of those nine Berkshire beat the index the following year. Which is either reassuring or a demonstration of how easy it is to find a pattern in nine observations.

The pressure that builds in that situation has a name, the conglomerate discount: the idea that a collection of unrelated businesses is worth less held together than broken apart, because no investor wants all of them and management attention is finite. Berkshire owns a railroad, a utility group, See's Candies, a fifth of American Express, and a great deal of insurance. There is an argument, made seriously and not only by activists, that the parts should be separated and the idle cash handed back.

Which brings us to why the chairman is a farmer.

The chairman's job at Berkshire, as Buffett himself described it in two thousand thirteen, is narrow almost to the point of being a single task. The chairman, he said, will only have to think about whether the board may need to change the chief executive. That is it. Abel runs the company. Howie guards the culture. And Buffett has been entirely unembarrassed about the mechanism: asked last year by the Journal why his son was getting the role, he said, he is getting it because he's my son.

The qualifications are not nothing. Howard Buffett has been a director since nineteen ninety-three, thirty-three years on that board, and has sat on the boards of Coca-Cola and ConAgra. He has run his own charitable foundation since nineteen ninety-nine. He has also farmed most of his life, served as a county commissioner in Omaha, and spent a couple of years as sheriff of Macon County, Illinois, which is a sentence I did not expect to read on Friday.

Here is the honest counterargument, and it is not weak. A non-executive chairman whose principal qualification is his surname is precisely the arrangement that governance specialists spend their careers objecting to, and the defence, that he will protect the culture, only works if culture is the scarce resource. It may not be. The scarce resource may be judgement about where to put three hundred and sixty-five billion dollars, and a chairman selected for loyalty to a founder's way of doing things is not obviously the person who improves that. There is also a quieter number in the accounts. Float grew by one point one billion dollars in the first half of this year, on a base of a hundred and seventy-seven billion, and underwriting profit fell thirteen percent. The engine is not broken. It is idling.

So what would have to happen for this to matter either way. Watch the cash line each quarter. If it keeps falling, Abel is deploying and the succession worked as designed, with the chairman invisible. If it climbs back toward four hundred billion, the pressure returns, and the question becomes whether a chairman chosen to say no is the right person in the room. Buffett's design rests on a bet that the hardest thing to preserve in a company is not capital allocation, which can be hired, but the willingness to sit still. He may be right. He has been right about most of this for sixty years. But he has arranged things so that the test only runs once he cannot comment on the result.

Something lighter before we finish, and it is four thousand years old.

Researchers using a portable X-ray scanner have read an Old Assyrian letter without opening it. The tablet is still sealed inside its clay envelope, excavated from a house at Kanesh in Anatolia and dating to the first half of the nineteenth century before the common era. The envelope carries eight impressions of the seal of a woman named Anna-anna, and until now reading the letter meant breaking it and destroying those seals, so nobody had. Her husband was away on business, she had been left to collect a debt from a man called Laqepum who refused to pay, and the letter is, in essence, her telling her husband to come home and deal with it. Four thousand years, eight seals, and the message inside is about somebody not settling up.

One idea before we finish.

In nineteen twenty-two a British physicist named Lewis Fry Richardson published a book proposing that you could forecast the weather by arithmetic. Take the observations, put them into the equations governing fluids and heat, and grind forward in steps. To demonstrate it he computed, by hand, one six-hour forecast for a point near Munich, from the fullest set of observations available, in May of nineteen ten. His answer was that surface pressure would rise by a hundred and forty-five hectopascals. The actual pressure that day barely moved.

The circumstance is worth having. Richardson did the arithmetic while driving an ambulance in France during the First World War, between convoys. The manuscript was lost during fighting and turned up months later under a heap of coal. And in the book he sketched what doing this for the whole planet would take: a hall like a theatre, sixty-four thousand human computers at desks, each responsible for one patch of the earth, and a conductor on a pedestal directing them with beams of coloured light, speeding up whichever regions fell behind.

The complication arrived eighty years later. Peter Lynch went back to Richardson's own numbers and showed the method was fine. The failure was in the starting data, which held small imbalances the equations amplified into nonsense. Smooth those first, a step now called initialization and done routinely by every forecasting centre on earth, and Richardson's arithmetic on Richardson's data gives a sensible answer. Whether that means he was right, or means his procedure as published was unusable and somebody else supplied the missing half, depends on what you think a method is. Richardson had left the field by then. He resigned from the Meteorological Office in nineteen twenty, when it was absorbed into the Air Ministry and he concluded his work on how gases disperse could serve poison gas, and spent the rest of his career studying the causes of war.
