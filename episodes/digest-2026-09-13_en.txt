Now the piece.

Here is a question that sounds like it belongs in a footnote. When does it become probable that a private company will go public?

Is it probable when the board first discusses it? When the company starts talking quietly to the Securities and Exchange Commission? When it files its registration document publicly, and the whole world can read the financials? Thirty days out? Seven?

Over the past decade, Wall Street and the big accounting firms have converged on an answer that is, on its face, absurd. An initial public offering is not probable until the moment it actually happens. This is the reporting of the Times, drawing on a new paper by Sven Riethmueller, a professor at Yale Law School, and it matters because that single word governs when billions of dollars of expenses hit a company's income statement.

Start with the instrument, because none of this makes sense without it.

For most of the history of startup compensation, employees got stock options: the right to buy shares later at a price fixed today. Over the last decade options have been largely replaced by grants of actual stock that vest over time. And at large private companies, the dominant version is something called a double-trigger restricted stock unit.

Two triggers, hence the name. The first is service: you have to stay for a minimum period. The second is a liquidity event, which in practice means the company goes public. Both have to happen. If there is no offering before the grant expires, typically after seven years, the employee gets nothing at all.

The first trigger is ordinary. The second one is the clever part, and its original purpose was genuinely humane. Under a plain stock grant, an employee owes tax when the shares vest. If those shares are in a private company, they cannot be sold, so people were being handed large tax bills payable on paper wealth they could not touch. Attaching the grant to a liquidity event solves that. No offering, no shares, no tax.

But it has a second effect, which is where the money is. Because accounting rules only require an expense to be recognised once the performance condition becomes probable, and the performance condition is the offering, the company does not have to book the cost of this compensation while it is private. Options are expensed automatically over the vesting period. These are not. So a company can pay its people in stock for seven years and show almost none of it as an expense.

And then it goes public, and the bill arrives all at once.

The numbers are the part to hold onto. Robinhood averaged about six million dollars of stock-based compensation expense per quarter in the year before it listed. In the quarter it went public, it booked a catch-up charge of one billion dollars. Pinterest went from three point seven million a quarter to a catch-up expense of nine hundred seventy-five million. Those are not typos and they are not rounding. They are the same business, described two completely different ways, eight weeks apart.

Now the convention, and who built it.

Robinhood filed its registration document publicly on the first of July, twenty twenty-one, and went public on the twenty-ninth. On the thirtieth of June, one day before that filing, it took the position that its offering was not probable. Pinterest declined to recognise the expense even after its filing was public, eighteen days before it listed.

This is not one company being aggressive. Riethmueller looked at ninety-one companies with private valuations above a billion dollars that listed between twenty fourteen and twenty twenty-four, and of the fifty-one with significant deferred expenses, fifty were audited by one of the four big accounting firms. The firms' argument is that an offering is always uncertain and depends on things outside the company's control. Markets can close, and sometimes do. That is not a stupid argument. It is just an argument that has never once resolved the other way.

An accounting professor at Temple named Steven Balsam puts the objection simply: there is a principle called conservatism, which says recognise expenses as early as you can and revenue as late as you can. This does the reverse. And as Riethmueller says the practitioners will tell you privately and never in writing, once a company has filed, the odds of the offering happening are quite high.

What it does to investors is the contested part, and I want to be careful here.

Riethmueller found that companies posting catch-up charges above a hundred and seven million dollars saw an average share price decline of ten point one percent, measured from ten days before results to ten days after, adjusted for the Nasdaq. Below that threshold, the underperformance was three point two percent. He is explicit that he is not claiming the charges cause the falls — only that they are a risk factor that ought to be disclosed properly and frequently is not. Rubrik, for instance, disclosed six hundred twenty-one and a half million dollars of coming charges in its filing, and did not quantify a further four hundred seventy-five million of related costs until after it was public.

The honest counterargument comes from two directions. Academics who reviewed the paper for the Times want more statistical work. Partha Mohanram at Toronto's Rotman School made the obvious point: companies with enormous deferred compensation are unusual in other ways too, and those other ways might be what moves the stock. And from the other side, John Berlau at the Competitive Enterprise Institute argues the accounting is simply correct — that you should not charge earnings for an event that has not occurred — and that if there is a problem, it is a disclosure problem, not a measurement one.

So why does this matter this morning rather than in a seminar.

Because the pipeline is Anthropic and OpenAI. Anthropic is preparing an offering at a rumoured two trillion dollar valuation. These are companies that have spent years hiring very expensive people with very large equity grants, at a scale nobody has previously tested this convention against, and neither would comment to the Times about its use of these instruments. Whatever the catch-up charge looks like, it will be a number without precedent, and it will land in the first quarter that retail investors can own the stock.

And there is a wrinkle that could make it worse. If the Securities and Exchange Commission follows through on its proposal to let companies report twice a year instead of four times, the reckoning simply arrives later. More people will have bought in before the accounts say what the compensation actually cost.

One idea before we finish.

In the summer of nineteen forty-eight, Kenneth Arrow was a twenty-seven-year-old consultant at the RAND Corporation, which existed to do mathematics for the Air Force. The problem in front of him was game theory applied to the Cold War, and it required treating the Soviet Union as a player with preferences — a thing that wants outcomes in a consistent order. The way Arrow told the story afterward, a colleague asked him whether that was legitimate, given that a nation is not a person but a collection of millions of people who want different things.

He went looking for the rule that would combine them, and proved instead that no such rule exists. If you insist on a handful of conditions that all sound like bare minimums — that the method produce a consistent ranking, that it respond to what people actually want, that no single person decides, and that the ranking of two options not depend on some third irrelevant option — then the only procedure satisfying all of them is a dictatorship. He wrote it up that summer as a RAND document and expanded it into his doctoral dissertation in nineteen fifty-one.

The complication is that the theorem may prove less than it appears to. Amartya Sen's argument is that the result depends on how little information Arrow allows himself. The framework takes only rankings: it knows that you prefer this to that, and nothing about how much. It cannot say that one person would be devastated and another mildly inconvenienced. Admit comparisons like that, Sen argued, and the impossibility loosens considerably — which means the question was never really whether democracy is coherent, but what a voting system is permitted to know about the people voting.
