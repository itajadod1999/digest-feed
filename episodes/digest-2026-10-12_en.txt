One story worth more time this morning: what the Houthis are doing to Saudi Arabia.

What happened. Since Thursday, repeated strikes on Riyadh's main airport have killed at least seventeen people, by the combined Saudi and Reuters counts. Airlines have cancelled more than a thousand flights, the Journal reports. The heads of several Western oil majors pulled out of an industry conference in Riyadh. And America's aviation regulator has warned its airlines about Saudi airspace.

The mechanism is asymmetry, which is the Times' framing. The Houthis are firing missiles and drones that cost a fraction of what it costs to intercept them, at targets that are expensive and symbolic. They don't need to win anything. Each strike only has to make Riyadh feel less safe to land in.

The players. Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman has staked the kingdom's future on diversifying away from oil: tourism, events, investment, business conferences. The Journal reports he is now moving between heavily guarded palaces with bunkers, and that rising defence costs threaten the money for that diversification push. He has pressed Washington for direct strikes, and the President said over the weekend America may join, while also promising no attack on Iran before the November third midterms.

The numbers in context. Saturday's attack was the deadliest in the kingdom in more than a decade, and the deadliest on any Gulf Arab state since the Iran war began in February. Saudi airspace is also one of the last two major air corridors between Europe and Asia. If it narrows, the cost lands on every airline flying east.

What would make it matter more. Watch whether America actually joins the strikes. Watch whether the Houthis' claimed hits on Dammam and on two Aramco sites, which Saudi Arabia hasn't confirmed, turn out to be real, because that moves it from airports to oil. One idea before we finish.

On the seventh of November, nineteen forty, four months after it opened, the Tacoma Narrows Bridge in Washington State twisted itself apart in a wind of about forty miles an hour. It was the third-longest suspension bridge in the world. The idea behind it belonged to Leon Moisseiff, one of the most respected engineers in America, who had worked on the Manhattan Bridge and consulted on the Golden Gate.

The circumstance is the interesting part. Moisseiff's theory said a long suspension bridge could be far slimmer than anyone had dared, because the weight of the cables and deck would hold it steady. The state engineer, Clark Eldridge, had designed Tacoma with deep, twenty-five-foot stiffening trusses. Moisseiff proposed shallow eight-foot girders instead, which cut the cost dramatically and looked elegant. It won. From the first weeks the deck rose and fell so visibly that drivers came out to ride it, and locals named it Galloping Gertie. No person died in the collapse. The only fatality was a dog named Tubby, left in a car.

The complication is what actually happened. For decades, physics textbooks used Tacoma as the classic case of resonance, wind pushing at the bridge's natural frequency, like a child on a swing. In nineteen ninety-one, K. Yusuf Billah and Robert Scanlan published a paper arguing that's wrong: the wind wasn't rhythmic, and the bridge was generating its own twisting motion through what engineers call aeroelastic flutter. Most engineers now accept their account, but the textbooks were slow to change, and exactly why the motion switched from heaving to twisting that morning is still argued over.
