One story worth more time this morning: the slow unwinding of the publicly traded private credit fund.

What happened. Bloomberg reports a run of business development companies, the listed funds that lend to midsize companies, are weighing strategic options, including sales. Among them are BlackRock's TCP Capital and WhiteHorse Finance, the clearest case, which Money Stuff walked through on Monday. WhiteHorse Capital wants to move about six hundred and eighty million dollars of mostly first-lien loans out of WhiteHorse Finance into a private continuation vehicle, and then delist the fund.

The mechanism is a gap between two prices for the same thing. WhiteHorse Finance reports a net asset value of eleven seventy-seven a share, the value its own loans are marked at. But the stock trades around seven dollars, about sixty cents on the dollar. And the bids coming in for the loans themselves are reportedly in the high nineties. So the market is valuing the fund far below what buyers say they'd pay for its contents. When that happens, the obvious move is to sell the contents, hand back the cash, and close the wrapper.

Why the discount exists is the real argument. One view is that public investors simply don't believe the marks, that private loans are carried at prices nobody has tested. The other is that the discount reflects the wrapper, not the loans: fees, leverage, a small fund nobody trades, and a sector out of favour. A sale puts a real price on the loans, which is the cleanest test there is.

The players. These are mostly smaller funds in a market Bloomberg sizes at one point eight trillion dollars, which has just seen record withdrawals from its nontraded funds. And the precedents point every which way: Boaz Weinstein has bid around seventy cents for some of these funds, while Blackstone and its employees bought into its private fund at par.

What would make it matter. If the WhiteHorse sale closes near par, it hands every discounted fund a template, and pressure from shareholders to use it. If the bids fade, or the price comes in well below the marks, the sceptics were right and the marks across the sector get a much harder look.

One idea before we finish.

In nineteen eighty, the economist Julian Simon, at the University of Maryland, made a public challenge to anyone who believed the world was running out of resources. Pick any raw materials you like, he said, and a date at least a year out. If their inflation-adjusted prices rise, I pay. If they fall, you pay. Paul Ehrlich, the Stanford biologist whose book The Population Bomb had predicted mass famine, took him up on it, with two colleagues, John Harte and John Holdren.

The circumstance matters. It was the end of the nineteen seventies, after two oil shocks, with commodity prices near their highs. Ehrlich's side chose copper, chromium, nickel, tin and tungsten, a thousand dollars' worth notionally, from September nineteen eighty to September nineteen ninety. Over that decade the world added roughly eight hundred million people. All five metals fell in real terms, tin by more than half after the tin producers' cartel collapsed in nineteen eighty-five. In October nineteen ninety, Ehrlich mailed Simon a cheque for five hundred and seventy-six dollars and seven cents.

The complication is that nobody agrees on what the bet proved. In twenty ten, the economists Katherine Kiel, Victor Matheson and Kevin Golembiewski reran it over every ten-year window from nineteen hundred to two thousand and eight, and found Ehrlich would have won about sixty-two percent of them. Simon, they argued, had picked a good decade. Then a twenty twenty study measured the same metals in hours of work needed to buy them rather than in dollars, and Simon came out ahead again. Ehrlich later proposed a rematch on fifteen environmental trends. Simon declined, saying those weren't measures of human welfare, and died in nineteen ninety-eight with it unresolved.
