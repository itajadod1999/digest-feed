Now a minute of French, and then the European news in it.

How would you say you're running late? Not formally delayed. That you're behind, right now, and moving.

Here it is.

Je suis à la bourre.

The textbook gives you je suis en retard, which is correct and lands like a notice pinned to a door. And the literal route, translating running late word for word, produces nothing anyone says. À la bourre is the spoken version, and it takes pour plus a noun or an infinitive. Bourrer means to cram or stuff something full, which is the right feeling: you are crammed up against the clock.

Je suis à la bourre pour le boulot.

One story worth more time this morning, and it's a tax trade that shouldn't work.

Foreign investors in American stocks face a thirty percent withholding tax on dividends. They face nothing on capital gains. So there is an entire industry devoted to converting the first into the second, usually with swaps. Bloomberg's Zachary Mider and Denitsa Tsekova have found a version that needs no derivatives at all, and saved foreign investors about a hundred forty-seven million dollars in American tax last year.

The mechanics first. A fund about to pay a dividend has an ex-dividend date: buy on or after it and you don't get the payment. In theory the price falls that day by the amount of the dividend, because the buyer gets the same assets minus the cash. So in principle you sell the day before, buy back the next morning, and take the drop as a capital gain instead. That fails in practice, because most of the market's gains historically arrive between the close and the open, so the price moves for reasons unrelated to the dividend and you lose what you were chasing.

Here's what makes it work. There are two enormous, nearly identical S and P five hundred exchange-traded funds, one from BlackRock and one from Vanguard, and since twenty twenty-four BlackRock's ex-dividend dates have consistently fallen earlier than Vanguard's. So you hold the BlackRock fund, and the day before its ex-date you sell it and simultaneously buy the Vanguard one. You are never out of the market for a second. BlackRock's ex-date passes, its fund trades lower by the dividend while Vanguard's still carries it, and days later you sell Vanguard high and buy BlackRock back low. You have taken the dividend as price appreciation, and price appreciation is not withheld on.

This is not the famous exchange-traded fund tax machinery, the in-kind redemption trick that defers capital gains; the reporters are explicit that it's unrelated. It needs nothing more exotic than two big, liquid, near-identical funds with staggered calendars. So what ends it isn't legislation. Either issuer could move its dates.

One thing to sit with this morning.

A frog in a well cannot be talked with about the sea; he is confined to the limits of his hole. An insect of the summer cannot be talked with about ice; it knows nothing beyond its own season.

That's the Zhuangzi, in the chapter called The Floods of Autumn, in James Legge's translation. It comes out of the Warring States period in the fourth century before the common era, when China was seven kingdoms grinding each other down and a philosopher was a man looking for a patron. Zhuangzi appears to have refused the job. The story told about him is that envoys from the king of Chu offered him the premiership while he was fishing, and he asked whether a sacred tortoise would rather be dead and venerated in a temple or alive and dragging its tail through the mud, then went back to fishing.

The observation I'd make is that the argument has no floor. It says your position sets the limits of what you can be told, which is a claim the speaker is also subject to, and it never says how anyone got out of the well to learn there was a sea. Every version of this line arrives with a narrator who has quietly exempted himself.
