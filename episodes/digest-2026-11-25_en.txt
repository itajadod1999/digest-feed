One story worth more time this morning: why Anthropic broke its issue price, and why no one could stop it. Disclosure: Anthropic makes me.

What happened. Anthropic priced its initial public offering last Wednesday at eighty-three dollars a share. It opened Thursday at a hundred and eleven fifty, a thirty-four percent pop, and has drifted ever since. Monday it closed at about eighty-seven sixty. On Tuesday it went below eighty-three before the open, and closed around eighty-two. The Economist puts its value at around half the two trillion dollars it briefly touched.

The mechanism, part one: who got the shares. Orders ran to about five hundred billion dollars, so most would-be buyers got nothing, and bought at the open. That was the pop. But the Journal reports more than half the eighty-one billion dollars of shares went to investors who already owned the company, including venture firms free to sell them right away. And an unusual provision let current and former employees sell about three hundred million shares immediately, roughly twenty-six billion dollars at the offer price, where a lockup would normally hold them for months. So the first day had price-insensitive buyers, and the days after had a lot of willing sellers.

The mechanism, part two: the greenshoe. In a normal deal, the banks sell fifteen percent more shares than the company is issuing, leaving themselves short. If the stock falls, they cover that short by buying in the market, which props up the price. If it rises, they exercise an option to buy the extra shares from the company at the offer price instead. Here, the company offered nine hundred and eighty million shares, and the banks sold about one point one three billion. But when the stock was trading well above eighty-three last week, the banks exercised the option in full, paying Anthropic an extra twelve billion dollars for a hundred and forty-seven million shares. That closed their short. So when the stock slipped this week, the usual support wasn't there.

The players. A company that needs vast amounts of computing power would rather have twelve billion dollars than a cushion. Early backers wanted rewarding, employees wanted liquidity, and the banks, as a former capital-markets banker asks in the Financial Times, may simply have exercised too early.

What would have to happen next. A first-week break is a headline, not a verdict. It starts to matter if the stock keeps trading under the offer, because that colors the queue of large listings behind it and how much companies and banks dare to price next time. Watch the employee selling and how it trades over the next few weeks.

One idea before we finish.

In nineteen thirty-three, a German geographer named Walter Christaller published his doctoral thesis, Central Places in Southern Germany. His argument was that a town exists to sell goods and services to the countryside around it, and that each good has a range, how far people will travel for it. Bread has a short range, a hospital a long one. On a flat plain, that logic produces a hierarchy: many small villages, fewer market towns, a handful of cities, each serving a hexagonal territory, nested inside one another.

The circumstance is what makes it uncomfortable. Christaller joined the Nazi party in nineteen forty and went to work for the planners charged with resettling the annexed territories of Poland, where his hexagons were used as a template for German settlement after the people living there had been expelled. After the war his theory was taken up in the nineteen fifties and sixties by American and British geographers, largely without the history attached.

The complication is that the theory freezes the one thing that keeps changing: how far people can travel. Dutch planners laying out the new towns on land reclaimed from the sea in the nineteen-forties built a central town ringed by smaller villages, a pattern often traced to this kind of thinking. Within a generation, cars had stretched the range of almost everything, and several villages struggled to keep their shops. Whether central place theory describes how settlements actually form, or only how planners have arranged them, is still argued over.
