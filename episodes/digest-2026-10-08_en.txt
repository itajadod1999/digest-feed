One story worth more time this morning: who is lending the money for the chips.

What happened. In one week, three of the biggest buyers of artificial intelligence hardware went looking for debt, on the Journal's reporting. SpaceX, the forty billion we covered. Broadcom, arranging more than fifty billion dollars for the custom chip it's building for OpenAI, having approached Apollo and Blackstone, though Bloomberg puts the figure closer to thirty billion. And Oracle, in talks with Apollo and Goldman Sachs on financing the hardware for a one-gigawatt data center.

The mechanism, and Oracle's version is the instructive one. Private investors would fund a separate entity that buys the chips and leases them back to Oracle. The chips sit off Oracle's balance sheet, the lenders hold them as collateral, and Oracle pays rent. It's sale-leaseback logic, the same structure that has long financed airplanes and rail cars, now applied to graphics processors. The lenders' safety rests on two things: the strength of whoever pays the lease, and what the chips are worth if that payer walks away.

Matt Levine frames the two ways to see it. In the narrow view, everything is in the fine print: the guarantee from the investment-grade tech company, who carries the risk if the building is late, how fast the chips depreciate, the power contracts. In the broad view, you're simply betting that someone, somewhere, will always want Nvidia chips, so even if this borrower fails, the collateral finds a home.

The numbers in context. Borrowing tied to artificial intelligence in American leveraged finance has gone from about twenty billion dollars in early twenty twenty-five to eighty-eight billion this year. Finimize notes SpaceX has an investment-grade rating but isn't getting investment-grade pricing, because investors want a substantial spread over Treasuries. And these deals are moving from public bond markets into private credit, which is to say to Apollo, Blackstone and the rest.

What would make it matter. Watch the pricing when these deals close, likely not before year-end. Watch chip depreciation: if a processor's useful life turns out to be three years rather than six, the collateral is worth much less than the loan assumes. And watch whether lenders accept two-page memos about building somewhere in the universe. If they do, that tells you more about the cycle than any spread.

One idea before we finish.

In eighteen forty-one, a Scottish journalist named Charles Mackay published Extraordinary Popular Delusions and the Madness of Crowds. Its most famous chapter describes Holland in the winter of sixteen thirty-six and thirty-seven, when people supposedly traded houses for single tulip bulbs, and the collapse that followed ruined families across the country. For a century and a half, tulip mania was the textbook bubble.

The circumstance is where it gets interesting. Mackay never went to the archives. He leaned on pamphlets written right after the crash, many of them by Calvinist moralists warning against greed. In two thousand and seven, the historian Anne Goldgar published the results of years in Dutch notarial records. She found very little evidence of the ruin. She couldn't identify anyone who went bankrupt because of tulips, and the trade was concentrated among fairly prosperous merchants and craftsmen. Earlier, in nineteen eighty-nine, the economist Peter Garber had argued that high prices for the rarest bulbs were not obviously irrational, since a single striking variety could be propagated and sold for years.

The complication is that the revisionists don't agree with each other either. Earl Thompson argued the crash came down to contract terms: Dutch authorities effectively turned futures contracts into options, so buyers could walk away by paying a small fraction. On that reading, prices were rational given the rules, and the rules changed. Others still see real speculative excess in the cheaper bulbs traded in taverns that winter. Whether tulip mania was a bubble, a legal accident, or a story about a bubble is still argued over.
