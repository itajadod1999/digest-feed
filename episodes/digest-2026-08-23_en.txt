One story worth more time this morning.

At midnight on Saturday the United States imposed tariffs of fifty percent on about twenty billion dollars of Canadian goods. Wine and spirits, furniture, dairy, cement, clothing, fishing rods, hockey equipment. On Saturday morning Carney gave a speech saying Canada had been attacked, that it is at war over trade, that Donald Trump had miscalculated, and that Canada will match the tariffs dollar for dollar starting the eighth of September, the Tuesday after Labour Day. The Canadian list covers American steel, dairy, appliances, agricultural equipment, pulp and paper, and electronics.

Start with why the number is twenty billion and not four hundred. Most trade between these two countries still moves duty free under the United States, Mexico and Canada Agreement, which exempts goods meeting its content rules. Separately there is a set of sector tariffs imposed under the national-security authority, Section two thirty-two, covering steel, aluminium, copper, lumber and autos, which does not care about that agreement at all. What took effect on Saturday is a third thing, a fifty percent rate aimed at goods sitting outside both. Which is why the list reads like the contents of a general store rather than an industrial policy.

Now the two accounts, because they differ, and the difference is the story.

The American version came from the trade representative, Jamieson Greer, in an interview with the Times. He says a deal was agreed earlier in the week and Canada declined to finalise it. The offer, as he describes it, would have removed the Section two thirty-two tariff on softwood lumber entirely, cut the auto rate to fifteen percent, brought steel and aluminium down to twenty-five percent for most Canadian exports, and added coordination on aerospace supply chains and critical minerals. A senior administration official's framing is that this would have left Canada in the best tariff position of any major exporter to the United States.

Carney's version is that Washington added demands in the final hours, and he named three.

The first is autos: how Canadian content inside a vehicle gets counted, and a refusal to extend relief to medium and heavy trucks. That is Ford at Oakville and General Motors at Oshawa. He said the approach would make building there uneconomic over time.

The second is that the United States wanted to limit Canada's ability to sign trade agreements with anyone else. Canada has concluded around twenty of those recently, including with Indonesia, the Emirates and India, and that diversification is the whole of the Carney economic strategy.

The third is the one that ended it. American demands touching Canada's protections for the French language and for Quebec culture. Carney switched into French to say that part, and used the word threats.

What both sides agree on is that there was an offer, that it covered lumber, autos and metals, and that it died over things introduced late. What they disagree on is whether those things were new or already agreed. Neither government has published a text, so hold the sequencing loosely whichever version you prefer.

The third demand is the one to watch, because it is not really trade policy. Tariff rates are a price negotiation, and prices can be split. Language law and cultural protection are not divisible that way, and no Canadian prime minister can trade them without losing Quebec. Put them in the text and a commercial negotiation becomes a sovereignty question, at which point there is nothing left to sign. The same logic runs through the second demand. Those twenty agreements exist precisely as insurance against a week like this one, so a clause restricting them asks Canada to hand over the hedge as the price of the shelter.

The dependence numbers explain why he might have signed anyway. Canada sends roughly seventy percent of its exports to the United States. Of the exports specifically exposed to tariffs, eighty-one percent went south last year. The Bank of Canada already has national output about one and a half percent below where it projected before any of this began, with growth around one point one percent this year. Economists put the direct hit from this particular round at roughly four tenths of a percent of Canadian output. Small in aggregate, and concentrated enough by sector to close named plants.

Carney is nonetheless one of very few leaders anywhere who has answered American tariffs with tariffs rather than absorbing them, and he has now done it twice. That is the live variable. Retaliating while selling seventy percent of your exports into the market you are retaliating against is not an obviously strong hand, and he is doing it anyway, which suggests he has concluded the domestic politics of folding are worse than the economics of a fight.

What would make this concrete, and there are two things. The first is the eighth of September, because a two-week fuse is also a two-week window, and if the Canadian list actually takes effect it means talks did not restart. The second is scope: whether Washington extends the fifty percent beyond that twenty billion dollars, into goods the agreement currently protects. That is the move that turns a sector fight into something that shows up in both countries' inflation numbers. Until then this is twenty billion dollars, which is a rounding error in a relationship this size, and a speech about war, which is not.

One thing to sit with this morning.

When I play with my cat, who knows whether I do not make her more sport than she makes me?

That is Montaigne, from the Apology for Raymond Sebond, the longest essay in the second book, written in the fifteen seventies. He had retired at thirty-eight to the tower library of his estate near Bordeaux, and France was in the middle of its wars of religion. The essay began as a favour to his dead father, who had asked him to translate a Spanish theologian's proof of the Christian faith, and it became the most corrosive thing Montaigne wrote, a long argument that human reason has no special standing.

The observation I'd make is that the line gets quoted as charm, and it is not charming. It is the sharp end of that argument: you cannot establish which of you is the entertainment. But the sentence may undercut the case it is making. Noticing that the cat might be playing with you takes exactly the reflective step Montaigne spends the essay insisting gives us no advantage.
