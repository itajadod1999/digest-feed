One story worth more time this morning: why bond investors don't share the stock market's enthusiasm for Anthropic.

What happened. The Journal reported on Sunday that several large bond fund managers expect Anthropic, if it borrows, to be rated double B. That's the top of the junk tier, one notch below investment grade. No agency has actually assigned a rating yet. But if that's where it lands, it would be the largest speculative-grade company in America by market value.

The mechanism. Credit ratings run in letters, and the line that matters sits between triple B minus and double B plus. Above it, a company is investment grade. Below it, many of the biggest buyers of bonds, insurers, pension funds and funds with strict mandates, can hold only a little or none at all. Crossing that line is a step change, not a rounding difference.

Why double B. Anthropic isn't profitable yet, it's less than six years old, and it has no other business to fall back on. And there are the commitments. The company's amended listing documents put its spending pledges, mostly for computing capacity and data centers, at close to nine hundred billion dollars over several years. About half of that starts coming due from twenty thirty.

The players. Craig Manchuk at Osterweis Capital told the Journal he wonders how anyone prices debt from a company with pledges that size. CoreWeave, the cloud provider, is already junk. And the route Nvidia used to smooth this, guaranteeing its customers' borrowing, is narrowing; last week its finance chief said that support will be more selective.

What the numbers mean in context. Anthropic has about ninety-six billion dollars of cash, before counting what the listing raised. Fund managers think that, with other sources, covers at least a year of commitments. So the question isn't next year. It's whether, in roughly three years, the company has the earnings and the track record to cross into investment grade before the heaviest bills arrive.

What would have to happen next. Watch for a formal rating from Moody's, S and P or Fitch, and for the form the first borrowing takes. Managers expect structured or chip-backed financing, debt secured by the hardware itself, which can earn a better rating than the company would get on its own.

One idea before we finish.

In nineteen fifty-eight, an economist named W. Braddock Hickman published a study for the National Bureau of Economic Research called Corporate Bond Quality and Investor Experience. He had followed American corporate bonds from nineteen hundred to the early nineteen forties and asked a plain question: how did investors actually do, by rating? His answer was awkward for the conventional wisdom. Low-rated bonds defaulted more often, as expected, but their higher interest more than paid for the losses. Over the whole period, investors who held a broad spread of them earned more than those who stuck to the safest grades.

The circumstance is that the period included the Depression, the worst stretch for corporate credit in American history, and the result still held. The book sat on library shelves for years until a business student named Michael Milken read it, and built a career, and eventually a market, on the idea that junk was mispriced.

The complication is that Hickman's low-rated bonds were mostly fallen angels, companies that had once been investment grade and slid. Milken's market was different: bonds issued as junk from day one. In nineteen eighty-nine, Paul Asquith and two colleagues at Harvard tracked those new-issue bonds as they aged, and found about a third of the early ones had defaulted within twelve years, far worse than the annual rates the industry quoted. Which study described the real risk of junk is still argued over.
