One story worth more time this morning: why so much is breaking at once.

What happened. In about ten days, Microsoft's email and workplace software went down worldwide, a router upgrade grounded flights nationally, JPMorgan Chase had a day of slow transactions, United delayed flights, an internal update knocked out some Apple services, and the National Security Agency's classified network failed during maintenance. On Wednesday the government said it has no evidence the outages are connected, or that hackers were behind them.

The mechanism most experts now point to is patching. New artificial intelligence models, including Anthropic's and OpenAI's, can find software flaws far faster than people. Finimize notes that Google fixed four hundred and thirty-three bugs in a single Chrome update, against eleven a year earlier. Jane Fraser, who runs Citigroup, put it this way: the volume of patches going through the world at the same time is five to ten times anything seen before.

Why that breaks things. Every fix is a change to a live system, and every change carries a small chance of knocking over something nobody knew depended on the old behavior. Organizations normally ration changes, testing and staging them. When a flaw is public, or attackers may be using the same tools to find it, nobody can afford to wait, and that rationing collapses. Microsoft's outage, by its own account, came from an old bug colliding with a traffic surge.

What would have to happen next. Watch whether outages cluster around big patch releases, and whether companies start publishing longer testing windows. Fraser's warning was that this could become routine. If she's right, the cost shows up as lost hours, not headlines.

One idea before we finish.

In nineteen seventy-four, a computer scientist named Manny Lehman, then at Imperial College London, set out what became known as his laws of software evolution. The first two are the ones people quote. A program that's used in the real world must be continually changed, or it becomes steadily less useful. And as it's changed, its complexity grows, unless someone does work specifically to reduce it.

The circumstance is the interesting part. In the late nineteen sixties, IBM asked Lehman to study programmer productivity. He ended up studying the operating system for its big System three sixty mainframes instead, release by release, with Laszlo Belady. What he noticed was that the system seemed to behave like a natural object. Its growth per release followed regular patterns, almost regardless of how many people IBM threw at it or what managers planned. The organization thought it was steering the software; the data suggested the software had its own momentum.

The complication came later. Lehman's laws were built from a handful of large commercial systems run by single companies. In two thousand, Michael Godfrey and Qiang Tu looked at the Linux kernel, built by thousands of volunteers, and found it growing faster over time, not slowing as Lehman's data predicted. Others answered that much of that growth was in device drivers bolted on at the edges, not the core. Nobody has settled whether the laws describe software, or just the way big companies used to build it.
