One story worth more time this morning, and it's the one at the pump that isn't gasoline.

Diesel in America went through six dollars and fifty cents a gallon on Sunday night. A month ago it was under five sixty. That's about sixteen percent in four weeks, on the input price of nearly everything that moves.

This is not a crude shortage. Brent slipped under a hundred dollars a barrel yesterday, the first time since the ninth. Oil is expensive, but oil is not the story. The story is refining: the gap between a barrel of crude and a barrel of the particular product you want out of it, which traders call the crack. Wholesale diesel in America and Europe is around two hundred and seven dollars a barrel, more than twice February. Crude has done nothing like that. The spread has.

Three things opened it, within weeks of each other.

The first is the strait. Almost none of the Gulf's refined output is getting through Hormuz or around it. The Gulf exports finished diesel, and those cargoes have nowhere to go.

The second is Russia. Ukrainian drones have been hitting Russian refineries, and Russia has halted nearly all its diesel exports in response. Notice what striking a refinery does. It doesn't take crude off the market. It takes diesel off the market, which is precisely the barrel the world is short of.

The third is China, which has restricted exports of refined products to protect its own supply. Together, those three have pushed global diesel exports about ten percent below last year.

Why this matters more than a gasoline spike. Gasoline is a consumer good; when it gets expensive people drive less and complain. Diesel is an input. It moves freight, runs farm equipment, powers construction and most of the world's shipping. It doesn't arrive as one line in the inflation numbers. It arrives distributed across the price of everything that had to be carried somewhere, several months later. Greg Ip made this argument in the Journal and called it oil's second strike: the first hit is the pump, the second is everything downstream, and it lands next year. Which is why two Senate candidates in the President's own party spent yesterday calling for the war to end.

What would change it. Refining capacity is the slowest thing in energy to add, and the West has barely built any in decades, so relief has to come from flows resuming rather than new supply: Hormuz reopening, Russian refineries left alone, China loosening quotas. Which makes Iran's offer on the strait, if it's real, a diesel story as much as a crude one.

And the honest complication for anyone waiting on the central bank. Raising rates does not build a refinery or reopen a waterway. It reduces demand for everything else until the average comes down. That's what the Federal Reserve chose on Wednesday, and the bond market's answer, front end up and long end flat, suggests it has already worked out the cost.

One idea before we finish.

In nineteen fifty-nine Ronald Coase published a paper about the Federal Communications Commission. The agency's whole procedure, he argued, holding hearings and deciding which broadcaster best served the public interest, was solving a problem that property rights would solve better. Sell the spectrum. Let whoever values it most buy it.

The economists at Chicago thought the reasoning underneath it was simply wrong, so in the spring of nineteen sixty they invited him to Aaron Director's house to explain himself. About twenty turned up, Milton Friedman and George Stigler among them. Stigler's account is that they took a vote at the start of the evening: twenty against Coase, one for. They argued for hours, then voted again. Twenty-one for Coase, none against. Stigler said his one regret was that nobody had thought to record it.

What he had argued is that harm is reciprocal. A factory's smoke ruins the laundry next door, and Arthur Pigou's answer, forty years earlier, was to tax the factory. Coase's answer was that stopping the factory harms the factory just as surely, and that if the rights are clearly assigned and bargaining is cheap, the two reach the same arrangement regardless of who started out holding the right.

The complication is what happened next. That became the Coase theorem, and Coase spent fifty years insisting it was the wrong half of his paper. The world of costless bargaining was a device, he said, meant to show the assumption is absurd. His actual subject was the world where bargaining is expensive, which is why firms and contracts and courts exist at all. He was still saying it in his Nobel lecture in nineteen ninety-one. The profession had taken his throat-clearing and named it after him.
