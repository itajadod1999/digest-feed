Now the world.

The Himalayan flood is the largest story of the week by any measure and it grew every day. Monday it did not exist. Wednesday morning a section of glacier six hundred metres wide broke free above the Nepal-Tibet border and fell a kilometre and a quarter, melting enough of itself on the way down to become a slurry that burst dams and ran more than forty miles down two valleys. Thursday the count was a hundred sixty-five. This morning it is at least five hundred seventy-nine, with more than two thousand missing, and roughly eight percent of Nepal's generating capacity destroyed.

Late Friday the President announced what he called the biggest oil deal in world history: American majority control of more than sixty-five billion barrels of proven Venezuelan reserves. Le Monde has the shape of it, seventeen strategic fields against more than a hundred billion dollars of investment. The administration says Washington holds over half the value of a new oil company, split between equity and guaranteed at-cost supply, going to the strategic reserve and the military, at no cost to taxpayers. Venezuela's interim president, Delcy Rodríguez, said she hoped it would revive the country. Almost no other detail has been released, and Chevron is separately in talks to expand there. Announced, not documented.

The Canada trade war became permanent furniture this week. Fifty percent tariffs on Canadian cars, parts and steel from the first of January, Canadian retaliation on the eighth of September, and on Thursday an executive order renaming Lake Ontario as Lake America. The reaction was bipartisan and unkind, and businesses around Niagara Falls have said flatly they aren't changing their signs.

And Iran's war turned six months old on Friday. The Economist's framing is the one to keep. It began with overwhelming force and the expectation that the government would crack. It killed the supreme leader. Six months on the hardliners are stronger, the strait is throttled, and the leverage that remains runs through China, which has twice declined to use it. The Times calls it the President's own version of a forever war. Oil fell five percent this week anyway, because the market has decided this is a sanctions fight rather than a supply emergency.

One story worth more time this morning, and it is Friday in Jackson Hole.

Kevin Warsh has been chairman since May and has said almost nothing about the economy in that time, which economists found infuriating and which he has now defended in writing. His argument is that transparency about future policy decisions is not a virtue in itself, and that heavy forward guidance creates what he called a hall-of-mirrors problem: the market watches the Fed, the Fed watches the market, and both stop watching the economy. That is a real position, held by serious people, and it is the first coherent thing anyone outside the building has had to work with.

Then he gave the assessment anyway. Inflation on the Fed's preferred measure is three point seven percent over twelve months and four point one over six, so the recent direction is the wrong one. Fifty-four percent of the goods and services in that basket rose more than three percent over the year. Unemployment is four point one percent and flat, claims are near historic lows, and he called the labour market consistent with full employment. Inflation, he said, is the more concerning of the two. If it stays here, the Fed has, in his words, work to do.

That is not forward guidance. It is a description of conditions plus a description of the reaction function, which gets you to the same place by a different road, and the market did the arithmetic instantly. Odds of a September rise went from thirty-five percent to fifty-eight on the Chicago Mercantile Exchange's data. The two-year jumped twelve basis points. The thirty-year, which spent last week at a nineteen-year high on the suspicion that this Fed lacked the nerve to raise, actually eased.

Which is the part I'd sit with. The complaint about Warsh all summer was that his silence was costing credibility. Friday suggests something narrower: that the silence was costing the short end and buying the long end nothing, and that one paragraph of honest description bought more than three months of quiet. There is one more inflation print before the September meeting. If it comes in soft, we find out whether Friday was a signal or a mood.

One last thing from the week, and it is very old and very small. Archaeology's summer issue tells the story of three jeu de paume balls found walled into a medieval house in the old university quarter of Orléans. About an inch and a half across, woven fabric wrapped in leather and packed with compressed wool, radiocarbon-dated to the sixteenth or seventeenth century. Paume was the ancestor of tennis, and by the late fifteen hundreds everyone in France played it, kings and clergy included. Somebody lost three balls into a wall, and the wall kept them for four hundred years.

One thing to sit with this morning.

Love turns, with a little indulgence, to indifference or disgust: hatred alone is immortal.

That is William Hazlitt, from an essay called On the Pleasure of Hating, published in eighteen twenty-six. He was forty-eight and comprehensively ruined. Three years earlier he had published a book-length account of his obsession with his landlord's nineteen-year-old daughter, which destroyed what was left of his reputation. His old radical friends had gone over to the Tories, he was writing to pay debts, and he died four years later.

The observation I'd make is that the sentence is doing something sly. It offers itself as a claim about human nature and it is really a claim about durability, which is a much weaker thing. Hazlitt is not saying hatred is stronger than love. He is saying it lasts, and he is right that it does, mostly because it costs nothing to maintain and asks nothing back. What is on record is that Hazlitt's last reported words were that he had had a happy life.
