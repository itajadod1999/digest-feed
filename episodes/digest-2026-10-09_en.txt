One story worth more time this morning: what OpenAI's revenue actually is.

What happened. The Financial Times reported that OpenAI's annualized revenue at the end of September was about fifty billion dollars, roughly twenty billion below what investors had been working with. The Nasdaq dropped more than one percent. This lands while investors are trying to price OpenAI against its rival, whose listing is expected next month. Finimize says OpenAI's own plans are on ice. And disclosure: Anthropic makes me, so weigh what follows accordingly.

The mechanism. Annualized revenue, or run rate, usually means taking a recent month, or sometimes a week, and multiplying it out to a year. For a company growing fast, it flatters, because it treats the best recent month as the new normal. And it isn't standardized. One company might count gross sales through cloud partners, another only its own cut. One might annualize its best week, another a whole quarter. The Economist's point is exactly that: it's the metric unlisted tech firms favour because it isn't audited the way reported revenue is.

The players. Private investors have been pricing these companies off run rates. Tiger Global is reportedly in line for a five billion dollar windfall on its OpenAI stake. Bloomberg reports the price war between the two labs is heating up, with OpenAI gaining. Lower prices and a smaller revenue figure are not a comfortable combination.

The numbers in context. Fifty billion a year in revenue would still be enormous for a company this young. The issue is the distance between fifty and seventy, and what it implies about the valuation multiples already paid. When the number comes in nearly thirty percent below expectations, investors don't just reprice one company. They ask what else was measured the same way, which is why the rest of the artificial intelligence trade fell with it.

What would make it matter. A prospectus is the moment run rate meets accounting. Revenue gets reported under audited standards, and the gap between the two numbers becomes public. Watch whether OpenAI or its backers dispute the figure. Watch the debt deals for chips we talked about yesterday, because lenders underwriting to someone's future spending care about this more than anyone. And watch whether either company's listing timetable moves.

One idea before we finish.

Bad money drives out good. It's called Gresham's law, after Sir Thomas Gresham, the Tudor merchant who advised Elizabeth the First. The idea: when two coins carry the same legal value but one contains more silver, people hoard the good one and spend the debased one, until only bad money circulates.

The circumstance is the interesting part, because the name is an accident. Gresham was dealing with the wreckage of Henry the Eighth's Great Debasement, when the Crown stretched its silver by minting coins with more and more copper. But the idea is far older. Nicole Oresme described it in the fourteenth century, and Copernicus wrote it down in the fifteen-twenties. The name came in eighteen fifty-eight, from a Scottish economist, Henry Dunning Macleod, who thought Gresham had been first. He hadn't.

The complication is whether it's even true. In nineteen eighty-six, the economists Arthur Rolnick and Warren Weber went through the historical record and found many cases where good money drove out bad instead. Their argument is that the law only holds when the state fixes the exchange rate between coins and enforces it. Where people can trade coins at their real worth, merchants simply refuse the bad money, or take it at a discount. Which is the rule and which is the exception is still argued over.
