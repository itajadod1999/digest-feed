One story worth more time this morning: how Washington is squeezing Ukraine, and what it's trading for.

What happened. On Friday the President suspended sanctions on Russian diesel so America could import it. On Sunday he announced an energy ceasefire that neither Kyiv nor Moscow had confirmed. Since then, Ukrainian officials say America has threatened to cut off intelligence sharing and weapons sales unless Ukraine stops hitting Russian refineries. And Ukrainian officials say new speed limits on SpaceX's Starlink terminals are disrupting their drone attacks.

The mechanism is the refinery campaign. By the International Energy Agency's count, a Russian refinery was hit on average once every three days in the first eight months of this year. The Times reports Russia's gasoline output is down about twenty percent on last year and diesel nearly thirty. It also tightens global diesel supply three weeks before the midterms.

The players. The White House's case is that the deal lowers fuel costs and helps the global market, and the President has blamed Zelensky for making problems for American farmers and ranchers. A person familiar with his thinking told the Times the ceasefire was meant only to stop refinery strikes. The catch, the Times notes, is that Ukraine has effectively no working refineries left, so the truce binds one side. A bipartisan group of twelve senators, including Mitch McConnell and Susan Collins, has written that the deal damages American security and betrays Ukraine.

The numbers in context. Energy analysts estimate the diesel deal could send about four billion dollars into the Russian economy. Goldman Sachs reckons it trims wholesale prices by only a dollar or two a barrel.

What would make it matter. Watch whether intelligence sharing actually stops, because that's the tool that warns Ukrainian cities of incoming missiles. Watch whether Ukraine keeps hitting refineries anyway. And watch whether any of those twelve senators move from letters to legislation, since Congress banned Russian energy imports until the war ends.

One idea before we finish.

In late August of eighteen fifty-four, cholera broke out around Broad Street in Soho, London, and killed more than six hundred people in a few weeks. A doctor named John Snow, who lived nearby, believed the disease spread through contaminated water, not through bad air, the miasma that nearly every authority blamed. He went door to door, mapped the deaths, and found them clustered around one public pump. On the seventh of September he put his case to the local parish board, and the next day they removed the pump's handle.

The circumstance is better than the legend. Snow's key ally was a local curate, Henry Whitehead, who set out to disprove him and instead found the likely source: a baby at number forty Broad Street whose nappies were rinsed into a cesspool a few feet from the well.

The complication is that the famous handle may not have ended anything. Deaths were already falling steeply before it came off, as people fled the district, and Snow himself conceded the point. The government's own inquiry in eighteen fifty-five rejected his water theory and stuck with miasma. Snow died in eighteen fifty-eight, still unvindicated, and it took another London outbreak in eighteen sixty-six before the statistician William Farr came round. Whether Snow's map changed minds, or the next decade of evidence simply wore the miasma theory down, is still argued over.
