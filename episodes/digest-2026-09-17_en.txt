To the city, where the fight is over pipe.

New York requires cast-iron plumbing in large new residential buildings. Jonathan Bowles, who runs the Center for an Urban Future, calls that a maddening bureaucratic hurdle and wants the city to allow plastic, which is cheaper and faster to install. The city already permits it up to five stories, a change made in twenty twenty-two. Most other American cities follow one of two model codes that allow it more broadly. New York wrote its own.

The objections are not frivolous. Plastic burns and melts and gives off corrosive smoke, which is why other codes require iron for the risers running floor to floor and allow plastic only for the branch lines into apartments. It is also louder, and one master plumber told the Times that people are very, very sensitive to any type of sound. And the trade is protecting jobs, because plastic goes in faster.

Now one story worth more time this morning.

The Russia sanctions bill matters more than the vote count suggests, because of what it targets. Previous measures went after Russian banks, Russian officials, and the price of Russian oil. This one goes after the buyers and the boats.

Start with the boats. The shadow fleet is several hundred ageing tankers with opaque ownership, frequently reflagged, carrying Russian crude without Western insurance. It exists because of the price cap the Group of Seven imposed in twenty twenty-two, which let Western shippers and insurers handle Russian oil only if it sold below sixty dollars a barrel. The intent was to keep the oil flowing while capping the revenue. Instead Russia bought a fleet and stopped needing Western services. The cap became optional and the revenue recovered.

Now the buyers. India and China take the overwhelming majority of seaborne Russian crude. The bill lets the President tariff goods from countries that buy Russian energy by up to a hundred percent. That is not a sanction on Russia. It is a tax on American importers of Indian and Chinese goods, designed to make those governments tell their refiners to stop.

Which is where it gets hard, and why members who wanted the sanctions still voted against the bill. A hundred percent tariff on India or China is an enormous economic act whose consequences arrive domestically and immediately. The authority is also discretionary: he has to choose to use it, against countries he is simultaneously negotiating with, one of whose leaders is here next week.

So watch two things: whether he signs it promptly, and whether Treasury designates any shadow-fleet vessels afterwards. Designating tankers is the part that works without starting a trade war, and the part that tells you whether the intent is enforcement or leverage.

One idea before we finish.

On the tenth of August, sixteen twenty-eight, the Swedish warship Vasa sailed out of Stockholm harbour, travelled about eleven hundred metres, caught a light breeze, heeled over, took water through her open gun ports and sank. Around thirty of the hundred and fifty aboard drowned. On paper she was among the most heavily armed ships in the world. She had been afloat about twenty minutes.

The circumstance is that nearly everyone involved had done their job. Gustavus Adolphus was at war and kept changing the specification. The master shipwright, Henrik Hybertsson, died in sixteen twenty-seven with the hull incomplete, and Dutch shipwrights of that period built from experience rather than drawings, so his successor inherited a ship without a plan. The crews were Swedish and Dutch and their measuring rulers differed by roughly two-thirds of an inch in the foot, which is how the hull came out asymmetric. And weeks before sailing, Admiral Klas Fleming watched thirty men run side to side across the deck as a stability test. They stopped after three passes because she rolled so violently they feared she would go over. She sailed anyway.

The complication is that the inquiry that September found nobody guilty, and reading the record it is hard to say who should have been. The king had approved the plans and the armament. The shipwright was dead. The admiral ran the test and let her sail, but the king was waiting. Historians have argued since she was raised in nineteen sixty-one over whether it was the ballast, the second gun deck, or the rulers.
