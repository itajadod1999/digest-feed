Now two stories worth more time, and the first is Lower Manhattan.

Tenants leased roughly four million square feet south of Canal Street in the first half of this year, excluding renewals. More than double the year before, and Downtown's strongest first half since twenty nineteen. The mechanism has two halves. The first is price. Downtown asking rents averaged sixty-one dollars and ninety-one cents a square foot in July, against eighty-five seventy-seven in Midtown and eighty-six thirty-four in Midtown South. A twenty-four dollar discount, in a market where Midtown supply has tightened enough that the discount is now the deciding factor rather than a consolation.

The second half is subtraction. Office-to-residential conversion is pulling the worst Downtown buildings out of the inventory permanently. Goldman Sachs's old offices at 55 Broad Street are becoming apartments. Every conversion improves the vacancy rate of what's left without a single new lease being signed.

You can see the result in the rents. Downtown asking rents rose eight percent year over year in July, the highest since twenty twenty, against four percent in Midtown and two in Midtown South. The cheapest submarket is raising rent fastest. The thing to watch is whether that discount closes far enough to stop the conversions, because the conversions are half of why it's working.

The second story is that six billion dollars, and why it disappointed.

A buyback is the Treasury going into the secondary market and purchasing its own outstanding bonds, usually older, less-traded ones. It doesn't reduce the debt. It takes supply out of the corners of the market where liquidity is thinnest, so dealers can hold and trade the rest without demanding as much compensation. Done well, it shaves a little off borrowing costs.

The scale is the point. When the Treasury announced this last month it committed to at least doubling the programme, to four billion. Yesterday it came in at up to six. More than promised, and the market pushed the ten-year to a nearly three-year high anyway.

The honest reading is that six billion is not a large number set against the long end of the American government bond market, and this was never going to fix it. What sets that yield is the supply of new debt, the inflation path, and whether foreign buyers keep showing up. What would make it matter is the programme scaling into something continuous and large. For now it's a signal, and the signal got read.

One idea before we finish.

In the nineteen forties, Barbara McClintock was growing maize at Cold Spring Harbor, tracking why kernels came out mottled instead of uniformly colored. What she concluded, by nineteen fifty, was that certain genetic elements physically move around the chromosome, switching other genes on and off as they land. Genes were supposed to sit still. That was most of what a gene was understood to be.

The circumstance is worth having. She wasn't an outsider. She'd been elected to the National Academy of Sciences in nineteen forty-four and made president of the Genetics Society of America the year after, before any of this. She presented the work at Cold Spring Harbor in nineteen fifty-one to a room that largely didn't take it up, then spent two decades mostly not publishing on it. The Nobel came in nineteen eighty-three, at eighty-one.

The complication is what the resistance was actually to. The familiar version is that a woman was ignored for thirty years. The historian Nathaniel Comfort went through her papers and argued the neglect story was built afterward, partly by her, and that transposition itself was accepted reasonably quickly once maize genetics had company. What stayed unaccepted was her larger claim, that these moving elements were the mechanism controlling development generally. Whether that makes the standard story wrong or just imprecise depends on which claim you think she was making, and people who have read the same notebooks disagree.
