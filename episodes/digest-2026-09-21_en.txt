One story worth more time this morning.

The Journal reports that few investors are in more pain from rising rates than America's apartment landlords, on a two trillion dollar debt problem that is getting worse. The mechanism is not the one people assume.

In twenty twenty-one and twenty twenty-two, an enormous amount of apartment property was bought with floating-rate bridge debt. Buy a building, renovate the units, raise the rents, refinance into cheap long-term agency debt in three years. Because the loan floated, the borrower had to buy an interest-rate cap, a contract that pays out if the reference rate goes above a set strike. Caps were cheap then, because nobody was pricing upside.

Every leg of that broke. Caps bought for a few hundred thousand dollars started costing millions to replace. Rent growth in the Sun Belt flattened as a record wave of supply delivered, and insurance and taxes ran away. Net operating income came in below plan at the exact moment debt service went above it.

Now the maturities arrive. Roughly sixty percent of loans from that vintage come due in the second half of this year, part of about nine hundred and thirty billion dollars of commercial property debt maturing in twenty twenty-six, a number swollen by extensions handed out while everyone waited for rates to fall. They did not fall. The Federal Reserve raised on Wednesday.

And here is what makes this a refinancing problem rather than a default problem, which is the distinction worth holding. A lender today underwrites at a higher rate, at lower leverage, and demands stronger debt-service coverage. Put those together and the new loan is simply smaller than the old one. A building that supported a sixty million dollar loan in twenty twenty-one might support forty-five million now, at the same occupancy, with tenants paying more rent than before. The borrower finds fifteen million dollars of fresh equity or sells. Nothing has gone wrong with the building.

The honest counterweight is that this is stress, not collapse. Distress runs around five point seven percent of a two and a half trillion dollar multifamily debt market, and delinquencies at the agencies and at banks are still modest. Concentrated pain in identifiable vintages is not a systemic event.

What would change it is the extensions. A lender extends when it believes the value is there and time is the only missing ingredient. If extensions stop and sales start, the marks on everybody else's books move with them, and a refinancing problem becomes a pricing problem fast.

One oddity to round it out. Archaeologists have worked out the rules of a Roman board game nobody could read: a limestone slab from Coriovallum, modern Heerlen in the Netherlands, carved in the last two centuries of the empire. They scanned it, found one diagonal worn noticeably smoother than the rest, then set two artificial-intelligence agents to play tens of thousands of games against each other under a hundred-odd rule sets borrowed from known ancient games. The answer is a blocking game, the family that includes noughts and crosses. Those had only ever been documented in Europe from the Middle Ages. This one is fifteen hundred years older.

One idea before we finish.

In nineteen forty-nine a New Zealander named Bill Phillips built a machine out of perspex tanks, pipes and coloured water, and used it to model the British economy. Money flowed as water. Tanks held savings, tax revenue, investment. Open a valve and you could watch government spending drain from one reservoir into another, the levels settling in front of you in real time. He demonstrated it to a room of sceptical economists and it worked perfectly.

The circumstance is the interesting part. Phillips was not an economist. He had been an electrical engineer, then a soldier, then a prisoner of the Japanese for three and a half years, where he built a hidden radio out of scrap. He came to the London School of Economics to study sociology, found the economics incomprehensible as words, and built the machine because hydraulics was the language he already had, assembling it in a garage for a few hundred pounds.

The complication is what happened to his name. Thinking about where the hydraulic model failed led him, in nineteen fifty-eight, to a paper plotting British unemployment against the rate of change of wages across nearly a century. It showed an inverse relationship, it became the Phillips curve, and within a decade it had hardened into a policy instrument: governments believed they could pick a point on it and buy lower unemployment with a little more inflation. Milton Friedman and Edmund Phelps argued the relationship dissolves once people anticipate inflation, and the nineteen seventies appeared to prove them right. Phillips had offered an observation about one country over one stretch of time. Whether a man owns the doctrine built on his data is a question nobody has settled, and he never much bothered defending it.
