One story worth more time this morning: France's bond market, and why it now looks like Europe's weak link.

What happened. On Thursday, France's ten-year borrowing cost touched nearly four point nine six percent, the highest since two thousand and two. The gap over German debt widened past one point two percentage points, the widest since twenty twelve. And France now pays more to borrow than Italy, and by the Journal's account, more than Greece. The euro fell to a seventeen-month low.

The mechanism. A government's borrowing cost is a running vote on two things: how much it needs to borrow, and whether it can be trusted to stop. France scores badly on both. Its budget deficit is the largest among its big peers except America's. And it has been governed for two years by minority cabinets that struggle to pass anything. When the global rate tide rises, as it has, the weakest credits feel it most, and hedge funds unwinding crowded bets made the selloff sharper last week.

The numbers in context. The prime minister, Sébastien Lecornu, warned last week that reality is catching up with France. Under the twenty twenty-seven budget, interest on the debt rises by twelve billion euros, to about ninety-one billion. The budget asks for forty-three billion euros of savings. And that's the collision: the students on the streets today are protesting run-down schools and teacher shortages, in a country whose interest bill is climbing faster than almost anything it could cut.

The players. The government wants to show the bond market discipline, the unions and students want more money for schools, and a presidential election is six months away.

What would make it matter. Watch whether the ten-year crosses five percent, a psychological line. Watch whether today's protests broaden into a general anti-government movement, which the Economist says the government fears. And watch the ratings agencies. A downgrade forces some investors to sell, and that's how a slow selloff becomes a fast one.

One idea before we finish.

In the nineteen seventies, a World Bank transport researcher named Yacov Zahavi noticed something odd in travel surveys from very different cities. People's average daily travel time seemed to hover around an hour, whether they walked, rode buses or drove. Faster transport didn't save them time. They just went farther. In nineteen ninety-four the Italian physicist Cesare Marchetti pushed it further and argued it was an anthropological constant.

The circumstance is what made it compelling. Marchetti pointed to city walls. Ancient Rome, medieval towns, even Venice, rarely grew wider than you could walk across in about an hour. Then horse trams, trains and cars arrived, and cities sprawled outward almost exactly in step with the speed of the new transport. On that reading, a new highway doesn't shorten commutes. It makes the city bigger.

The complication is that the constant doesn't hold up when you look closely. In two thousand and four, Patricia Mokhtarian and Cynthia Chen reviewed decades of studies and found that travel time budgets varied a lot by income, age, job and city, and only looked stable as broad averages. American commutes have also lengthened since then. Whether there's a real human limit on time spent moving, or just a statistical coincidence in averages, is still argued over.
