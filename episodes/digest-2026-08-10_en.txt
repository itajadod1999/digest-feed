Starting with markets.

Friday closed the best week since April for all three American indexes. The S and P five hundred finished at a record just under seventy-seven sixty. The Nasdaq closed near twenty-six thousand seven hundred, the Dow a shade above fifty-four thousand. The ten-year Treasury sits at about four point six six percent. Europe is at records too, with both the German and French benchmarks at new highs.

This week is about prices. The July consumer price index lands Wednesday, producer prices Thursday, retail sales Friday. Consensus has headline inflation at roughly three point four percent, down a touch from June, and core at two point five. Both still well above the Federal Reserve's two percent target. After the jobs report, traders pushed the expected timing of the next rate hike out to December from October.

Brent crude rose about one percent to around eighty-four dollars a barrel. Which is worth sitting with. The Strait of Hormuz has been shut for months, and Brent has never come near the hundred and fifty dollars a lot of analysts forecast for exactly this scenario. That's the second deep dive.

Berkshire Hathaway's numbers landed Saturday, and I flagged them as the thing to watch this week. They were a real change. Ten billion went into Alphabet, four and a half billion into buybacks, better than five billion into other equities. Berkshire was a net buyer of stocks for the first time in three years. Warren Buffett spent his last three years in the job doing precisely the opposite.

Now real estate.

The largest American apartment sale since June of twenty twenty-four closed last week. BlackRock paid one point six three billion dollars for eleven properties from Camden Property Trust. Thirty-six hundred and twenty units across Los Angeles, Orange, Riverside and San Diego counties, ninety-six percent occupied. JLL brokered it and arranged five hundred sixty-six million dollars of agency acquisition financing. For Camden it is an exit from California after twenty-eight years.

Now a number that needs context, because on its face it contradicts what I told you yesterday. CRED iQ puts July distress across the commercial mortgage-backed securities universe at ten point nine one percent. The specially serviced share is ten point three eight, up forty-two basis points on the month. Office is the outlier at sixteen point six five percent. Multifamily is eleven point two one.

Yesterday I gave you Trepp's delinquency rate at seven point eight six. Both are correct. CRED iQ counts any loan that is specially serviced or thirty days late, which picks up loans handed to a special servicer while still paying interest. Trepp's headline counts payment delinquency. The gap between them is, roughly, the loans sitting in negotiation rather than default. That group is now the larger of the two.

And one for the rent roll. The average asking rent in the San Francisco metro is three thousand seven hundred twenty-eight dollars a month, according to CoStar. Up eighteen percent in under two years, and past New York for the most expensive rental market in the country, for the first time since twenty nineteen.

Over to technology.

Mark Zuckerberg published a fourteen-page essay this morning laying out Meta's philosophy on artificial intelligence, and released an open-weight model called Muse Glimmer alongside it. The pitch is that an open model close in ability to Meta's best closed model can run locally on a laptop, which would change the economics of the industry. He argued explicitly against centralizing control of the technology, which is a shot at Anthropic and OpenAI.

He also proposed that companies submit models for government review repeatedly during training rather than once at the end. And Meta is setting up a one billion dollar fund for police and fire departments in the towns that host its data centers.

That last one is the tell. Local opposition to data centers has become a real constraint on where they get built. The Journal reports the backlash is pushing developers toward the Permian Basin in West Texas and New Mexico, where a new land rush is under way.

Over to France.

Following up on yesterday's arithmetic problem. Paris is drafting the twenty twenty-seven budget now, and the shape of it is out. Three billion euros of additional savings, two from the state and one from social security. The framing is openly defensive: hold the line until the next majority arrives. Without correction, the deficit reaches five point nine percent of output by twenty twenty-seven. Debates begin in October.

And the Middle East.

The Strait of Hormuz talks have gone backwards. On Saturday, Mohammad Bagher Zolghadr, then the secretary of Iran's Supreme National Security Council, published a list of conditions. The United States must lift the naval blockade, end sanctions, release frozen Iranian assets, end the war, and pay reparations. The strait, he wrote, does not open until America corrects its behavior. Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi still calls the arrangement with Oman very close, while ruling out direct talks for now. Then yesterday, Iran replaced Zolghadr. More on that shortly.

Separately, Prime Minister Netanyahu rejected the fifteen-point Gaza plan backed by the Trump administration's Board of Peace. He told his cabinet the army will not withdraw until Hamas is, in his words, genuinely disarmed. Heavy weapons, less-heavy weapons, all weapons. Nickolay Mladenov, the Board of Peace's representative for Gaza, said the plan remains the only way forward. It is a rare public break between Netanyahu and the president.

One other thing, closer to the kitchen. The cyclospora outbreak tied to iceberg lettuce now spans fifteen states, with regulators tracking about twenty-two thousand confirmed and suspected cases. One more small thing before the deep dives.

One story worth more time this morning. Private markets have a plumbing problem, and it is showing up in two places at once.

Yesterday I walked through the redemption gates in semi-liquid private credit funds. Investors asking for money back, funds prorating, and the word semi doing all the work. Today the Journal reports the layer underneath, which is the loans themselves. Default rates are at recent highs, and its analysis of quarterly filings finds loan health and investor returns both getting worse, even while managers describe the trouble as behind them.

Now put that next to private equity, which is running its own version of the same problem.

The Times reports that as of June thirtieth, private equity firms held thirty-three thousand five hundred seventy-five companies they have not sold. That is double the number a decade ago, and it has grown three years running.

Consider what should have fixed it. Stock indexes are at records, and the first half of this year was the second busiest for initial public offerings in more than a decade. And still: since twenty twenty-two, only seventy private equity-backed companies have listed on American exchanges.

Two reasons. Higher rates mean the natural buyer, which is another private equity firm using debt, cannot pay what the seller wants. And these portfolios are heavy in software, where valuations have compressed as investors question what artificial intelligence does to future earnings.

Here is the number that turns a timing problem into an investor problem. From July first, twenty twenty-two through March thirty-first of this year, American private equity returned six point four percent annualized, according to MSCI. The S and P five hundred returned fifteen point two over the same window. The Nasdaq returned nineteen point three. The asset class was sold on an illiquidity premium: accept that you cannot sell for years, get paid more for the inconvenience. For nearly four years, the premium has been negative.

And the two halves feed each other. Pension funds and endowments that cannot get money out of private equity still have capital calls to meet. The semi-liquid credit wrapper is the one place in their private book with a redemption window, so that is where they knock. Which is part of why redemption requests keep running above the caps, and why the gating story and the exit story are one story seen from opposite ends.

What breaks the logjam is a real exit market. Either a sustained window where sponsor-backed companies list at prices sellers accept, or firms taking lower prices and clearing inventory. The first has been promised for three years running. The second is what eventually happens, at marks below where the assets are carried today. Watch whether any large sponsor is willing to be the first to sell cheap in public.

The second story. Why the oil market refuses to panic about a closed Strait of Hormuz, and what changed in Tehran this weekend.

Start with the price. Roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil normally moves through that channel, and before the war the standard scenario for a closure put crude at a hundred and fifty dollars or above. It is eighty-four.

The Economist's answer is that China simply stopped buying as much. Chinese imports fell, and because China is the largest importer of crude on earth, its demand is now the variable that clears the market. On this reading, China has displaced OPEC plus as the great oil power. OPEC plus manages supply. China manages demand. Demand turned out to be the stronger lever.

There is a second mechanism in China's own numbers this morning. Factory gate prices rose three and a half percent over the year, down from four point one, and consumer prices rose half a percent, the smallest rise since January. The statistics bureau credited part of both to lower domestic oil prices. So the country holding the crude price down is importing disinflation from having done it.

Now the politics, because cheap oil is why everyone could afford to be patient, and patience just got harder.

Saturday's Iranian demands are essentially the June memorandum, which Iran says the United States violated and which Washington has not agreed to restore. President Trump's response, given to Axios by phone, was that the United States is low-keying it. He said Washington is only semi-negotiating, and that he is watching Iranian inflation and the fact that Tehran has no money.

Then yesterday Tehran changed the person who runs the file. Mohsen Rezaei is seventy-two. He ran the Revolutionary Guard for sixteen years, through most of the Iran-Iraq war. He now holds the secretary's job at the Supreme National Security Council while also serving as the Supreme Leader's representative to that same council. That second part is not a small detail. It collapses two separate lines of authority into one person. He is also on record supporting Iranian control of the waterway, which is the precise thing the White House has said it will not sign.

Here is where accounts differ, and it is worth being plain. Everyone agrees on the facts. The appointment happened, the demands were published, nothing is signed, the strait is not open. Outlets disagree on the meaning. Some read the change as a hardening, the old guard taking the file back. Others read it as consolidation that could make a deal easier to deliver, since a fragmented Iranian leadership is part of why nothing has closed.

What to watch is narrow. Whether ship traffic through the strait moves above a trickle, which is the only measure here that is not a statement. Whether that list of five demands shrinks. And the oil price on a day with no headline attached to it. If Brent can sit at eighty-four with the strait shut, the leverage Tehran believes it holds is worth less than Tehran thinks.

One last small thing before the close, and it is an old one.

At Phaleron, near Athens, there is a mass grave of men buried shackled together, roughly twenty-six hundred years old. The assumption for decades was that they were outsiders. A team at Arizona State tested strontium isotopes in tooth enamel from sixty-six of them, plus ninety-seven other adults at the site. Enamel forms in childhood and never renews, so it records where a person grew up. Out of one hundred sixty-three people, seven were not local. The shackled men were Athenians.
