One story worth more time this morning: how much is a private loan worth on a day when nothing happens?

What happened. Two weeks ago, Apollo began publishing daily valuations across its roughly eight hundred and fifty billion dollar credit business. This week, at an industry conference in Beverly Hills, its rivals pushed back. Ted Koenig of Monroe Capital called daily marks on illiquid assets "an illusion of precision, not accuracy." Drew McKnight, co-chief executive of Fortress, said that if you're simply marking a book against a leveraged loan index, it tells you nothing about whether a given loan is actually worth par.

The mechanism. A public bond has a price every second. A private loan to a mid-sized company doesn't trade at all, so the lender values it, usually once a quarter, using the borrower's financial statements, comparable deals and a model. Between those dates, if nothing has changed at the borrower, the mark tends to sit still, even if public credit markets fall several points.

That's the heart of the argument, as Matt Levine laid it out in Bloomberg's Money Stuff on Thursday. If your private loans truly have nothing to do with public credit, then marking them to a public index is wrong. But they're loans to companies, much like the loans that trade in public. When the public ones fall two points in a day, it's hard to argue yours are worth exactly what they were yesterday.

Why it matters. Private credit's pitch to investors has partly been steadiness: returns that don't swing with the market. That steadiness is worth a lot, and it helps justify fees far above an index fund's. Daily marks that track public credit would make portfolios look more volatile. Their defenders would say more honest, and that matters more now that these funds are being sold to individual savers, not just pensions and insurers. And the timing is pointed: this was the week public credit wobbled over artificial intelligence debt, much of which sits in private funds.

What would make it matter. Whether other big managers follow Apollo, or whether regulators start asking the same question. The first quarter in which daily marks and quarterly marks visibly disagree will be the real test.

One idea before we finish.

On the seventh of November nineteen-forty, the Tacoma Narrows Bridge in Washington State tore itself apart in a steady wind of about forty miles an hour, four months after it opened.

The circumstance is the interesting part. The local engineer's design called for a deep, stiff truss under the deck. Leon Moisseiff, a New York engineer who had worked on the Manhattan and Golden Gate bridges, proposed something far slimmer and cheaper, backed by a theory that long suspension bridges could do without heavy stiffening. Money was tight, the federal funders liked the savings, and Moisseiff's design was built. From opening day it bounced so visibly that locals called it Galloping Gertie.

The complication is the explanation. Generations of physics textbooks taught that the wind matched the bridge's natural frequency, a textbook case of resonance, like a singer shattering a glass. In nineteen ninety-one, Yusuf Billah and Robert Scanlan argued in the American Journal of Physics that this was wrong: a steady wind has no rhythm to match. What killed it, they said, was flutter, where the twisting of the deck itself pulls energy out of the wind and feeds the next twist. Engineers largely agree with them now. Many textbooks still haven't changed, and exactly how the early vertical bouncing turned into the fatal twisting is still argued over.
