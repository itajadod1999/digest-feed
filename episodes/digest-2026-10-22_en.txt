One story worth more time this morning: the bug in a large share of the internet.

What happened. On Wednesday the volunteers who maintain PHP, the programming language behind an estimated seventy percent of websites, including everything built on WordPress, released emergency fixes for two critical flaws. Both let an attacker run their own code on a server without logging in, and both are rated nine point two out of ten. Wiz, the security firm Google owns, says every PHP release of the past decade is hit by at least one of them. Researchers from Wiz, OpenAI and a firm called Calif found them with the help of artificial intelligence models.

The mechanism. The usual discipline is coordinated disclosure: researchers tell the maintainers privately, a fix is built, and the bug is announced with the patch. Here the group pre-announced on Monday that a fix was coming, so administrators could prepare, then released it Wednesday morning. The trouble is that a patch is also a map. Attackers compare the old code with the new, find what changed, and build an exploit. That used to take days or weeks. Cloudflare saw attacks on Wednesday, and proof-of-concept code is already public. Finland and the Netherlands took some government sites offline rather than wait.

The players. The PHP Foundation is a loose volunteer collective. The bug was flagged years ago but judged too complex for a human to exploit. The models that found it can presumably help weaponise it too.

What would make it matter. Whether a major site falls in the next few days. And the long tail: servers running old PHP versions that no longer get fixes at all. The open question for everyone in security is how you ship a fix to millions of machines when the fix itself tells attackers where to look.

One idea before we finish.

In nineteen sixty-one, Jane Jacobs, a writer at Architectural Forum with no planning degree, published The Death and Life of Great American Cities. What made a neighbourhood safe and alive wasn't open space and clean separation of uses, but density, short blocks, a mix of old and new buildings, and shops and homes together, so there were always eyes on the street.

The circumstance is what made it a fight rather than a book. She lived on Hudson Street, and Robert Moses, the most powerful builder in New York, wanted to run Fifth Avenue's traffic through Washington Square Park and later drive an expressway across Lower Manhattan. In the same year the book came out, the city proposed clearing part of the West Village for urban renewal. Jacobs chaired the committee that stopped it. Moses lost the park road and, eventually, the expressway.

The complication is that history has not settled who was right. Historians including Kenneth Jackson and Hilary Ballon argued in two thousand seven that Moses built bridges, parks and pools the city still depends on. And the economist Edward Glaeser has argued that Jacobs' own preference for low, old buildings, enshrined in landmark districts and community review, made the neighbourhoods she defended scarce and expensive, the opposite of the cheap, mixed streets she wanted. Whether her tools outlived her intentions is still argued at every zoning hearing.
