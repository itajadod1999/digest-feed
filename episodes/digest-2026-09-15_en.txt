And a story about float, in the least likely place imaginable.

Guidepost Montessori, the preschool chain run by a company called Higher Ground, has gone into bankruptcy, and the mechanism behind it is a real estate one. When you sign a commercial lease, the landlord frequently hands you cash upfront to build out the space — a tenant improvement allowance. So opening a preschool generates cash, while operating one consumes it. Higher Ground opened thirty-three schools in twenty twenty and twenty-one more the next year, and as long as it kept opening, the money kept arriving. Cumulative losses reached a hundred fifty-four point six million dollars. The co-founder says the model is sound and widely misunderstood. Staff at one school called it the Montessori Ponzi.

How would you say that something suits you. Not that you like it, and not that you approve — that it happens to work out well for you, that it makes your life easier. A friend offers to move dinner to Thursday and that is better for you anyway.

Ça m'arrange.

Two wrong routes, and you have probably taken both. The first translates the English directly and says the thing works for you, which in French says something about employment. Now one story worth more time this morning.

Go back to that five percent, because the standard explanation is incomplete. There is a fourth participant in the bond market, and it is newer than the other three.

Hedge funds now hold a record seven percent of the Treasury market. That is the Journal's reporting this morning. They came into the year with about two trillion dollars of government paper, in a market trading one point two trillion dollars a day, and the New York Fed has begun quietly asking investors whether new risks are creeping in.

The change is mostly about who left. For decades the marginal holder of long-dated Treasuries was a pension fund or an insurer — an institution with liabilities stretching thirty years out, wanting matching assets, holding what it bought. Those buyers have been stepping back, and what filled the space is a much faster kind of money.

A great deal of it is doing one trade, and that trade is the fault line. It is called the basis trade. A Treasury bond and a futures contract on that same bond should track each other almost exactly, because they are claims on the same cash flows. In practice a small gap opens between them, a few basis points. A fund buys the bond, sells the future, and collects the difference when the two converge.

A few basis points is not a business. So the fund borrows against the bonds it has just bought, in the overnight repurchase market, and runs the trade at fifty or a hundred times leverage. Now it is a business.

The trade is not reckless on its own terms. The danger is the lender asking for more collateral on a day when prices are moving quickly. The position has to be cut, which means selling Treasuries into a falling market, which moves prices further, which produces more collateral calls. That is roughly what happened in March of twenty twenty, when the deepest market in the world briefly stopped working and the Federal Reserve had to buy hundreds of billions of bonds to restart it.

So the holder base has changed in a way that matters at precisely the wrong moment. A pension fund watching yields reach five percent does nothing. A leveraged fund may be obliged to do something, and the something is selling.

Nor is this only an American arrangement. Le Monde reported this morning that hedge funds are increasingly speculating on French government debt, in the same week French yields hit their highest since two thousand eight. Two papers, two countries, one observation.

What would make this more than a structural footnote. Watch the repurchase market rather than the ten-year. If overnight funding costs spike, that is leverage being called in, and it shows up there first. The yield is the symptom.

One idea before we finish.

In nineteen eleven, Alfred Wegener came across a paper listing identical fossils on opposite sides of the Atlantic — plants and land animals that could not possibly have swum. The accepted explanation was land bridges: strips of continent that had risen, ferried the species across, then conveniently sunk. Wegener decided that was a worse idea than the one nobody would say out loud, which is that the continents had once been joined and had moved apart.

The circumstance is that he was the wrong man entirely. Wegener was a polar meteorologist and a balloonist — he and his brother had once held the world endurance record for staying aloft — who spent his career on the Greenland ice cap, where he died in nineteen thirty. The profession noticed the credentials. The president of the American Philosophical Society said that to believe Wegener, they must forget everything learned in the last seventy years and start over.

The complication is that his critics had a real argument and he had no answer to it. Wegener proposed that continents ploughed through the ocean floor, driven by the earth's rotation and by tidal forces. Harold Jeffreys had the mathematics to check it, and showed those forces were orders of magnitude too weak — that any force large enough would have stopped the planet turning. Jeffreys was right. Drift was accepted in the sixties only when seafloor spreading supplied a different mechanism entirely: the continents do not plough through the ocean floor, they ride on it. So the correct conclusion was rejected for fifty years for a good reason, by people doing their jobs properly. Jeffreys never accepted plate tectonics either.
