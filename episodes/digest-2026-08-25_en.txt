One story worth more time this morning.

Operation Economic Outcast. Strip out the branding and Monday produced sixty designations and a list of sectors, considerably less than had been promised since Wednesday. Bessent was explicit about why. Countries would be given time to remedy their bad behaviour, and Washington did not want to blow up the global financial system. That is a real constraint said out loud, and it is the whole story.

The mechanism is secondary sanctions. Primary sanctions bar Americans from dealing with Iran, and those have been in place for decades and are close to exhausted. Secondary sanctions bar everyone else, by threatening any foreign bank, shipper or refiner that touches Iranian trade with the loss of access to the dollar system. They are extraordinarily powerful, and they are aimed at friends and rivals rather than at Iran, which is exactly why they are hard to fire. The country whose cooperation decides this is China, and China has now twice declined.

So the administration has a choice it keeps postponing. Sanction a large Chinese bank and you get real pressure on Iran, a fight with Beijing, and possibly the end of the trade truce weeks before Xi Jinping arrives in Washington. Don't, and Tehran learns the ceiling here is rhetoric.

Meanwhile Iran has found a revenue line inside the crisis. It has begun charging transit fees of up to two million dollars a voyage on some vessels through Hormuz, case by case rather than by rule. Under the law of the sea a coastal state may charge for services rendered, pilotage and the like, but not for passage through an international strait. The Economist calls the idea a phantom tollbooth, and notes Washington has floated collecting one too. A strait that generates rent is a strait somebody fights over.

What would make this concrete is narrow. Whether any named foreign institution gets designated, rather than a vessel or a shell company. And whether Hormuz traffic climbs out of the twenties, because the Pakistani channel is the only part of this actually about ending the war.

The second story, and it is a mechanism worth having.

South Korea had the world's best stock market for most of a year. Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix both reached trillion-dollar valuations on memory demand, and the benchmark index more than tripled. Then it fell about forty percent in six weeks across June and July, erasing something like two and a half trillion dollars. Korean retail investors took most of it.

A new paper by Yinhong Zhao at Princeton argues much of that violence was manufactured. A leveraged exchange-traded fund promises twice the daily move of a stock. To hold that ratio it must buy more when the stock rises and sell when it falls, and it does that at the close. Two things follow. The trade is predictable, because you can see the fund's size and the day's move. And it is self-enlarging, because the more you push the price up in the day, the more the fund must buy at four o'clock.

So traders buy early, the price rises, the mandated purchase grows, and they sell into it. The fund buys high, and the next day it reverses.

Scale is what makes Korea different. Single-stock leveraged funds listed there this year and went from four point three trillion won to fourteen trillion in three weeks. The daily forced rebalance in SK Hynix averaged twenty-two percent of the stock's entire traded volume, and hit fifty on the worst day. Zhao's estimate is that this took annualised volatility in that stock from about eighty-five percent to about a hundred thirty-seven over nine weeks, and moved nineteen percent of the products' terminal wealth away from their mostly retail holders.

Twenty-two percent of daily volume, in a trade nobody chose to make. At that scale the tail is not wagging the dog. The tail is the dog.

One thing to sit with this morning.

Look back at the eternity of time that passed before we were born, and see how it has been nothing to us. This is the mirror nature holds up to the time that will come after our death.

That is Lucretius, from the third book of On the Nature of Things, written in Latin hexameter in the first century before the common era. Almost nothing is known about the man. What survives is a hostile line from Saint Jerome three centuries later, claiming he was driven mad by a love potion, wrote the poem in his lucid intervals, and killed himself at forty-four. The poem nearly vanished with him, coming down through a single manuscript found in a German monastery in fourteen seventeen.

The observation I'd make is that this is a symmetry argument, and symmetry arguments are only as good as the response they get. He is right that we are untroubled by the endless stretch before our birth. He is plainly wrong that we therefore feel the same about the stretch after. Everyone reads it, agrees it is tidy, and goes on being afraid.
