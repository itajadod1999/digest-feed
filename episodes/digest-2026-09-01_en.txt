One story worth more time this morning, and it is the Japanese bond yield.

Japan's ten-year yielded three percent today, the first time since September nineteen ninety-six. Thirty years. The whole global curve moved with it: American, European and British borrowing costs all rose. Here is why a number in Tokyo moves a number in New York.

For thirty years Japan was the place where money was free. The Bank of Japan held rates at zero or below, Japanese savings institutions could earn nothing at home, and an enormous pool of capital went abroad looking for yield. Japanese life insurers and pension funds are among the largest foreign holders of American Treasuries, French government bonds, Australian debt. Japan is the world's largest net creditor nation.

That flow runs one way only while domestic yields sit near zero. When a Japanese insurer can buy a ten-year at home paying three percent, in its own currency, with no hedging cost, the case for a foreign bond at four point seven nine gets much harder, because the hedge eats most of the difference. Money that has been leaving Japan for three decades acquires a reason to come home. That is the mechanism, and it is why every large bond desk watches Tokyo.

The proximate cause is inflation and fiscal worry, and behind both, oil. The war restarted Sunday, energy prices rose, and the market's inference was that central banks will tighten rather than ease. The whole Japanese curve moved, not just the ten-year.

What would make it matter more than it already does. Whether Japanese institutions actually repatriate, which shows up with a lag in quarterly flow data rather than in daily prices. And whether the long end here keeps steepening. The thirty-year touched a nineteen-year high two weeks ago on doubts about this Federal Reserve's nerve. If Tokyo is competing for the same money, that steepening has a second driver with nothing to do with Warsh.

The second story, and it is a structure I did not expect to see.

The Venezuelan oil arrangement announced Friday now has terms. The White House says Venezuela has granted a company called North American Blue Energy Partners hundred-year drilling rights across seventeen fields holding sixty-five billion barrels of proven reserves. The American government takes a thirty-five percent stake in that company, Venezuela's second-largest private producer, is guaranteed the right to buy twenty percent of output at cost, and gets first refusal on the other eighty.

That is not a trade deal and not a sanctions settlement. It is the United States government taking equity in a foreign oil company and locking in below-market physical supply for a century.

And the entity leading it, the Times reports, is the Pentagon's Office of Strategic Capital, created under the previous administration to lend to American defence suppliers too slow-moving for venture money. Its purpose was domestic industrial capacity. It is now structuring a sovereign resource acquisition abroad, a different job entirely.

What would make it real is documentation. What exists this morning is a White House announcement and terms described by the parties. No contract has been published. Chevron is separately in talks to expand there. Announced, not filed.

One thing to sit with this morning.

Habit is thus the enormous fly-wheel of society, its most precious conservative agent. It alone is what keeps us all within the bounds of ordinance.

That is William James, in the chapter on habit in The Principles of Psychology, eighteen ninety. He worked on the book for twelve years and loathed it by the end, describing it to his publisher as a loathsome, distended, tumefied, bloated, dropsical mass. In his twenties he had spent years in a depression severe enough that he considered ending his life, and credited his recovery to reading a French philosopher on free will and deciding his first act of free will would be to believe in it.

The observation I would make is that the metaphor works against him. A flywheel smooths a machine's motion. It also makes the machine very hard to turn. James goes on in the same passage to say approvingly that habit keeps the fisherman at sea through the winter and the miner in his darkness, and that it dooms us to fight the battle of life on the lines of our nurture. He is describing a trap and recommending it as furniture.
