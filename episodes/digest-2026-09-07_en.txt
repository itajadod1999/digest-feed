To deals and credit, where the largest number of the morning is Chinese.

Beijing's finance ministry says it will inject more than three hundred sixty billion yuan into state-owned banks and insurers, about fifty-four billion dollars, raised through special sovereign bonds and spread across eight institutions. It is the country's biggest recapitalization in nearly two decades. The stated logic is that more capital gives lenders more capacity to lend, into an economy the government's own language concedes is sputtering.

Worth being precise, because the two get confused. Capital is not liquidity. Chinese banks are not short of deposits. What they are short of is room on the balance sheet to absorb losses on property loans they have already made, and a bank thin on capital does not lend more, it lends less. This is the state paying to remove that constraint. Whether the lending then happens is the separate question, and it is the one that decided how well this worked the last time China tried it, in the cleanup that began in nineteen ninety-eight.

Now the one story worth real time this morning, and it is a blockade that both sides are running against each other.

The war with Iran is in its seventh month and has moved almost entirely to sea. The weekend: American forces struck three Iranian oil tankers on Saturday, disabling two and destroying the third, after Iranian missiles were fired at two American warships. Iran said it then went after six vessels linked to America, and has warned of what it calls a painful response if there is another strike.

The pattern behind that is a policy. On Axios's reporting, the President approved what the administration calls tanker for tanker: every Iranian attack on shipping in the strait is answered by crippling an Iranian ship. It is deterrence stated as arithmetic, which is its appeal. Its problem is that it is symmetrical, and symmetrical rules escalate by construction.

Now the mechanism, because a blockade is not one thing and the two sides are running very different kinds. Iran's is kinetic and cheap. It attacked at least a dozen commercial vessels in August, and it does not need to sink them; it needs to make insurance unaffordable and crews unwilling. The American blockade is legal and financial, built out of sanctions and enforced by the fact that nearly all oil is paid for in dollars, which means through banks that answer to Washington.

Right now the American version is winning on the water and losing in the accounts. The Navy is escorting small tankers through and millions of barrels a day are still leaving the Gulf, while Iran has not shipped a cargo of crude in almost two months. But the Journal reported this weekend that the Treasury identified about nine billion dollars of Iranian funds moving through American banks in twenty twenty-four alone, disguised through foreign partners and front companies. The financial blockade leaks at the one point it was supposed to be strongest.

That is why this stays stuck. A country that cannot sell oil and cannot be starved of money has no forcing deadline in either direction. Iran's options reduce to a deal or an escalation, and firing at an American warship over the weekend is not a government choosing the deal.
