One story worth more time this morning: why Truist sold Carlyle seven point seven billion dollars of perfectly good loans.

What happened. LightStream is the online lender Truist inherited from SunTrust. It makes unsecured personal loans to borrowers with strong credit, for things like home projects and cars. Truist is selling essentially the whole book to Carlyle's asset-backed finance team, led by Akhil Bansal. These aren't troubled loans. That's the point.

The mechanism. A bank holds capital against every loan it carries, and a book of unsecured consumer loans ties up a lot of it. Sell the book, and that capital comes free for whatever the bank would rather do: buy back shares, lend to its core customers, or simply look stronger to regulators.

The buyer's side. Asset-backed finance is private credit's newest frontier. Instead of lending to a company, the fund buys or finances pools of assets that throw off cash: consumer loans, car loans, equipment leases, music royalties. Prime borrowers, fixed rates and loans that pay themselves down each month suit buyers who need steady, predictable cash, especially insurance companies, which have become the deepest pool of money behind the big private credit firms. Carlyle and Truist have done this before: four hundred and fifteen million dollars of student loans in twenty twenty-four. This is eighteen times bigger.

What it means in context. It's the same migration we've watched in commercial property and corporate lending, now reaching the household. Loans that used to sit on regulated bank balance sheets, marked at cost, move to private funds, marked by their managers.

What would make it matter. Two things. Whether other regional banks follow with their own consumer books, which would make this a trend rather than a trade. And how these pools behave if unemployment rises. Prime borrowers default rarely in good times. Nobody has watched a large private-credit consumer book through a real recession yet.

One idea before we finish.

In November nineteen seventy-seven, The Economist ran a piece about the Netherlands and gave a name to something odd. In nineteen fifty-nine, the Dutch had found one of the largest natural gas fields in the world, at Groningen. By the seventies, gas exports were pouring in, and the country was doing worse: the guilder strengthened, factories lost out abroad, and unemployment climbed. The magazine called it the Dutch disease. In nineteen eighty-two, two economists, W. Max Corden, an Australian then at Oxford, and J. Peter Neary, an Irishman, gave it a model. A booming export sector pulls workers and capital toward itself and pushes up the currency, and the other traded industries, manufacturing above all, get squeezed from both sides.

The circumstance matters, because the Netherlands was no oil state. It was a rich, diversified, well-run economy, and it still caught it. That's what made the idea travel, from Norway to Nigeria to Australia's mining boom.

The complication is that nobody agrees how much of it is a disease. Some Dutch economists argued the real problem in the seventies was what the government did with the money, spending gas revenue on welfare and wages the economy couldn't sustain afterward. Corden himself was careful to say that a shrinking factory sector may just be an economy adjusting to getting richer. Norway, which banked its oil money in a sovereign fund, suggests the outcome is a choice. Whether it's a law of resource economies or a policy mistake with a catchy name is still argued over.
