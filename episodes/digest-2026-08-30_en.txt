One story worth real time this morning, and it is a structure rather than an event.

The Journal reported this weekend that wealthy Americans are pouring billions into something called private-placement life insurance. Miriam Gottfried's framing is the useful one: a Roth individual retirement account with no contribution limit. Here is the machinery underneath that.

Start with ordinary life insurance. You pay a premium, the insurer holds and invests it, a cash value builds inside the contract, and when you die a death benefit goes to your beneficiaries free of income tax. Crucially, the money compounding inside that contract is not taxed year by year. That treatment is old and deliberate: society decided widows should not be taxed on the proceeds.

Private-placement life insurance takes that wrapper and changes what is inside it. Instead of the insurer's conservative portfolio, the cash value goes into whatever the buyer and the carrier agree on: hedge funds, private credit, private real estate, the whole alternative shelf.

Now think about why those particular assets. A hedge fund trading actively throws off short-term gains, taxed at ordinary income rates. A private credit fund throws off interest, also ordinary income. Both generate tax forms every spring and a bill whether or not you took any cash out. These are the most tax-inefficient assets a rich person can own. Put them inside the insurance wrapper and the annual bill goes away. Nothing is realised, nothing is reported, it compounds gross, and if the policy is held until death it passes to heirs without ever being taxed at all.

That is the entire product. It is not an investment strategy. It is a container.

The numbers come from the Senate. Ron Wyden ran an eighteen-month investigation through the Finance Committee and reported in early twenty twenty-four that the domestic market held at least forty billion dollars across the seven largest carriers, owned by roughly three thousand Americans. The average death benefit was almost thirteen million dollars. The committee also obtained the marketing decks, which pitched the product explicitly as a tax-free way to hold private equity and hedge funds and as a route around income, gift and estate tax. Minimum premiums run one to two million dollars and usually a good deal more, which is why the count is three thousand people rather than three million.

Now the catch, which is the reason this has stayed a niche rather than a national scandal.

For the contract to be insurance in the eyes of the Internal Revenue Service, and not a brokerage account wearing a costume, the policyholder cannot control the investments. This is the investor-control doctrine, and it is not a statute. It is decades of rulings pointing the same direction: you may choose the manager or the strategy when you sign, and after that you may not pick up the phone and tell anyone to sell Nvidia. You cannot vote the positions or direct the trades. Separate rules cap how much of the account any single holding can be.

Cross the line and the consequence is not a fine. The Service simply treats you as the owner of the underlying assets and taxes you on everything the account has earned, going back. The wrapper does not fail gently.

There is a second requirement people forget. To be life insurance at all, under section seventy-seven oh two of the code, the contract has to carry real mortality risk, meaning a death benefit meaningfully larger than the cash value. So you are obliged to buy insurance you may not want in order to get the tax treatment you do. That is the toll, and the whole calculation is whether the toll is smaller than the tax.

So what would have to happen for any of this to change.

Legislation is the obvious answer and probably the wrong one. Wyden introduced the Protecting Proper Life Insurance from Abuse Act on the thirteenth of April this year, which would reclassify policies meeting certain tests as private placement contracts and strip the insurance treatment. It is a serious bill, drafted by people who have read the marketing decks, and it is going nowhere on its own: Wyden is the ranking member, not the chairman, and a provision like this moves only when it gets attached to a larger tax vehicle as a revenue offset. Watch the next reconciliation bill, not the calendar.

The nearer risk is enforcement. Investor control is a doctrine rather than a bright line, which means it gets decided one audit at a time, quietly, for years before anyone outside sees a case.

And the reason the balance is shifting is not about insurance at all. It is about where yield now lives. As more of the world's lending has moved out of banks and public bonds and into private credit funds that generate ordinary income, the pile of assets suffering the worst possible tax treatment has grown enormously, and a wrapper that defers that tax gets more valuable automatically. The forty billion number is two years old. Nobody has published a newer one, and the Journal's reporting on the current growth is single-sourced.

The thing I would actually watch is discipline at the carriers. The product works because buyers genuinely give up control. If it gets sold widely enough to people who are used to managing their own money, some of them will keep managing it, and the doctrine will get tested by someone who did not mean to test it.

Now a minute of French, and then the European news in it. New week, fresh phrase, and this one is about register rather than vocabulary.

How would you say something is a hassle? Not difficult in any technical sense. A pain. A slog. The thing you complain about by text.

Here it is.

C'est galère.

The obvious version fails because it is flat rather than wrong. The textbook reaches for a word meaning difficult or complicated, and both are correct and both land like a form letter. The noun underneath this one is a galley, the ship convicts were sentenced to row, and French kept it for anything that feels like that.

It bends in every direction.

Quelle galère. On a galéré pour trouver un taxi.

And note there is no article in the plain form. Two words, nothing between them. Adding one is the tell.

One thing to sit with this morning.

The fish-trap is employed to catch fish; but when the fish are got, the men forget the trap. Words are employed to convey ideas; but when the ideas are apprehended, men forget the words. Fain would I talk with a man who has forgot the words.

That is Zhuangzi, in James Legge's translation, from a chapter probably assembled a century or more after the man himself was writing, in the fourth century before the common era. It was the Warring States period, when philosophers were mostly employed advising rulers how to win. Zhuangzi declined that work. The story he left about himself is that when the king of Chu sent officials to make him prime minister, he asked whether they had heard of the sacred tortoise, dead three thousand years and kept wrapped in a box in the ancestral temple, and whether it would rather be honoured in a box or alive dragging its tail in the mud.

The observation I would make is that the last line is a joke at the expense of the first two. He wants to find a man who has forgotten words so he can have a conversation with him, which will require words. And I think the analogy is doing less work than it appears. A trap and a fish are two different objects. With language they are not separable in the same way. Very often the phrasing is the idea, and what you have when you discard the words is a different, smaller thing that you have told yourself is the same one.
