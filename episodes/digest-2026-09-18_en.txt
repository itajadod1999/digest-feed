One story worth more time this morning, and it concerns an offering whose book closes Monday.

Following up on Wednesday. The National Stock Exchange of India is going public, having placed about seven hundred and three million dollars with anchor investors at a valuation near forty-six billion. If it prices there it is the third-largest listing in Indian history. Now read that beside the Bloomberg Markets feature this week on how India built, and then broke, the largest options casino in the world.

The mechanism was calendar design. The exchanges scheduled their index options to expire on different days, so a trader had a fresh bet available every day of the week. Entry cost roughly the price of a cup of chai. A small move the right way doubled your money in minutes; the wrong way took all of it. In dairy towns like Gangapur, people traded from doorsteps and under trees, three generations crowded around one phone.

Here is what it produced. India's markets regulator found that nine out of ten individual traders in futures and options lost money, at an average of about a hundred and twenty-eight thousand rupees a year. In the twenty twenty-three fiscal year alone, retail traders lost more than five hundred billion rupees, most of it to professional firms on the other side of the trades.

So the regulator closed it. In late twenty twenty-four it roughly tripled the minimum contract size, from five million rupees to fifteen million, and limited each exchange to one index with weekly expiries. Within weeks the most hyperactive derivatives market on the planet had deflated.

Which is the thing to hold in mind about Monday. Transaction charges on those contracts were the engine of the exchange's earnings, and the state throttled that engine two years ago. So watch where the book prices in the range, and what the exchange says about how much revenue still comes from derivatives. One trader quoted in the piece, asked what he missed, said it was not the money. It was the sense of possibility. It was, he said, a beautiful madness while it lasted.

One idea before we finish.

In eighteen forty-three a Louisiana engineer named Norbert Rillieux patented a way of boiling sugar. Cane juice had to be evaporated down to crystals, and the method then in use, called the Jamaica train, meant enslaved workers ladling boiling syrup by hand between a row of open kettles over fires. Rillieux's machine put the pans in sequence under reduced pressure and used the vapour coming off the first to heat the second. The same heat, used twice, burning a fraction of the fuel and making better sugar.

The circumstance is that Rillieux was a free man of color, born in New Orleans in eighteen oh six and educated in Paris, where he taught applied mechanics. He came home with a piece of thermodynamics nobody in the sugar business had thought to apply, and installed his first working version in eighteen thirty-four, nine years before the patent.

The complication is what the invention actually did. It eliminated the most dangerous job on the plantation and it freed nobody. Cheaper, better sugar made the plantations more profitable, and more profitable plantations bought more people. A labour-saving device inside a slave economy does not reduce the demand for labour. It raises the return on it. Rillieux was eventually pushed out of the industry he had transformed, went back to Paris, and spent much of the rest of his life on hieroglyphics.
