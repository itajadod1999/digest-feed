One story worth more time this morning: the long end of the bond market.

What happened is the close I gave you up top, the highest ten-year yield since two thousand and seven. DealBook adds that Treasuries are on pace for their worst month since twenty twenty-four. The Economist puts the average new thirty-year mortgage at nearly seven percent, almost double what it was before the pandemic.

The mechanism. A long-term yield is roughly two things added together. One is where investors expect short-term rates to be over the life of the bond. The other is the term premium, the extra return they demand for tying up money for ten or thirty years and carrying the risk that inflation or supply surprises them.

Both pieces are rising. The Fed raised rates a quarter point on September sixteenth and signalled another; DealBook says traders put the odds of one at the next meeting around seventy percent. Meanwhile inflation has been above target for five years, the war is squeezing fuel, and the government keeps borrowing heavily.

One idea before we finish.

In the nineteen seventies, Arthur Burns, the Federal Reserve chairman, faced inflation he didn't want to fight with higher rates. So, as Stephen Roach, then a young economist on the Fed's staff, has told the story, Burns kept finding reasons to set prices aside. Food went, because of bad harvests. Energy went, because of OPEC. Then other items followed, each blamed on some special factor. By Roach's account, by the time Burns was done, roughly two thirds of the consumer price index had been excluded. What was left was the first version of what we now call core inflation.

The circumstance matters. Burns was under heavy pressure from President Nixon to keep money loose before the nineteen seventy-two election, and the idea that most inflation was temporary and someone else's fault was convenient. Inflation went on to reach double digits, and it took Paul Volcker and a brutal recession to end it.

The complication is that the idea outlived its inventor. Stripping out food and energy became standard, and there's a respectable case for it: volatile prices say little about next year, and some research finds core measures forecast headline inflation better than headline does. The Dallas Fed's alternative, a trimmed mean, drops the most extreme price moves each month rather than the same categories every time. Whether core is a better signal or a better excuse depends on who's doing the trimming, and that argument isn't settled.
