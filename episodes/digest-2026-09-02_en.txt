The textbook hands you an adjective meaning I am lazy, which is a verdict on your character rather than a report on your evening. French makes it a possession instead. La flemme is a noun, something you have tonight and might not have tomorrow, and it takes de plus an infinitive.

One story worth more time this morning, and it is about who actually controls a German company.

Volkswagen's chief executive, Oliver Blume, took a cost-cutting plan to his supervisory board that would double planned job losses to a hundred thousand, and named four German plants, Emden, Hanover, Zwickau and Neckarsulm, as sites he could not confirm viable uses for in the twenty thirties. The board rejected it, twelve to nineteen. He is now weighing whether to go around it and put the plan straight to shareholders, on the Journal's reporting, which calls that a nuclear option.

Here is why he can't simply do what he wants. German law calls it codetermination. At a company Volkswagen's size, half the seats on the supervisory board, the body that hires and fires management and approves restructuring, belong to worker representatives. Not advisers. Voting directors. Normally the chairman breaks ties for the shareholders, so the arrangement is balanced rather than worker-controlled. At Volkswagen it currently is not: labour holds an outright majority after a shareholder director resigned this year.

Then the second lock. The state of Lower Saxony owns a stake large enough to block key decisions, has held it for sixty years, and contains most of the plants in question. So a plan to close German factories must pass a board where the workers have the votes and the state has a veto. That is not a negotiation Blume wins on the merits. The number driving him is a twenty percent cost disadvantage against rivals, mostly Chinese. The board meets again Friday.

What would make this matter beyond Wolfsburg is precedent. Codetermination is the load-bearing element of the German industrial settlement, the thing that bought seventy years of labour peace. If the country's biggest industrial employer decides it cannot restructure through that structure and goes over its head, every other German board is watching. And if he tries and fails, he probably does not keep his job.

The second story is shorter, and it is the climate wording, because the wording is the policy.

The United Nations Environment Programme said one and a half degrees is gone. Not at risk. Missed. What it proposed instead is two-part: hold the overshoot as low as possible, then return below the threshold before the century ends.

That second half is doing enormous work. Coming back down does not happen by emitting less; emitting less only slows the climb. Returning below a threshold you have crossed requires net negative emissions, pulling carbon out of the air faster than the world puts it in. Those technologies exist at demonstration scale and cost hundreds of dollars a tonne, against tens of billions of tonnes emitted a year. So the new target converts a problem of restraint into one of construction, and moves the deadline past everyone now negotiating. What would make it real is a price: nothing gets built at scale until somebody is obliged to pay for removal rather than choosing to, and no government has written that into law.

One thing to sit with this morning.

If we had a keen vision and feeling of all ordinary human life, it would be like hearing the grass grow and the squirrel's heart beat, and we should die of that roar which lies on the other side of silence. As it is, the best of us walk about well wadded with stupidity.

That is George Eliot, in Middlemarch, published in eight instalments across eighteen seventy-one and seventy-two. Mary Ann Evans began as a translator of German biblical criticism, lived unmarried with George Henry Lewes for two decades and was cut socially for it, and wrote most of the book after Lewes's son died of spinal tuberculosis in her house, slowly and in pain, which stopped the work for months.

The observation I'd make is that the metaphor concedes more than she means it to. She describes insensibility as a physical necessity, padding that keeps the roar out, and then spends eight hundred pages removing exactly that padding from her reader. Which suggests either that she doesn't believe the claim, or that the roar is survivable in measured doses and the question is quantity rather than kind. And the phrase does two jobs. She intends it as anatomy; it reads as an insult.
