One story worth more time this morning. American gross federal debt passed forty trillion dollars, and within hours the Treasury Secretary did something that tells you more than the milestone does.

The number hit forty point zero four seven trillion on Tuesday, disclosed Wednesday. A decade ago it was nineteen point four trillion. The government expects to borrow more than two trillion this year alone, and the drivers are not mysterious: decades of higher spending on Medicare and social security, tax cuts, pandemic stimulus, and now the Iran war and tariff refunds.

Forty trillion is a round number, and economists will tell you it is symbolic. That is true. What is not symbolic is what it costs to borrow. On Tuesday the thirty-year Treasury yield touched five point three four percent, the highest in nineteen years. Then the Treasury announced it will at least double the maximum size of its buyback operations, from two billion dollars to at least four billion, starting the ninth of September, targeting the ten-to-twenty and twenty-to-thirty year parts of the curve. The thirty-year fell nearly nine basis points, to just under five point two.

Here is the mechanism, because a buyback is not what it sounds like. The Treasury is not retiring debt. It is buying back older, less liquid long bonds and funding that by issuing something else, usually shorter paper. The total does not shrink. What changes is the mix, and specifically how much long-dated supply the market has to absorb. Less long supply, lower long yields. That is the trade.

So this is a demand-side patch on a supply-side problem. The deficit is exactly what it was on Tuesday. And leaning shorter means refinancing more of the debt more often, which is fine while rates behave and expensive when they don't.

The framing worth holding is the Journal's. Scott Bessent is now acting as the country's bond trader in chief, managing the curve as a position. Treasury secretaries have always chosen the maturity mix. Doing it visibly and reactively, the day after a nineteen-year high, is a different posture, and the market will expect it every time yields spike.

What would make this concrete is the next auction, and specifically the ratio of bids to paper offered at the long end. The buyback moved the price. Whether it moved the buyers is a different question.

One thing to sit with this morning.

But the iniquity of oblivion blindly scattereth her poppy, and deals with the memory of men without distinction to merit of perpetuity.

That is Sir Thomas Browne, from Hydriotaphia, or Urn Burial, published in sixteen fifty-eight. Browne was a physician in Norwich, and the occasion was entirely literal: forty or fifty burial urns had been dug up in a field at Old Walsingham, in Norfolk, and he wrote a book about them. He believed they were Roman. They were Anglo-Saxon, by several centuries.

The observation I would make is that blindly is the load-bearing word, and it turns the sentence into nearly the opposite of the lament it sounds like. If forgetting is genuinely random, obscurity is not a verdict on anybody. And the essay was occasioned by an identification Browne got wrong, which is a fairly direct demonstration that the record does not only lose things. It also keeps the wrong ones.
