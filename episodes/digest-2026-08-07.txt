One story worth more time this morning.

A one point one billion dollar commercial mortgage-backed securities loan on a Hollywood portfolio owned jointly by Hudson Pacific Properties and Blackstone has moved to special servicing with its maturity days away.

Here's the structure. The collateral is three studio lots, Sunset Gower, Sunset Las Palmas and Sunset Bronson, plus office buildings including sixty forty Sunset, the Harlow, and three Netflix-leased properties called Icon, Cue and Epic. Barclays, Bank of America, Wells Fargo and Société Générale co-originated the loan back in 2021 as a two-year, floating-rate, interest-only mortgage with extension options, and those extensions carried it to now. Hudson Pacific is on the hook for roughly half, about five hundred sixty-six million dollars. Blackstone holds the rest.

Special servicing is the part worth understanding. In a securitization, the master servicer collects payments and hands them to the trust. When a loan defaults, or when default becomes imminent, the loan transfers to a special servicer, who is the only party with authority to modify terms, grant an extension, or move to foreclose. A transfer ahead of maturity is not the same as a default. It is usually the borrower and lender formalizing that the loan will not pay off at par on the maturity date, so that a negotiation can legally begin.

That's what happened here. Hudson Pacific's chief financial officer, Harout Diramerian, told investors on Wednesday's call that the borrower and the special servicer have agreed on terms for a longer-term extension, plus a thirty-day extension to finish the documentation. That thirty-day window is the real deadline now.

The tenant situation is why an extension is the likely outcome rather than a foreclosure. Netflix leases about seventy-five percent of the office space and about sixty-nine percent of the studio space in the portfolio. A trust holding paper on buildings with a credit tenant of that quality generally does better modifying than taking the keys and trying to sell soundstages into a thin market. Hudson Pacific, for its part, reported a one hundred five million dollar loss for the quarter, so its capacity to write a large check to reduce the balance is limited. The likely shape of a deal is a term extension with some combination of a paydown, a new interest rate cap, and cash management.

Put this next to the origination data from earlier. Office lending was up forty-seven percent last quarter and commercial mortgage-backed issuance up sixty-eight percent year over year. Lenders are clearly back. And a one point one billion dollar loan on trophy Hollywood assets with Netflix in most of the space still could not be taken out at par. That gap, between a functioning lending market and specific 2021-vintage floating-rate paper, is where the next year of workouts lives.

And a second story briefly.

SoftBank borrowed ten billion dollars from a group of banks using its stake in OpenAI as collateral. The cash funds another ten billion dollar purchase of OpenAI shares by October, the last piece of a thirty billion dollar investment announced earlier this year.

A margin loan against publicly traded stock is routine. You pledge shares, you borrow against them, and if the price falls through a trigger, you post more cash or the lender seizes the collateral. What makes this one unusual is that OpenAI is private, loss-making, and has no continuous market price to mark against. There is no closing print to test the trigger against each night. SoftBank has said the deal does require it to inject additional cash if the valuation falls, which means the parties have agreed on some other mechanism for establishing that value.

Matt Levine's framing at Bloomberg is the useful one. A large share of the data center financing announced over the past year is, underneath, credit extended against OpenAI's and Anthropic's future payments, with more creditworthy public companies standing in between to make the paper investment grade. So the biggest thing in finance right now, loosely speaking, is lending against artificial intelligence equity. Borrowing against OpenAI to buy more OpenAI is just the most literal version of it.

The thing to watch is a mark. A down round, or a large secondary trade printing below the trigger level, is what would turn this from a financing story into a margin call story.
