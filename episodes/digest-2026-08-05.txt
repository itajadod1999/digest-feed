And the Middle East.

The Strait of Hormuz has been the central fact in oil and rates since February. There is now a draft agreement in circulation, and reporting on it is worth going through carefully, because the accounts differ.

Bessent said a deal could come today or tomorrow. Secretary of State Marco Rubio said very shortly. Qatar, acting as a mediator, confirmed that draft language was being circulated among the parties. Iran's foreign minister, Abbas Araghchi, said separate talks with Oman were, in his phrase, on the way to being finalized. According to a Pakistani source cited by The National, Pakistan has been passing messages between Washington and Tehran.

Where accounts diverge is on whether the United States and Iran are talking directly. Washington describes an imminent bilateral outcome. Tehran denies direct negotiations, and an outlet affiliated with the Revolutionary Guard said the agreement will be delayed as long as American threats of military attack continue. Iranian officials have also said publicly that the waterway will not return to its pre-war status.

The reported terms of the draft, and these are reported, not confirmed: inbound vessels would use northern Iranian waters, outbound traffic would use southern Omani waters, the arrangement would run sixty days and could be extended, no tolls during that period, and the parties would work to clear naval mines from the median lane within thirty days. A previous understanding reached on June seventeenth collapsed after attacks on tankers in and around the strait.

One story worth more time this morning.

Commercial mortgage-backed securities distress just posted its sharpest move of the year, and the shape of it matters more than the headline number.

CRED iQ put the overall distress rate at ten point nine one percent in July. That figure captures loans that are either in special servicing or thirty days or more delinquent. Underneath it, the delinquency rate rose twenty-four basis points to eight point six eight percent, but the special servicing rate rose forty-two basis points to ten point three eight percent. Trepp, tracking a slightly different universe, had the volume of loans thirty days or more past due jump eight percent in a single month, to forty-seven and a half billion dollars.

Here is the part to sit with. Special servicing is running well above delinquency. That gap means loans are being transferred to workout before borrowers actually stop paying. A loan goes to special servicing on imminent default, on a maturity the borrower cannot refinance, on a request for modification. It is the servicer and the borrower getting ahead of the problem rather than waiting for a missed payment. So the July move is less a wave of defaults than a wave of people picking up the phone.

By property type: office at sixteen point six five percent distressed, mixed-use at thirteen, multifamily at eleven point two one, industrial at two point three five, and self-storage at basically nothing. Office being worst is not news. Multifamily above the all-property average is the number that should hold your attention, and the mechanism there is refinancing, not occupancy. Roughly one hundred sixty-two billion dollars of multifamily loans mature this year, up more than half from last year. A great many of those were underwritten in twenty twenty and twenty twenty-one at floating rates against rent growth that did not materialize, and they are maturing into a ten-year Treasury near four point seven and a Federal Reserve that may raise rather than cut.

And then, in the same market, the top of the stack is functioning perfectly well. Brookfield and the Qatar Investment Authority financed two Manhattan West with a one point nine billion dollar securitization in May, on a building ninety-six percent leased at average gross rents around one hundred thirty-two dollars a foot. Tishman Speyer refinanced The Spiral for two point eight five billion at the start of the year with a bank group led by JPMorgan.

That is the real story in the numbers. It is not one market getting worse. It is two markets, and the distance between them is widening.

The second story worth more time is a financing structure.

Blackstone has been sounding out investors on a debt package of at least thirty-six billion dollars to fund Anthropic's use of Google's custom artificial intelligence chips. Bloomberg reported the details Tuesday. The proceeds buy Google's tensor processing units, which Anthropic then leases.

The proposed structure is tranched like a securitization: roughly six billion dollars of A-one notes, twenty-five billion of A-two notes, and four and a half billion of B notes. Broadcom, which helps Google build the chips, is backstopping payments on the largest portions.

Consider what that backstop is doing. Lenders are being asked to fund hardware for a company that is not yet profitable, in a technology whose useful life nobody can price with confidence. The credit is not really Anthropic's. It is a manufacturer's payment guarantee wrapped around a lease on equipment. That is the same logic as an aircraft or equipment lease financing, with one difference: an aircraft has thirty years of residual value data behind it, and these chips have none.

If it prices near thirty-six billion, it would be among the largest private credit deals ever assembled. And it would be the second one in about two months. Apollo and Blackstone put together roughly thirty-five billion for an Anthropic chip lease from Google in late May. Anthropic has since filed confidentially for a listing in the United States. Separately, Amazon has now surpassed three trillion dollars in market value, and the four largest cloud providers are spending more than seven hundred billion dollars on infrastructure this year.

What would make this matter beyond technology: seventy billion dollars in two months, from private lenders, against depreciating hardware. If the collateral turns out to be shorter-lived than modeled, the losses do not land at a bank with a regulator. They land in private credit funds. Watch where these notes end up.
