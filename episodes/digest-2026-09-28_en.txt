One story worth more time this morning: Prologis and Segro.

What's happening today. Shareholders of Segro, one of Britain's biggest warehouse landlords and the owner of Europe's largest data centre hub, in Slough, vote on an all-share takeover by Prologis, the American logistics giant. Each Segro share becomes about nine hundredths of a Prologis share, with a cash option capped at three and a half billion pounds across all holders. The Economist values the deal at about fourteen billion pounds, roughly eighteen and a half billion dollars, which would make it one of the largest foreign takeovers of a listed British company.

How it got here is the interesting part. Prologis first offered about twelve point six billion pounds in June. Segro's board called that and two later offers opportunistic. What changed its mind was its own shareholders. Norway's sovereign wealth fund said the bid merited consideration, the investment manager C C L A pushed too, and after a fourth proposal, billed as best and final at about ten pounds thirty-two a share, the board recommended it.

The mechanism is a British one, a scheme of arrangement. Instead of chasing each holder to tender, the deal goes to a court-supervised meeting. It passes if a majority of the holders voting, representing at least seventy-five percent of the shares voted, say yes. Then a judge sanctions it, and it binds everyone, including the holders who voted no. That's why the deal can be won or lost in a single morning.

What's Prologis actually buying? A portfolio of roughly nineteen billion pounds, with tenants like Amazon and Royal Mail, and, more to the point, data centre land in Slough. Segro started life in nineteen twenty as the Slough Trading Company; a century later, that's its most valuable address.

What would make this matter beyond one deal? First, the vote itself, and then the court and the regulators; Prologis expects completion in the first half of next year. Second, whether it becomes a template. London-listed property companies have spent years valued below what their buildings are worth, and a bidder paying in its own shares doesn't have to borrow at today's rates to close the gap.

One idea before we finish.

Between nineteen twenty-four and nineteen thirty-two, at Western Electric's Hawthorne Works outside Chicago, researchers ran a series of experiments on factory workers that produced one of the most famous findings in social science. Output seemed to rise almost whatever they changed: brighter light, dimmer light, longer breaks, shorter ones. The explanation that stuck, popularised by Elton Mayo of Harvard, was that workers responded to the attention itself. In nineteen fifty-eight a sociologist, Henry Landsberger, named it the Hawthorne effect.

The circumstance is less flattering than the legend. The lighting tests were backed in part by the electrical industry, which hoped to prove that better lighting paid for itself. They didn't show that. The later tests put five women assembling telephone relays in a separate room, with an observer, fewer supervisors and a new pay scheme, all changing at once.

The complication is that nobody agrees the effect was there. In nineteen seventy-eight Richard Franke and James Kaul re-ran the relay room numbers and argued most of the gain came from ordinary things: two of the slowest workers were replaced partway through, discipline tightened, and the Depression made every job precious. In twenty eleven Steven Levitt and John List recovered the lighting data, long thought lost, and found little sign of the dramatic pattern in the textbooks. Yet a twenty fourteen review found real evidence that being studied can change behaviour. Whether it ever happened at Hawthorne itself is still argued over.
