American markets reopen this morning after Labor Day, and they come back to an oil price that moved while they were closed.

Overnight the Houthis struck four cities in southern Saudi Arabia and set fire to energy facilities there, and Brent crude went to just under a hundred dollars a barrel. Futures on all three American indexes point modestly lower. Asia was worse. The Topix fell about one point eight percent, and the yen strengthened past a hundred fifty-three to the dollar, its best level in months, on expectations the Bank of Japan raises rates next week. That travels further than it looks, because a stronger yen makes the carry trade less attractive, and unwinding a carry trade means selling whatever the cheap yen was buying.

The week's real event is still ahead. The producer price index Thursday, the consumer price index Friday, the last inflation readings the committee gets before it meets on the sixteenth. The European Central Bank also decides Thursday, and is expected to take its rate to two and a half percent.

Now two stories worth more time, and the first is a trade war that turned personal.

Canada's retaliatory tariffs took effect just after midnight, covering roughly seven hundred American products. Steel, dairy, home appliances, clothing. About twenty billion dollars of imports a year, sized to mirror what Washington imposed on Canada, with rates as high as fifty percent. Talks collapsed on the twenty-first of August, when Mark Carney walked out and said his country was at war. Seventy-five percent of Canadians polled backed him for it.

The American response has been to point at the scoreboard. Scott Bessent, the treasury secretary, said you cannot be in a tit-for-tat with someone thirteen times your size, and elsewhere called Canada a little yippy dog antagonising a German Shepherd. The President said Bombardier will not sell aircraft in the United States unless it builds them there, and that threat has weight: roughly half the five thousand one hundred aircraft flown by Bombardier's customers are based in America.

Here is the mechanism, because a retaliatory tariff is not really an economic weapon. Canada cannot out-tax the United States and knows it. What a retaliation list does is choose whose voters feel it: you pick products from states whose senators are up for election, and you accept that your own consumers pay part of the cost. The Economist's read is that Canada's list is calibrated for precisely that. Political pressure, priced in tariff schedules.

The numbers underneath suggest Carney is playing a longer game. Canadian exports to the rest of the world hit a record twenty-five point six billion Canadian dollars in July, up fifty-six percent since January of twenty twenty-five, while exports to the United States fell eleven point six percent to fifty point five billion. That second number is still twice the first, which tells you how far this has to run before diversification is a strategy rather than an aspiration. What would make it matter beyond a bad week is if it holds, and supply chains that reroute do not reroute back on a handshake.

The second story is a small building in SoHo, and a lesson in how distressed offices change hands now.

428 Broadway is a six-story office building known as the Suspenders Building. It got into trouble the ordinary way: WeWork was its anchor across four floors, WeWork filed for bankruptcy in twenty twenty-three, and the sixty million dollar loan went into default.

What happened next is the part to follow. Late last year an affiliate tied to the Chetrit Organization, the family that owned the building, bought that defaulted loan from the lender, LoanCore, for forty million. The borrower's side now held its own debt at a twenty million dollar discount to face.

Then it foreclosed. Judicially, through a court, which is slower than the alternative and does something the alternative does not: it wipes out the junior claims sitting behind the first mortgage. Mezzanine lenders, mechanics' liens, unpaid contractors, anyone in line after the senior loan. Title passed on the eighteenth of August, the foreclosure was recorded on the twenty-eighth, and days later the building sold to Sagehall for forty-seven million. Forty to buy the debt, forty-seven to sell the building, everything junior extinguished on the way through, and the buyer gets clean title, which is what a lender will finance.

None of that is exotic or improper. Buying your own defaulted loan at a discount has a name, loan-to-own, and courts see it constantly. What makes it worth noticing is the volume, because it is being run repeatedly across Manhattan offices whose anchor tenants disappeared. Watch whether exit prices keep landing near the discounted debt rather than the old valuation, because that is the market telling you which number was real.

One idea before we finish.

In nineteen eighty, the Soviet Union explained an anthrax outbreak in the city of Sverdlovsk as contaminated meat. American intelligence said it was an accidental release from a military laboratory. Matthew Meselson, the Harvard biologist, examined the Soviet account and concluded it was plausible. He said so publicly, and because he was the most credible arms-control scientist in America, his saying so mattered.

The circumstance is the interesting part. Meselson had spent his career arguing that biological weapons were militarily useless and morally indefensible, and had persuaded the Nixon administration of it, which is a large part of why the United States gave up its own program. He had also watched intelligence agencies inflate Soviet threats before. So when handed an explanation that hung together epidemiologically, his instinct was the one that had served him well. Distrust the alarm.

In nineteen ninety-two, Yeltsin's government let him in. He took a small team to Yekaterinburg and did the fieldwork himself, tracking down a pathologist's preserved tissue and notes. Nearly every victim had been in a narrow strip of ground running downwind of the military facility. Animals had died further along the same line, and the wind records matched. It was airborne. He published in Science in nineteen ninety-four, and the finding was that he had been wrong.

The complication is that this gets told as a story about intellectual honesty, and it is one, but the twelve years in between were not free. His credibility was doing work for the Soviet account the entire time. Two things that don't reconcile: a scientist who followed the evidence to a conclusion against himself, and a decade in which the best available skeptic was the cover story's most useful asset.
