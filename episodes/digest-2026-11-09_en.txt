One story worth more time this morning: what a four hundred million dollar price on Sixty Wall Street actually says.

Start with the arithmetic. The building is about one point six million square feet. Four hundred million is about two hundred and fifty dollars a foot. In twenty seventeen, Singapore's fund paid one point oh four billion for ninety-five percent, which grosses up to roughly one point one billion for the whole thing, or close to six hundred and eighty-five dollars a foot. So the same tower is changing hands at a little over a third of what it fetched eight years ago.

The mechanism is simple. An older tower downtown is valued on the income it can produce, and its tenants have had five years to shrink or move to newer buildings. A buyer at two fifty a foot is either betting the space can be refilled at that basis, or planning something else, a conversion or a repositioning. Commercial Observer's report doesn't say which.

Who the players are matters too. The seller is a sovereign fund with no lender forcing its hand, and it is choosing to realize a loss of roughly two thirds. That's not a foreclosure. It's patient capital deciding to stop waiting.

Now put it beside two other numbers: Starwood's loan-loss provision, and Trepp's figure for commercial mortgage-backed securities in special servicing, eleven point four percent as of August, the highest since early twenty thirteen. Those are three separate views of the same thing: older office values marked down to where trades actually clear.

And the counterweight is real. Starwood also wrote its biggest quarter of new lending in four years. Lenders aren't leaving. They're lending against today's prices.

What would make this one matter more is the close. If it closes at four hundred, it becomes a comparable sale every appraiser in the Financial District has to reckon with.

One idea before we finish.

In two thousand two, a Danish planning scholar named Bent Flyvbjerg, then at Aalborg University, published a paper with two colleagues in the Journal of the American Planning Association. They had gathered cost data on two hundred and fifty-eight transport projects in twenty countries, spanning seventy years. Nine in ten came in over budget. Rail projects ran over by an average of forty-five percent, bridges and tunnels by thirty-four, roads by twenty. And the error hadn't shrunk in seventy years, despite better data and better models.

The circumstance gives the paper its edge. Flyvbjerg had spent years studying how a city-centre plan in Aalborg was bent by the people with power over it. If forecasters were simply making honest mistakes, he reasoned, the errors should be scattered, some too high, some too low, and they should improve as methods did. They weren't scattered, and they didn't improve. So the paper's title asked the question outright: error or lie. Their answer was that budgets were being underestimated on purpose, to get projects approved, a practice they called strategic misrepresentation.

The complication is that the question never closed. Critics, among them the engineers Peter Love and Dominic Ahiaga-Dagbui, argued that the result depends heavily on which estimate you treat as the baseline. Measure from the earliest rough figure and nearly everything overruns. Measure from the budget at contract signing and the overruns shrink a great deal. Flyvbjerg has defended the method ever since. Whether planners lie, or early estimates are just a different kind of number, still depends on whom you ask.
