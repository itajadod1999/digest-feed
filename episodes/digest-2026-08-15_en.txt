Over to technology, and the strangest result of the week.

An unreleased Anthropic model improved a bound tied to the Riemann hypothesis, the most famous open problem in mathematics. Not a solution. The company says plainly it does not expect this approach to produce one. What it did was raise the proven share of the relevant zeros sitting where the hypothesis predicts, from forty-one point six percent to sixty-seven point two.

For scale, human number theorists moved that constant about one and seven tenths of a percentage point in the thirty-one years between nineteen eighty-nine and twenty twenty. This added twenty-five and a half points in roughly a day and a half, across about sixty subagents and thirty-one million output tokens. Two outside experts reviewed the paper. The Journal reports it started when an employee out for a jog told the model to have a go.

One story worth more time this morning. PayPal is in talks to sell itself, and the buyer is the company that spent a decade taking its business.

The Journal reports PayPal is negotiating with a group made up of Stripe and the private equity firm Advent International. The opening bid was sixty dollars and fifty cents a share, valuing the company at more than fifty-three billion dollars, a twenty-eight percent premium to where the stock was trading. PayPal rejected it as too low. Talks continued, and people close to it say a deal could come together within weeks.

Two things make this unusual. The first is the shape of the buyer. Stripe and Advent would take equal stakes, which means a strategic acquirer and a financial sponsor splitting control of the same asset. That is rare, because they want different things. A sponsor wants a defined exit in five to seven years. A strategic buyer wants the business permanently, folded into its own. Structuring a deal that satisfies both, on governance and on the eventual exit, is genuinely hard.

The second is the financing, and this is the part worth understanding. About fifty billion dollars of the purchase is committed bank debt. Committed financing means the banks sign a binding agreement to fund the whole amount at signing, before they have found anyone to buy the loans from them. They then syndicate it out to other lenders and to the debt markets over the following months. If the market turns while they are holding it, the banks eat the difference. That is why commitment letters carry flex provisions letting them move pricing and terms, and it is why fifty billion dollars of it is a real statement about credit conditions. Banks do not underwrite that kind of paper when they are worried about placing it.

Then there is whether it is allowed. Stripe and PayPal are two of the largest online payment processors in the world, and a combination goes straight into antitrust review here and almost certainly in Europe. The buyers will argue that payments now includes Apple, Adyen, Block and the card networks, so the relevant market is far wider than it looks. That argument has worked before and it has failed before.

So what would make this concrete: a signed price, and the composition of the equity. If Advent is writing most of the check and Stripe is taking a smaller strategic position, this is a leveraged buyout with a partner. If they really are equal, it is a merger of the two biggest names in online payments, and the review will take years.

The second story. The American consumer has stopped cooperating, and the surveys and the spending are no longer telling the same story.

Start with the numbers together. Retail sales down six tenths in July. Sentiment at fifty-one, down eight percent in a month. Twenty-three thousand jobs lost in July, which nobody forecast. Labor force participation at its lowest in fifty years outside the pandemic. Annual inflation at three point four percent, above target. And expectations for inflation a year out at four point three percent.

That last one is the one to watch, and here is why. Central banks care about expected inflation more than realized inflation, because expectations are self-fulfilling. If people believe prices will rise four percent, they ask for pay rises to match and accept price increases without switching, and the belief produces the outcome. Economists call it anchoring. Anchored expectations mean a central bank can look through a bad print. Unanchored expectations mean it cannot.

Four point three percent is not a crisis reading. It is high enough to be uncomfortable when actual inflation is three point four and the target is two.

Which puts the Federal Reserve in the same bind I described on Wednesday, tighter. Inflation above target argues for raising rates. A labor market shedding jobs and a consumer pulling back argue for cutting them. Rising expectations argue against waiting to find out. Morning Brew's read is that Kevin Warsh and the rest of the committee hold steady in September, and holding is what you do when the two halves of your mandate point opposite ways.

There is one more piece, from the other end of the market. The Treasury sold thirty-year bonds this week at five point two two percent, up from five point zero six last month and the highest since August of two thousand one. Long rates are what mortgages and corporate borrowing are priced off. So the consumer is slowing while the cost of the credit that consumer runs on is at a twenty-five-year high. Those two facts do not resolve each other.

What to watch is narrow. August retail sales, and whether the Michigan expectations number goes above four and a half. Below that, this is a soft patch. Above it, the argument inside the Federal Reserve changes shape.
