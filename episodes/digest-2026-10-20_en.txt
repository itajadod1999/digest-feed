One story worth more time this morning: the refinancing gap in American apartments, where three data points from the last two days line up.

The first is a study from CRED iQ, reported by Commercial Observer. It looked at a hundred and thirty-six billion dollars of agency and commercial mortgage-backed apartment loans maturing through twenty twenty-eight. Thirty-one percent of that balance can't refinance without fresh equity from the owner, a shortfall of about six point eight billion dollars, and the September rate hike alone added eight hundred and forty-six million to it. Loans written in twenty twenty-one and twenty twenty-two are worst: fifty-seven percent of that vintage needs new money.

The mechanism is simple. A twenty twenty-one loan was sized against a valuation set when rates were near zero and rents were climbing fast. At maturity, the new lender sizes the loan on today's income and today's rates. The new loan comes out smaller than the old one, and the owner has to write a cheque for the difference, sell, or hand over the keys.

The second data point is what happens when the cheque doesn't get written. The Real Deal, citing Morningstar, reports a one point three billion dollar Blackstone loan on Sun Belt apartments went to special servicing after it matured this month. Most had been paid down through sales; about five hundred and eighteen million was left, against six remaining properties. Blackstone got a three-month forbearance to sell them. Single-sourced so far, but it's the pattern in miniature: a sponsor selling its way out, asset by asset, with the servicer holding the clock.

Bank of America wrote off a hundred and five million, up from two. Both banks' totals of troubled loans fell, which suggests they're clearing problems rather than finding new ones. One idea before we finish.

In the autumn of nineteen fifty-five, two traders, Vincent Kosuga and Sam Siegel, cornered the onion market on the Chicago Mercantile Exchange, controlling almost every onion in Chicago. Then they flipped, went short, and shipped their onions out and back to make the glut look bigger. By March nineteen fifty-six, a fifty-pound bag sold for ten cents, down from two dollars seventy-five in August, and for a while the onions were worth less than their sacks. In nineteen fifty-eight, Eisenhower signed the Onion Futures Act, sponsored by a young congressman named Gerald Ford. Onions are still off-limits to futures trading.

The idea came after. In nineteen sixty-three, a Stanford agricultural economist named Roger Gray went back through four decades of onion prices and split them into periods: no futures market, a developing one, a busy one, and the ban. His finding was the opposite of what Congress intended. Prices had been calmest when futures traded most heavily, and wilder both before the market existed and after it was shut. Speculators, in his reading, had been smoothing the seasons, not distorting them.

The complication came ten years later. In nineteen seventy-three, Aaron Johnson looked at the whole decade of the sixties, with no onion futures at all, and found it the least volatile on record. Then in the two thousands onion prices swung harder than corn or oil. A small, clean experiment, and it still hasn't settled whether speculators calm a market or rock it.
