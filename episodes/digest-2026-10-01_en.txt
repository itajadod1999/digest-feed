One story worth more time this morning: who is lending the money for the artificial intelligence build-out.

What happened. In the last few days, three separate pieces of news have pointed at the same question. KKR published research estimating that technology spending on artificial intelligence is heading toward about eight trillion dollars by twenty thirty, and that about a fifth of the investment-grade bond index is exposed to the theme. The Financial Times reported Nvidia is in early talks with insurers about sharing the risk on loans backed by its chips. And SoftBank settled an eleven point one billion dollar bond sale, the largest high-yield corporate deal on record, at rates up to nine and three quarters percent, to make its final payment into OpenAI.

The mechanism. Much of this spending used to come out of cash flow at a handful of very rich companies. Increasingly, it's borrowed. Smaller cloud operators buy chips with loans secured by the chips themselves. The risk is in that collateral: if the chips fall in value faster than the loan amortizes, the lender is left holding equipment worth less than the debt. Nvidia's insurance idea would cover lenders if pledged chips can't be resold for enough to repay. KKR warns that leases and joint ventures mean the true exposure is bigger than the reported debt.

The players. KKR, as a large private lender, has every reason to flag risk and to price it. Nvidia wants financing to stay cheap for its customers. And SoftBank has borrowed heavily against a stake it can't sell until OpenAI lists.

What the numbers mean in context. Micron's quarter shows the money is real, and the Economist reports American pre-tax profits are up about thirty percent in a year, largely on this spending. The question isn't whether demand exists today. It's what happens to loans written against hardware if demand slows, or if the next generation of chips arrives faster than expected.

What would have to happen next for this to matter. Watch for chip-backed loans showing up in private credit funds and asset-backed deals, whether insurers actually sign on with Nvidia, and how SoftBank's new bonds trade if OpenAI's listing slips further.

One idea before we finish.

In eighteen sixty-five, a twenty-nine-year-old economist named William Stanley Jevons published a book called The Coal Question. Its claim was counterintuitive: making steam engines more efficient wouldn't reduce Britain's coal use. It would increase it. Cheaper power per ton of coal would open up so many new uses that total consumption would rise.

The circumstance is what made the book famous. Britain's industrial lead rested on cheap coal, and Jevons argued the easy seams would run out, costs would climb, and the country's dominance would fade. John Stuart Mill praised it in Parliament, and a royal commission on coal was set up in eighteen sixty-six. Jevons was wrong about the timing and underrated oil, but the paradox outlived the prophecy. In January of last year, after DeepSeek released a cheaper model, Microsoft's Satya Nadella posted that the Jevons paradox strikes again.

The complication is that nobody agrees how often it actually happens. Economists call the effect rebound, and a major review for Britain's Energy Research Centre in two thousand seven, led by Steve Sorrell, found that for most household energy uses the rebound is partial: efficiency still saves energy overall, just less than engineers predict. Full backfire, where use actually rises, seems most plausible for general-purpose technologies like steam, or perhaps computing. Which category artificial intelligence falls into is exactly what the people spending trillions on it are betting on.
