One story worth more time this morning: merchant cash advances, and the small businesses they're sinking.

What happened. A Wall Street Journal analysis found that more than half of the small businesses filing for federal bankruptcy in the first nine months of this year owed money to merchant cash advance companies. The Journal puts the effective cost of these advances as high as two thousand percent a year.

The mechanism. Legally, an advance is not a loan. The funder buys a slice of the business's future sales at a discount, paying, say, fifty thousand dollars today for something like seventy thousand of receipts later, and collects by pulling money straight from the company's bank account, daily or weekly. Because it's structured as a purchase, the funder generally sidesteps interest-rate caps and the disclosure rules that apply to lenders. And because the term can be a few months, a markup that sounds modest becomes a triple or quadruple-digit annual rate.

The trap is the withdrawals. They come out whether or not there's money left for payroll. So a business short of cash takes a second advance to cover the first, then a third. The Journal calls it a debt-driven death spiral.

The players. Funders pitching cash in twenty-four hours, with past credit trouble no obstacle. Borrowers too small or too stretched for a bank line. And the courts, especially in New York, where many of these contracts are written, and where judges have spent years deciding whether particular advances are really loans in disguise. The answer usually turns on whether the funder truly bears the risk that the business simply fails.

What would make it matter more. New York and California already require an estimated annual rate to be disclosed on this kind of financing. Watch for other states following, for bankruptcy judges treating advances as loans, and for whether rates that stay high keep pushing small firms toward the one source of money that says yes in a day.

One idea before we finish.

In nineteen sixty-five, Clair Patterson, a geochemist at Caltech, argued that the lead in ordinary Americans' bodies was far above anything natural, and that most of it came from industry, above all leaded petrol.

He hadn't set out to study health. A decade earlier he had dated the Earth, at about four and a half billion years, using lead isotopes in meteorites. To get a clean reading he had to build one of the first ultra-clean laboratories, because lead was everywhere: in the air, the dust, the reagents. Then he went looking for natural levels, in deep ocean water and later polar ice, and found modern levels dramatically higher.

His opponent was Robert Kehoe, the leading authority on lead toxicity, whose laboratory was funded by the lead and petrol industries. Kehoe held that no harm had been demonstrated at the levels people actually carried. Show the damage, he said, then regulate.

The awkward part is that Patterson proved contamination, not injury. The evidence on children came later, from Herbert Needleman in nineteen seventy-nine, and Needleman's own work was attacked and investigated before he was cleared. Which side should carry the burden of proof, when harm is plausible but not yet shown, was never settled between them.
