Back in English, for the story worth more time this week. It's the gap between the two oil prices.

Most people hear oil as one number. It's really several, and the two that matter are Brent, a blend from the North Sea that prices most crude moving by sea, and West Texas Intermediate, priced at a storage hub in Cushing, Oklahoma, and the benchmark for American barrels. Normally they trade within a few dollars of each other.

This week they split. Brent ended the week slightly higher. The American benchmark fell almost eight percent, its first weekly drop since late August. The gap between them finished near twelve dollars a barrel, several times its usual width.

The reason is geography. Brent is the price of a barrel that has to cross water, so it carries the cost of the strait being shut: the rerouting, the insurance, the chance the next tanker gets hit. The American barrel mostly doesn't cross water, so talk of a truce drains the fear out of it quickly and there's less to put back when the talks stall.

The pinch point isn't crude at all. It's diesel. Refining capacity has been knocked out on two fronts, Ukrainian drones on Russian refineries and the war in the Gulf, so the world is short of distillate even where it has crude. America refines more than it uses and exports the difference. Europe took more than sixty percent of its diesel imports from the United States in August. That's why the export ban idea kept resurfacing this week and kept getting knocked down. Keeping diesel at home might lower the American pump price. It would also export the shortage to allies heading into winter, and the energy secretary himself said bans don't work.

It's also why the bond market cared. Record diesel feeds straight into trucking and shipping costs, which the Journal reports are at their highest since the pandemic supply crunch by some measures.

What would have to happen for the gap to close? The honest answer is the strait. A reopening would pull Brent down toward the American price quickly, and the premium in diesel would follow more slowly, since refineries don't come back overnight. If the Journal's reporting is right and the President has turned down the seven-day plan and expects to resume bombing after November, the premium has at least five more weeks to run. Friday's two percent drop in Brent happened after the rejection was reported, so the market may not be fully convinced either. That one is worth watching.

One idea before we finish.

In eighteen seventy-three Walter Bagehot, then editor of The Economist, published a book called Lombard Street. Its most famous passage says that in a panic, the central bank should lend freely, to any solvent firm, against good collateral, at a high rate of interest. Lend freely so the panic stops. Lend only against good collateral so the bank doesn't bail out the insolvent. Lend at a high rate so nobody comes to the window who can borrow anywhere else.

The circumstance was a specific disaster. In May eighteen sixty-six, Overend, Gurney and Company, the largest discount house in London, failed, and the day became known as Black Friday. The Bank of England, a private company that was then still ambivalent about being anyone's lender of last resort, raised its rate to ten percent and lent heavily, and the panic subsided. Bagehot's argument was that the Bank should admit this was its job, announce it in advance, and do it on principle rather than by improvisation.

The complication is that nearly every word of it turns out to be arguable. What counts as good collateral in a panic, when prices for everything have collapsed? Solvent at what prices? And the high rate, the part that was meant to limit the damage, is the part central banks most often drop. In two thousand and eight the Fed lent enormous sums at very low rates, against collateral it widened as it went. Some economists say that was Bagehot updated for a bigger system. Others say it was precisely what he warned against, and that it taught markets the window would always open cheaply. Historians still argue over whether the Bank ever really followed his rule at all.
