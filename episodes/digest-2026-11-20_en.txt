One story worth more time this morning: why Alphabet has turned up in the municipal bond market.

What happened. On Bloomberg's reporting, Alphabet is listed as the funding recipient on two prepaid energy bond deals totaling three billion dollars, issued through the Southeast Energy Authority, with Goldman Sachs running them. The utility receiving the energy is the Salt River Project, which serves the Phoenix area. Alphabet did a one point six billion dollar deal of the same kind on November fourth.

The mechanism. Public utilities can borrow tax-free, so their bonds pay less than a company's taxable bonds. Normally, borrowing tax-free and parking the money somewhere that pays more is banned outright; that's called an arbitrage bond. Prepaid energy is a carved-out exception. The utility sells tax-exempt bonds and hands the proceeds up front to a funding recipient. In return, the recipient commits to pay for years of gas or electricity delivered to the utility, usually at a discount. The utility locks in cheaper energy, and the recipient gets a large pile of money now, at a financing cost subsidized by the tax code.

Who's involved. For years the recipients were banks. Now insurers, a real estate investment trust, and Alphabet are doing it. Bloomberg counts about forty billion dollars of prepaid energy bonds this year, past last year's record of thirty-one, and BlackRock says the sector has passed a hundred billion outstanding, growing more than twenty-five percent a year since twenty twenty.

What it means. As Levine puts it, ten years ago nobody would have called Google an energy company. Now it is one, and it might as well capture some of the tax subsidy on the way.

What would make it matter. Watch whether other hyperscalers follow Alphabet into the municipal market, and whether Congress or the Internal Revenue Service decides a carve-out written for town gas utilities was never meant to finance data center power.

One idea before we finish.

In nineteen seventy-one, the Chicago economist George Stigler published a paper called The Theory of Economic Regulation. Its claim was blunt: as a rule, regulation is acquired by the industry and is designed and operated primarily for its benefit. Agencies don't drift into the arms of the people they oversee by accident. The industry asks for the rules in the first place, because the state can give it things it can't get alone: subsidies, price floors, and above all, barriers to new competitors.

The circumstance is that Stigler wasn't arguing from theory alone. He went to the data. One of his examples was the weight limits states set for trucks in the nineteen thirties. Where railroads competed hardest with trucking, the limits tended to be tighter. Where farmers needed trucks to get crops to market, they tended to be looser. The rules mapped onto who had the most at stake and could organize, not onto any obvious public need.

The complication arrived within a decade. If regulation serves the regulated, it should be very hard to undo. Yet in nineteen seventy-eight Congress deregulated the airlines, over the objections of most of the airlines, and trucking followed in nineteen eighty, over the objections of the truckers and the Teamsters. Sam Peltzman, his colleague, spent years refining the theory to fit; others said ideas had simply mattered more than Stigler allowed. Stigler won the Nobel in nineteen eighty-two, partly for this paper, in the middle of the very wave of deregulation it struggled to explain.
