One story worth more time this morning: the week the bond market started asking how artificial intelligence gets paid for.

What happened. On Wednesday, CoreWeave's nine and five-eighths percent bonds due twenty thirty-two fell below eighty cents on the dollar, a yield of roughly fifteen percent. By Thursday the annual cost of insuring its debt against default was about eleven point four percent, which Morning Brew reckoned implies better than a sixty percent chance of insolvency within five years. The shares bounced almost six percent on Thursday after Jensen Huang said its borrowing costs were falling, then fell about twelve on Friday. Nvidia lost around two hundred and seventy billion dollars in market value over the week, by Finimize's count.

The mechanism. CoreWeave and the other so-called neoclouds buy huge numbers of Nvidia chips, rent the computing to companies like Microsoft and OpenAI, and borrow against those rental contracts to pay for the chips. The structure holds if two things hold: money stays affordable, and the chips keep their value for the life of the loan. Wednesday's inflation report pressed on the first, by raising the odds of another rate rise. Doubts about the second have been building for weeks.

How far it spread. By Friday the selling had moved past the neoclouds to bonds financing data centres leased to Meta and Microsoft, tenants nobody worries about. The fear there wasn't default but delay: power shortages, local opposition, construction setbacks. Oracle, which has borrowed heavily to build for OpenAI, now sits one notch above junk.

The private side. Much of this debt doesn't trade at all. It sits in private funds, marked quarterly, which is why the week's argument over Apollo's daily valuations mattered. Monroe Capital's Ted Koenig called daily marks "an illusion of precision, not accuracy." But Tuesday's WhiteHorse Finance story showed the gap from the other side: a listed lending fund trading around sixty cents on the dollar of its own stated value, while bids for its loans reportedly came in the high nineties. When public prices and private marks disagree that much, one of them is wrong.

The honest counterargument. Demand hasn't cracked. Taiwan Semiconductor reported its sixth straight record quarter, and CoreWeave's chief executive, Mike Intrator, says the backlog covers the debt. Jefferies calls it an indigestion period. All of that can be true while the financing is still too expensive.

What would make it matter. Three dates. The Fed on the twenty-eighth, because each extra rise raises the cost of rolling this debt. Whether CoreWeave or its peers can borrow again, and at what price. And the Anthropic listing in November, which is, for what it's worth, the clearest public test of what investors will pay for the demand side of this story.

Back in English, and one idea before we finish.

In March of nineteen twenty-nine, Harold Hotelling, a mathematician and statistician at Stanford, published a short paper in the Economic Journal called Stability in Competition. Picture one street, customers spread evenly along it, and two shops. Each wants as many customers as possible, so instead of spreading out to serve the town, both creep toward the middle until they stand side by side.

The circumstance is what makes it more than a puzzle about shops. Hotelling wasn't really writing about geography. He was writing about sameness. He complained in the paper that cider was too homogeneous, that Methodist and Presbyterian churches were too alike, and that the Republican and Democratic parties offered voters almost the same thing. His street explained why competition, supposed to produce variety, so often produces two near-identical choices. Anthony Downs turned it into the median voter theory in nineteen fifty-seven.

The complication is that the mathematics didn't hold. In nineteen seventy-nine, three economists, Claude d'Aspremont, Jean Gabszewicz and Jacques-François Thisse, showed that once the two shops compete on price as well as location, Hotelling's own model has no stable outcome when the shops get close. Change one assumption about the cost of travel, and the shops do the opposite: they flee to the two ends of the street to avoid a price war. And in politics, parties in many countries have spent the last two decades moving apart, not together. Whether Hotelling found a law or a special case is still argued over.
