One story worth more time this morning, and it's two words from contract law: force majeure.

What happened. Oracle sent a force majeure notice to the developer of Project Jupiter, a data centre campus in New Mexico designed for two point four five gigawatts, enough to power about one point eight million homes. The developer is a unit of Blue Owl Capital. Oracle isn't walking away as the tenant. It's reserving the right to delay its payments if the campus doesn't come online in twenty twenty-eight as planned, after a permit key to its energy supply was denied amid local opposition. About twenty banks lent roughly eighteen billion dollars for construction, and on Bloomberg's reporting that debt already trades below ninety cents on the dollar.

Why it matters is the mechanism. Most data centre financings are built on a simple bargain. A developer borrows to build, a technology giant signs a long lease, and lenders are really lending against that tenant's promise to pay. The tenant's credit is what makes a speculative building financeable.

So the fine print on delay is everything. Matt Levine points to Meta's big data centre financing with Blue Owl last October. Per S and P's report on it, the tenant can't terminate during construction, and even a force majeure delay only lets it abate rent up to a cap of seven hundred and fifty million dollars, sized to the insurance. Lenders there know their worst case in advance.

Nobody outside has seen what Oracle's contract says. If a denied permit counts as an event beyond anyone's control, the tenant's payments can pause while interest on eighteen billion dollars keeps accruing. That's why a notice that changes nothing on paper moved the loan, and the shares of both Oracle and Blue Owl, down about three and a half percent. Blue Owl says the commitments are unchanged.

There are two risks in this market, and they point in opposite directions. The one everybody discusses is building too much: demand fades and data centres sit empty. The nearer one is building too slowly, where demand is fine but this site, in this county, can't get its power. Jupiter is the second kind, and a law firm, Quinn Emanuel, has already told clients that force majeure clauses will be central to the disputes ahead.

What would matter next: other tenants sending similar notices on stalled projects, or the energy permit coming through, which turns this back into a scheduling problem.

One idea before we finish.

In September eighteen fifty-four, cholera killed more than six hundred people in about ten days around Broad Street in Soho, London. A doctor named John Snow, who believed cholera spread through water rather than bad air, traced the deaths to one public pump and persuaded the local board to take off its handle.

The circumstance is the best part. A local curate, Henry Whitehead, set out to prove Snow wrong. He went door to door, knew the parish intimately, and ended up finding the likely source: a baby who fell ill just before the outbreak, whose mother had emptied the water from its nappies into a cesspool a few feet from the well. The sceptic became Snow's strongest witness.

The complication is that the famous story may be tidier than the facts. Deaths were already falling before the handle came off, as Snow himself acknowledged. The celebrated map was drawn afterwards, for the second edition of his book, not used to find the pump. And the official inquiry in eighteen fifty-five rejected his water theory. His stronger evidence was elsewhere, comparing households supplied by two water companies drawing from different stretches of the Thames. Whether the handle saved anyone, or simply became the symbol, historians still argue.
