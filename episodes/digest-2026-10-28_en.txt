One story worth more time this morning: Ares Capital and MidCap, and why business development companies are starting to merge.

What happened. Ares Capital, the largest of these publicly traded private credit funds, agreed to buy MidCap Financial from Apollo. MidCap shareholders get ten dollars and eighty-eight cents a share: about a dollar twenty-one in cash, paid by Ares Management, and nine sixty-seven in Ares Capital stock. That's a thirty percent premium to Monday's close.

The mechanism. These funds lend to mid-sized companies, and their shares can trade well away from the value of the loans inside. MidCap reports net asset value of about twelve eighty-six a share, but closed Monday at eight thirty-six, a thirty-five percent discount. So the same price is two things at once: a thirty percent premium to the market, and a fifteen percent discount to book.

Matt Levine's point in Money Stuff is about the stock leg. The Ares shares are counted at the greater of net asset value or market price, and Ares Capital trades around four percent below its net asset value. So the nine sixty-seven in stock is worth closer to nine thirty-two at market. His wider point is that many of these funds trade at much deeper discounts, and when they buy each other with their own stock, the headline will lean on book value, not price.

Why it matters. Levine expects more consolidation. A fund stuck at a deep discount has few ways out: buy back stock, liquidate slowly, or sell to a bigger fund whose shares trade nearer book. What would make this a trend is the next one, especially between two funds that both trade at deep discounts.

One more: Israel's election, and the arithmetic of a hung Knesset.

What happened. Turnout was the highest in a generation, in the first vote since October seventh. Counting goes on, and the outlets frame it differently. The Journal says Likud has moved into the lead, denying Benjamin Netanyahu's opponents a victory. The Economist's headline is that Mr Netanyahu has lost his majority, with Likud perhaps a quarter smaller. Le Monde says the Netanyahu camp is clearly down, but Gadi Eisenkot's opposition failed to unseat Likud. All agree that neither bloc can form a government on its own.

The mechanism. The Knesset has a hundred and twenty seats, so sixty-one is a majority. Once results are certified, the president consults every party and hands one leader the first chance to build a coalition, with twenty-eight days plus a possible extension of fourteen.

The players. Arab parties drew record support, and the Times says either side may need two of them unless someone defects. Mr Netanyahu has effectively ruled them out, and Mr Eisenkot has balked. The Economist reports that Mr Netanyahu said on social media that the left and the Arabs were stealing the election, without offering evidence.

What to watch: whether any party in either bloc is willing to switch sides, and whether Mr Eisenkot can stomach governing with Arab support. The Times says it could take weeks or months.

One idea before we finish.

In the summer of nineteen forty-eight, Robert Merton, a sociologist at Columbia, published an essay in the Antioch Review called The Self-Fulfilling Prophecy. A false belief about a situation, he argued, can produce the behaviour that makes it come true. He opened with a parable. In nineteen thirty-two, the Last National Bank, run by a man he called Cartwright Millingville, is perfectly solvent, until a rumour says it isn't. Depositors queue to withdraw, and the queue is what breaks it.

The bank was the easy example. The case he cared about was race in American labour. White unions kept Black workers out on the grounds that they were strikebreakers. Locked out of the unions, many had no work except as strikebreakers, and so the belief manufactured its own proof.

The complication came twenty years later. In nineteen sixty-eight, Robert Rosenthal and Lenore Jacobson told teachers at a California school that some pupils were about to bloom intellectually. The pupils had been picked at random, and they gained more. Pygmalion in the Classroom became famous, and then contested. Later reviews, notably by the psychologist Lee Jussim, found such effects real but small, and teachers' expectations mostly accurate. Which leaves the hard question: how often a prophecy causes the outcome, and how often it simply saw it coming.
