One story worth more time this morning. The commercial mortgage-backed securities market is having its best year in a long while, and almost none of it is the kind of lending most people picture.

Private-label issuance in the United States reached seventy-six point two billion dollars through July, by Trepp's count. Strong by any recent standard. But the composition is the story.

Nearly all the growth is in what the market calls single-asset, single-borrower deals, and the two structures behave nothing alike. A conduit deal pools dozens of unrelated mortgages on unrelated buildings from unrelated borrowers, and the diversification is the product: if four loans go bad, the other sixty carry the bonds. A single-asset, single-borrower deal is one building, financed on its own and cut into bonds by seniority. No diversification at all. What you own is that asset with that sponsor, and your protection comes entirely from where your slice sits in the stack.

Office has been the biggest driver of that growth, which sounds surprising until you look at which offices. Trophy assets, named sponsors, signed rent rolls, financed one at a time on their own merits. Lenders will underwrite a specific tower they can go and stand in. What they will not do is buy a blind pool with unknown office loans scattered through it. The recovery in office lending is real, and narrow by construction.

The other new category is data centers. Trepp now counts them as a distinct single-borrower type, absent from conduit entirely, at about seven and a half percent of issuance. Hold that one lightly. The share rests on five loans. It is concentrated, not representative.

Now set it against the maturity wall. Roughly sixty-five billion dollars of commercial mortgage-backed securities debt comes due before year end, and more than a trillion and a half of commercial real estate loans mature by then across all lender types. A maturity wall is just the fact that these loans were written with balloon payments, on the assumption that at the end of the term the borrower would refinance rather than repay. That assumption was made when rates were lower and values were higher. Every loan hitting its date now faces three doors: refinance at today's cost, write a check of fresh equity to close the gap, or hand back the keys.

So the honest read on seventy-six billion dollars is that the capital markets are open, and open selectively. Watch whether conduit volume picks up in the fourth quarter. That is the tell for whether this widens out or stays a market for trophies only.

The second story. The Los Angeles Lakers just sold for twelve and a half billion dollars, and the interesting part is who is selling.

A group led by Bob Iger, the former head of Disney, and Josh Kushner, the venture investor behind Thrive Capital, is buying control, according to ESPN. If the league approves it, it is the highest valuation ever paid for a sports team anywhere.

Now the timing. The record it breaks is about a year old, and this same franchise set it. Last summer Mark Walter bought control from the Buss family for roughly ten billion dollars. So the asset is up about twenty-five percent in twelve months, in a business with no earnings growth to speak of, and the team lost LeBron James in the interim.

That is not a story about basketball improving. It is a story about a seller. Walter chairs Guggenheim Partners and owns the Dodgers, and he is under federal investigation over alleged tax fraud involving billions of dollars of loans to his own companies. Bloomberg reports the sale will help him raise cash to pay those loans down. When the marginal seller is a forced seller and the price still rises a quarter, that tells you something about the depth of the bid.

And the bid has changed shape. On Tuesday I mentioned Apollo putting two point six billion dollars into the Yankees' parent company, and fifty-two private equity deals in professional sports this year against seventy-one in all of last year. Iger and Kushner are the next expression of it. Iger advises Thrive, whose experiences arm bought into the San Francisco Giants earlier this year and was close to buying a piece of the World Cup until the backlash over FIFA's private investment plan killed it. The two were working on a Las Vegas expansion team before pivoting to buy an existing one.

The mechanism worth understanding is why these prices keep clearing. There are thirty teams and the league controls how many exist. Media rights are contracted years forward and negotiated collectively, so revenue is unusually visible. And the league now permits institutional capital in minority positions, which created a bid for stakes that never had one.

What would make this matter beyond the sports pages is the approval vote, and then the first real test: a franchise that has to sell at a bad moment. Every price here has been set in a rising market by a buyer who wanted in. Nobody has had to find out what one of these is worth when nobody does.
