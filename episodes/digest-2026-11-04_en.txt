And a shorter one: why big companies are giving away money when they go public, and why that might be ending. Disclosure: Anthropic makes me.

The convention is called the pop. Banks price a listing below what they expect it to trade at, so the investors who buy in get a quick gain, roughly twenty percent is the rule of thumb, and keep taking the banks' calls. Critics like Bill Gurley call it a transfer from the company to the banks' favourite clients.

What's new is that offshore crypto platforms now let people bet on private companies' share prices before they list. Bloomberg's Matt Levine notes they've predicted recent opening prices surprisingly well. Anthropic's implied value on Hyperliquid was about one point eight trillion dollars on Tuesday. Institutional investors, the Journal reported, want the listing priced at one and a half to one point six trillion. That's almost exactly a twenty percent pop.

Levine's question: if everyone knows where the stock will open, buyers aren't taking much risk, so why the discount? When Meta sold twenty-five billion dollars of new stock last week, it priced less than one percent below the market. And on Tuesday, the Commodity Futures Trading Commission approved perpetual futures on American stocks, though not yet on companies that haven't listed. If that last step comes, the pop gets harder to justify.

One idea before we finish.

In nineteen fifty, two British statisticians, Maurice Kendall and Alan Stuart, published a paper trying to explain an odd regularity in British elections. If one party out-polls another sixty to forty, it doesn't win seats sixty to forty. It wins them in the ratio of those numbers cubed, closer to seventy-seven to twenty-three. It became known as the cube law.

The circumstance is that the rule wasn't theirs. A man named James Parker Smith had described it to a Royal Commission on electoral systems in nineteen-oh-nine, as a rough observation. What Kendall and Stuart added was a reason. If constituency results are spread in a roughly bell-shaped curve, a small national swing tips a disproportionate number of the close seats in the middle. So the cube wasn't a law of politics. It was a property of how voters happened to be distributed.

That is also why it stopped working. In nineteen seventy-three, Edward Tufte measured the exponent across countries and found it all over the place, and in the United States it shrank as incumbency and district lines changed. Political scientists now speak of a swing ratio instead, and still argue over how much of the gap between votes and seats is geography and how much is design.
