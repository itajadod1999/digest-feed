One story worth more time this morning: the artificial intelligence build-out has hit its first real credit wobble.

What happened. On Wednesday, CoreWeave's shares fell about seventeen percent. The bigger move was in its bonds. Its nine and five-eighths percent notes due twenty thirty-two fell below eighty cents on the dollar, a yield of roughly fifteen percent, which is distressed territory for a company with tens of billions of dollars in contracted revenue. The price of insuring its debt against default nearly doubled in a week, from about three hundred and seventy basis points to more than six hundred and forty.

The mechanism. CoreWeave and the other so-called neoclouds buy enormous numbers of Nvidia chips, rent the computing power to companies like Microsoft and OpenAI, and finance the chips with debt secured against those rental contracts. It works as long as two things hold: borrowing stays affordable, and the chips stay valuable for the life of the loan. Wednesday's inflation print hit the first. The Chinese memory maker CXMT's progress, and Broadcom chief Hock Tan likening many investors to gamblers in a casino, have chipped at the second.

The broader numbers. Morgan Stanley counts artificial intelligence-related debt issuance worldwide at about twenty-three billion dollars in September, down from a peak of a hundred and thirteen billion in June. Several data-centre listings have been shelved. Another builder, Volta, is reportedly selling five billion dollars of debt at eleven percent or more, though that's single-sourced, from Semafor. Jamie Dimon says JPMorgan is tightening up a little on credit. Jefferies calls it an indigestion period.

The other side. Demand hasn't cracked. Taiwan Semiconductor's record quarter came with a reportedly raised outlook for the year. David Solomon at Goldman says demand for computing still exceeds supply. CoreWeave's chief executive, Mike Intrator, says the market is acting irrationally and the backlog covers the debt.

What would make it matter. Three things over the next six weeks. The Fed on the twenty-eighth, because each extra hike raises the cost of rolling this debt. Whether CoreWeave or its peers can borrow again, and at what price. And the Anthropic listing in November, the clearest test of what public investors will pay for the demand side of this story.

One idea before we finish.

Between nineteen twenty-four and nineteen thirty-two, at Western Electric's Hawthorne Works outside Chicago, researchers set out to find the lighting that made factory workers most productive. Output went up when they brightened the lights. It went up when they dimmed them. In one test it held up with the lights turned down to roughly moonlight. Then, in a separate room, a handful of women assembling telephone relays were given rest breaks, shorter hours, and free snacks, and output rose almost regardless of what changed. Elton Mayo and Fritz Roethlisberger concluded the workers were responding to being watched and consulted. It became known as the Hawthorne effect.

The circumstance is what later reviewers fixed on. Two of the five women in the relay room were replaced in nineteen twenty-eight for being uncooperative, and one of the replacements became the fastest worker in the room. Pay was tied to the group's output. And from nineteen twenty-nine, everyone in Cicero, Illinois knew what losing a factory job meant. In nineteen seventy-eight, Richard Franke and James Kaul reran the numbers and argued that discipline, money and fear explained most of the gain.

The complication is that the original lighting data were long believed destroyed. When the economists Steven Levitt and John List found them in archives and analysed them in twenty eleven, they found some sign of a Hawthorne effect, but much weaker than the legend, and tangled up with the fact that the lights were changed on Sundays and output rose early in the week anyway. Whether people work differently because they're being observed is still a live question. Whether Hawthorne ever showed it is another.
