Now a minute of French, and then the European news in it.

How would you say that something has nothing to do with it. Not that it is irrelevant in some formal sense. That flat, faintly impatient correction you make when somebody has connected two things that are not connected.



Here it is.

Ça n'a rien à voir.



Ça n'a rien à voir.

This is the calque trap in its purest form. English says nothing to do with, so the hand reaches for the verb meaning to do. French reaches for the verb meaning to see. There is nothing to reason toward. The expression simply took the other verb, and the word-for-word version gives you away in one syllable.

Now one story worth more time this morning.

Kevin Warsh is expected to raise rates a quarter point this afternoon, the first increase in three years, at the first meeting he has chaired. Futures markets put the odds at about ninety-two percent, up from seventy a week ago. The repricing happened last Friday, when August core prices rose three tenths of a percent in the month, more than expected.

Here is what makes it genuinely hard. Most of the inflation on the page comes from oil, and oil is expensive because of a war and a closed strait. That is a supply shock, and the orthodox response to a supply shock is to look through it, because raising rates does not produce more crude. It only reduces demand for everything else until the price of everything else comes down. Tightening into a supply shock is the standard way to get a recession with the inflation still in it.

The argument for doing it anyway is about expectations. If people come to believe three or four percent is simply the going rate, the shock stops being temporary and becomes the baseline. Warsh appears to hold that view. He said at Jackson Hole last month that the inflation reports do not tell him underlying trends have improved.

Then there is the President, who has spent two years demanding lower rates and appointed Warsh because his predecessor would not deliver them. Kevin Hassett, running the National Economic Council, said at the weekend that the President supports the Federal Reserve's right to decide independently. That sentence is doing a lot of work.

And there is the reason the long end will not cooperate either way. In mid-August the Treasury secretary said he would expand buybacks of long-dated government debt to hold yields down, tranches of up to four billion dollars, near-weekly. Since then the ten-year has gone from four point six five percent to above five. The Economist made the arithmetic plain this morning: gross federal debt is about forty trillion dollars, roughly ten thousand times the size of each buyback. The bond market is not broken. It is working exactly as it should, and what it is saying is unwelcome.

So what to watch is not the quarter point. It is the projections. If the committee signals more increases, it has decided the oil shock is spreading. If it moves once and says nothing further, it has decided this is a price shock it intends to sit out.

One idea before we finish.

In October eighteen fifty-nine a steam clipper called the Royal Charter went down off Anglesey, two days from Liverpool, coming home from Melbourne with gold aboard. Around four hundred and fifty people died on that ship, and more than a hundred and thirty other vessels sank in the same storm. The man who took it hardest held a government post nobody thought mattered. Robert FitzRoy ran the Meteorological Department at the Board of Trade, and had been arguing for years that a gale could be seen coming.

The circumstance is the good part. FitzRoy was not a meteorologist. He was the naval officer who had captained the Beagle and carried Darwin around the world, and he attacked the problem like a sailor: put barometers in the fishing ports, wire the readings to London, work out where the weather is going. He began hoisting warning cones over the harbours in eighteen sixty, launched a storm warning service in February eighteen sixty-one, and that August printed in the Times a thing that had not previously existed. He needed a word for it and invented one. Forecast.

The complication is that his critics were not being obtuse. The scientific men of the period held that the atmosphere was not predictable in any rigorous sense, and FitzRoy could not show them a method. His forecasts were judgement, they were often wrong, and the wrong ones were collected and quoted back at him. And many fishing fleet owners hated the warnings, because a cone over the harbour meant the boats stayed in and earned nothing, on the word of a man in London who might have it wrong. FitzRoy died in eighteen sixty-five and the Board of Trade suspended the forecasts. The fishermen had asked for that.
