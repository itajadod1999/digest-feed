One story worth more time this morning: what Nvidia actually was to the rest of the market, and what changes now.

Start with the plumbing. Nvidia isn't just the company that sells about four out of five of the world's artificial intelligence chips. It has become a financier. Morning Brew puts its guarantees on data center leases at about a hundred billion dollars, and its stakes in other companies at more than a hundred and ten billion, among them OpenAI, CoreWeave, Nebius and Anthropic. Many of those companies use the money to buy Nvidia's chips. DealBook calls it the central bank of the artificial intelligence economy.

That's why the CoreWeave deal matters. A junk-rated borrower gets an investment-grade price by pledging its chips and contracts, and that collateral is only as good as the industry around Nvidia. Over the past month Nvidia has lost more than a trillion dollars of market value, and the shares are more than thirty percent below their peak. Analysts are already asking whether the appetite for backstopping customers leaves with Huang.

One idea before we finish.

In November nineteen sixty, the journal Science published a paper titled Doomsday: Friday, thirteen November, A.D. twenty twenty-six. That's today. Its authors were Heinz von Foerster, an Austrian physicist running a cybernetics lab at the University of Illinois, and two of his students. They fitted a curve to estimates of world population going back two thousand years, and found it wasn't growing exponentially. It was growing hyperbolically, the growth rate itself rising as the population rose. Extend that curve and it goes to infinity on a specific date: today.

The circumstance is what made the paper. The postwar population boom was at full speed, and the standard debate was Malthusian: people would multiply until food ran out. Von Foerster argued the reverse. The more people there were, the better they got at cooperating and inventing past natural limits, so growth fed itself. His descendants, the paper said, would not starve. They would be squeezed to death. The date was a provocation, but the model was meant seriously, and for a while it fit unnervingly well.

Then the curve broke. Global population growth peaked in the late nineteen sixties at a bit over two percent a year and has fallen since, to under one percent. Falling birth rates as countries grew richer simply weren't in the model. But the idea didn't die. In nineteen ninety-three the economist Michael Kremer used the same hyperbolic history to argue that more people means more ideas, which means faster growth. Whether von Foerster found a real law that later broke, or just fitted a neat curve to a few dozen rough estimates, is still argued.
