One story worth more time this morning: apartments are now the problem child in commercial mortgage bonds.

What happened. Trepp puts the delinquency rate on commercial mortgage-backed securities at eight point oh two percent in September, up seventeen basis points in a month, or about forty-eight and a half billion dollars of loans. Office is still the worst, at about twelve percent. But multifamily jumped thirty-five basis points to eight point oh four. A year ago it was six point five nine.

The mechanism. A loan counts as delinquent once a payment is thirty days late, or when it reaches maturity and the borrower can't pay it off. That second route is doing the work. Many apartment loans in these pools were written in twenty twenty-one and twenty twenty-two, at very low cap rates, often floating rate with interest-rate caps that ran two or three years. As the caps expire and the loans come due, the owner has to refinance with the ten-year near five point three percent. Often the building's income won't support a new loan of the same size. So the owner puts in fresh equity, negotiates an extension, or stops paying.

The players. Borrowers and special servicers are extending where they can. In Queens this month, the four hundred and twenty-five million dollar loan on the JACX office complex was pushed out to twenty twenty-nine. Then there's the capital waiting for the wreckage. On the Walker Webcast this week, Chris Lee of K K R Real Estate described the wave of multifamily maturities as an opportunity, while calling for a highly selective approach. And the borrowers who can refinance are taking money out: CRE Daily says cash-out deals are now fifty-five percent of conduit refinancings.

What the numbers mean. These are bond-market loans, a slice of the apartment market, not the whole of it. And not every measure is worsening. CRED iQ, which counts a wider range of trouble, has overall distress at about ten point eight percent, slightly lower than a year ago, with Chicago the worst big city at about a quarter. So it's a rotation: office distress plateauing at a very high level, apartments catching up.

What would make it matter. Watch the long end; a ten-year back toward four and a half would make many of these refinancings work again. Watch whether servicers keep extending or start foreclosing. Wells Fargo has scheduled an October twentieth auction of a Boston office tower, a hundred Summer Street, though that's on a single trade report. And watch the junior bonds. If apartment loans start being written down rather than extended, the lowest slices feel it first.

One idea before we finish.

In nineteen eighty-four, a Yale sociologist named Charles Perrow published a book called Normal Accidents. His argument was that in some systems, serious accidents are not failures of care but a property of the design. Two features produce them: interactive complexity, where parts affect each other in ways nobody anticipated, and tight coupling, where one failure reaches the next faster than anyone can step in. Combine them and, sooner or later, small failures line up into a big one.

The circumstance is the interesting part. Perrow wasn't an engineer. After Three Mile Island in March of nineteen seventy-nine, the presidential commission investigating it asked him for an outside view. He saw a chain of ordinary things: a stuck valve, an indicator light that showed what operators had commanded rather than what had actually happened, maintenance tags hanging over other gauges. Each was minor. Together, in a plant where everything was connected and fast, they produced a partial meltdown. The official account leaned on operator error. Perrow's view was that they'd been handed a system nobody could understand in real time.

The complication is that a group at Berkeley, Todd La Porte, Karlene Roberts and Gene Rochlin, set out to show he was too pessimistic. They studied aircraft-carrier flight decks, air traffic control and the Diablo Canyon nuclear plant, and argued that some organizations run dangerous, tightly coupled operations with remarkably few accidents, through training, redundancy and a culture of reporting mistakes. In nineteen ninety-three, the political scientist Scott Sagan tested both theories against near-misses with American nuclear weapons, and found the record looked more like Perrow's world. The two schools still argue about whether reliability can be engineered, or whether the near-misses are just accidents that haven't happened yet.
