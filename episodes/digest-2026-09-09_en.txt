Now two stories worth more time, and the first is a tax break unwinding across the country.

For two decades, states competed for data centers with sales-tax exemptions on the equipment inside them, and the fiscal notes on those bills routinely promised the cost would be negligible. That has not aged well. Virginia has the world's largest data center market, and its exemption was worth nearly two billion dollars in the twenty twenty-five fiscal year. In twenty twenty-two it was a hundred thirty-six million. That is not growth in a program. That is a different program.

The mechanism matters, because the exemption is not the problem by itself. A sales-tax break on servers is uncapped by construction: it scales with whatever gets bought, and what gets bought is now set by a global buildout nobody in a statehouse in twenty ten was modeling. Then there's the second cost, which never appears in the tax line. A data center's electricity load lands on a grid whose upgrades every ratepayer helps fund, so the budget hit and the utility-bill hit arrive from different directions and get argued about separately.

The retreat is now general. Lawmakers in at least twenty-eight states filed bills this year to amend these exemptions. In June alone, Illinois paused its program, Arizona enacted a three-year moratorium, New Jersey froze its incentive, and one California city banned data centers outright. North Carolina is phasing its break out entirely by twenty thirty-two.

Set that beside the Virginia project I told you about on Sunday, killed on a defective hearing notice, and you have the two pressure points. Local land use decides whether a campus gets built. State tax policy decides the return on all of them. Watch whether any repeal survives an operator threatening to build one state over, because that threat created the exemptions and it has not stopped working.

The second story is why that mathematics claim isn't settled.

The Navier-Stokes equations describe how fluids flow, and we use them constantly — they are in every weather model and every aircraft design. What nobody has proved is that their solutions always stay well-behaved, that you cannot start from perfectly smooth conditions and have the mathematics run off to infinity in finite time. That is the open problem, one of seven the Clay Mathematics Institute attached a million dollars to in two thousand.

The difficulty is that in mathematics a proof is not an announcement. It is a document other mathematicians read line by line, for months, and that has not happened here. Meanwhile a mathematician at New York University says he was days from publishing his own solution, with a co-author at Anthropic — that disclosure again — and other academics say the result is being described as more than it is.

So the test is narrow and checkable: either the proof gets posted and survives people who have spent careers on this equation, or it doesn't. The priority fight and the timing against a public offering are atmosphere — but that is the condition under which announcements outrun proofs.

One idea before we finish.

In nineteen seventy-four, Ronald Coase published a paper called The Lighthouse in Economics. The lighthouse had been the standard classroom example of a public good for a century — Mill used it, Sidgwick used it, Samuelson used it in the textbook that taught economics to a generation. The argument seemed airtight: you cannot bill a ship for looking at your light, so no private party will build one, so the state must.

Coase went and looked at the records. In eighteen twenty there were forty-six lighthouses in England and Wales, and thirty-four had been built by private individuals, paid for through dues collected at ports when ships docked. The circumstance is the interesting part. Coase had spent his career arguing that economists reason from armchairs and then legislate from the conclusions, and the lighthouse was the cleanest demonstration going: everyone had used it and nobody had checked it.

The complication is that he may have won the small point and lost the large one. Van Zandt, working the same records in nineteen ninety-three, found those private owners held Crown patents and statutory monopolies, and had their dues set by law and collected by government customs agents. The state never left. It moved from building the thing to enforcing the bill. Whether that still counts as private provision depends on where you put the interesting boundary, and nobody ever agreed on that.
