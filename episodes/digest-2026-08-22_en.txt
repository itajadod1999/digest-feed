One story worth more time this morning, and it is the one underneath all the others. The Journal's summary of it, published Friday, was the wild week when Scott Bessent was schooled by the bond market.

The forty trillion dollar milestone is symbolic. What is not symbolic is that the thirty-year Treasury spent Tuesday at a nineteen-year high, and that the Treasury Secretary responded within a day, doubling the maximum size of his buyback operations from two billion dollars to at least four billion, starting the ninth of September.

A buyback is not what it sounds like. The Treasury is not retiring debt. It buys back older, less liquid long bonds and funds the purchase by issuing something else, usually shorter paper. Total borrowing does not shrink by a dollar. What changes is the maturity mix, and specifically how much long-dated supply the market must absorb. Less long supply, lower long yields. That is the entire trade, and it treats a symptom. Leaning shorter also means refinancing more of the debt more often, which is comfortable while rates behave and expensive when they don't.

The deeper problem is that the buyer base changed underneath all of this. Pension funds that historically absorbed long government paper have stepped back, and technology companies are now issuing corporate bonds at enormous scale to build data centres. Two very large borrowers competing for the same pool of long-duration money, and one of them can offer a spread.

What Bessent added this week was forward guidance, hinting more operations may come. JPMorgan's team said publicly that repeated buybacks carry credibility risk, which is a polite way of saying the market will learn to wait for the next one before it buys. An intervention that works once is a tool. An intervention the market expects is a subsidy, and it gets priced.

What would make this concrete is the next long-dated auction, and the ratio of bids to paper offered. The buyback moved the price. Whether it moved the buyers is an open question, and gold is one answer.

One last thing from the week, and it is for the ear. The Economist's word of the week was ongaku kissa, the Japanese music café. Listening bars: a serious sound system, a wall of records, low light, and a firm expectation that you shut up and listen. They date to postwar Tokyo, when almost nobody could afford a stereo. One thing to sit with this morning.

There is a limit to our life, but to knowledge there is no limit. With what is limited to pursue after what is unlimited is a perilous thing.

That is Zhuangzi, from the third chapter of the book that carries his name, written in the fourth century before the common era, during the Warring States period in China. What is known about him is thin and mostly anecdote. He supervised a lacquer garden in the state of Song. When the king of Chu sent officials to offer him the premiership, he is supposed to have asked whether a sacred tortoise, dead three thousand years and wrapped in silk in the royal temple, would not rather be alive and dragging its tail through the mud. Then he sent them away.

The observation I would make is that the sentence is arithmetic wearing the clothes of wisdom. Any finite life pursuing any unlimited thing loses on the same terms, so the argument does not single out knowledge at all. It works just as well against pursuing skill, or ease, or the Way. And the chapter it opens goes on to praise a butcher who has spent nineteen years getting one cut exactly right.
