One story worth more time this morning: America's exit from Iraq.

What happened. The last American and coalition forces left Erbil Air Base by today's deadline, under a deal struck with Baghdad in September twenty twenty-four. The Patriot missile batteries went with them. Iraq's prime minister, Ali al-Zaidi, declared a two-day holiday and called today sovereignty day.

The numbers. The Journal puts the cost at more than twenty-three years and one point seven trillion dollars. Troops left once, in twenty eleven, and came back in twenty fourteen to fight Islamic State.

The mechanism, and the problem. Islamic State is mostly defeated. But the Shia militias that fought it, many backed by Iran, are stronger than ever. During the war with Iran, the Economist notes, they've been attacking bases and energy infrastructure across the region from Iraqi soil, including the drone strike on the Saudi pipeline this month.

The players. Al-Zaidi wants the militias to hand in their weapons and says the withdrawal can open negotiations. Some may disarm; the hardline groups don't look willing. And the Journal reports the embassy in Baghdad now sends about two kidnapping warnings a week; the paper's own team left the city this month after officials warned of a militia kidnapping plot.

What would have to happen next for this to matter. Watch whether the militias test the gap, against the embassy or Gulf energy, now that the air defences are gone. And watch whether any disarmament talks actually start. A deal with Iran through Qatar would change the militias' incentives more than anything in Baghdad.

One idea before we finish.

In nineteen sixty-eight, a German mathematician named Dietrich Braess published a short paper with an uncomfortable result. In a road network where every driver picks the fastest route for themselves, adding a new road can make everyone's trip slower. Not just some drivers. In his example, all of them.

The setting was unglamorous: the mathematics traffic planners use to predict how drivers spread across a network. The problem is that each driver's best choice ignores the delay they impose on others. A shortcut that looks good to each person individually can pull enough traffic onto a few shared links that the whole system settles somewhere worse.

The famous real-world example happened here. On Earth Day in nineteen ninety, the city closed Forty-Second Street, and traffic engineers braced for gridlock. In a much-quoted Times story that Christmas, the city said traffic actually flowed better.

The complication is that nobody can cleanly prove Braess's mechanism caused any of it. A British study in nineteen ninety-eight, led by Sally Cairns, looked at dozens of road closures and found something different: a share of the traffic simply disappears. People change routes, times, modes, or don't make the trip at all. That's a behaviour story, not a network one. Whether the paradox actually shows up in real cities, or only on a blackboard, is still argued over.
