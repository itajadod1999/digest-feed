Starting with markets.

The July jobs report was the week, and it landed Friday morning as a shock. Nonfarm payrolls fell by twenty-three thousand. Forecasts had clustered around a gain of eighty to one hundred thousand. This was the first outright decline in months, and the revisions were worse than the headline: one hundred three thousand jobs were taken out of the May and June counts.

The damage was broad. Government payrolls fell fifty-three thousand. Restaurants and bars shed twenty-six thousand, retail nineteen thousand, finance fourteen thousand. Health care added twenty-two thousand, which is slower than its recent run.

The unemployment rate fell, to four point one percent from four point two. That sounds like good news and mostly isn't, because it came from about two hundred sixty thousand people leaving the labor force rather than finding work. Average hourly earnings rose three point two percent over the year, to thirty-seven dollars and sixty-two cents. That's the slowest wage growth since May of twenty twenty-one.

One forecaster looks very good this morning. Adam Schickling, a senior economist at Vanguard, was nearly alone in calling for something close to zero, at eighteen thousand, and in warning of significant downward revisions to earlier months. He got both.

Now set that against what the Federal Reserve had been saying all week. Lisa Cook, a Fed governor, and Neel Kashkari, the Minneapolis Fed president, both made the case for raising rates to tamp down inflation. Alberto Musalem in Saint Louis went further and said the Fed is already late. And the Middle East.

The Strait of Hormuz has moved all week without arriving. Thursday, Iran said its arrangement with Oman had been agreed in principle, with a joint statement in final drafting. The structure is a divided passage: ships enter through a route in Iranian waters under Tehran's coordination and exit through a route in Omani waters under Muscat's, with a joint coordination center managing traffic. Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi agreed to the framework over the weekend, and, according to a senior American official and a regional source cited by Axios, the Supreme Leader and the Supreme National Security Council signed off Tuesday. The channel has run through calls between the American envoy Steve Witkoff, Araghchi, and Oman's foreign minister Badr al-Busaidi, with Qatar, Pakistan and Saudi Arabia also mediating.

Friday, Iranian state media published terms. No tolls or service fees. But also a ban on American and Israeli vessels, fines of up to twenty percent of cargo value for violators, and no passage for countries deemed to have caused Iran war damage until Iran is compensated. President Trump said a lot of progress had been made and suggested an announcement could come soon.

This morning it is looser again. Araghchi says the agreement is very close, but that a full reopening depends on the United States honoring commitments from a June memorandum of understanding that Tehran says were violated. A Revolutionary Guard spokesman, Hossein Mohebbi, said the reopening has its own separate mechanism and is not tied to the Oman talks at all. Hassan Qashqavi, of parliament's security commission, said the framework is agreed but still needs approval at higher levels. Reporting describes an interim sixty-day arrangement, extendable, with mine clearance in the central corridor still under discussion. President Masoud Pezeshkian said he will strongly defend the June memorandum and dismissed speculation that he might resign.

Coverage of this differs and it is worth flagging how. Everyone agrees a route has been drafted, that Oman is the counterparty on paper, that nothing is signed, and that Iranian officials have said the strait will not go back to being the open international waterway it was before the war. Where outlets split is on what it represents: some frame it as a breakthrough taking shape, others as an agreement forming on terms the White House explicitly said it would not accept, since Trump has said he will not sign anything giving Iran control of the strait. Those positions have not been reconciled in anything published so far. A previous understanding, reached in mid-June, collapsed within weeks after attacks on tankers.

Now, three stories worth more time.

The first is the Federal Reserve, because two separate things happened to it this week and they point in opposite directions.

The first is what you already heard. The entire hawkish case at the Fed rested on a labor market that was holding up. Cook, Kashkari and Musalem all argued in the same week for tightening. Bank of America had three hikes penciled in for this year. Then payrolls printed negative, with a hundred three thousand jobs revised away from the two prior months, and wage growth at a five-year low. You cannot argue that a tight labor market is feeding inflation when the labor market just shed jobs and people are leaving it. September went from a coin flip to roughly two in five.

The second thing is that the White House moved again to remove Lisa Cook from the Board of Governors. A letter dated August fifth, signed by deputy chief of staff Dan Scavino, gives Cook twenty-one days to respond to allegations that she made false statements on mortgage applications, specifically that she declared two homes, one in Ann Arbor, Michigan and one in Atlanta, as her primary residence. That claim originated in a criminal referral last August from Bill Pulte, the director of the Federal Housing Finance Agency. Cook has not been criminally charged. She has denied the allegations repeatedly and has said the president tried to remove her, in her words, on a manufactured pretext because she refused to bow to political pressure.

The procedural history is the part that makes this a live story rather than a repeat. The administration tried to fire Cook once before. In June the Supreme Court blocked it, five to four, on the ground that she was entitled to notice of the allegations and an opportunity to respond before removal. The Court did not rule on whether the allegations have merit. So the ruling did not settle the question of whether the president can remove a Fed governor. It set out a process he would have to follow first. This letter is that process. Twenty-one days to respond, and then the administration can decide it is unsatisfied and proceed.

Coverage differs here in a predictable way, and it is worth being precise about what is and isn't in dispute. Nobody disputes the letter exists, its date, who signed it, or the twenty-one day clock. Nobody disputes that Cook has not been charged with a crime. What outlets differ on is framing: some describe it as the administration following the exact procedure the Court laid out, others as a second attempt at the same removal using the Court's roadmap. The underlying legal question, whether a Fed governor can be removed for cause and who decides what counts as cause, is unresolved.

What makes the timing matter is the collision of the two. The removal effort lands in the same week that the data undercut the case for hikes. If Cook is removed and replaced, the vote that shifts is a vote that had just been arguing for tighter policy, not looser. That is the piece to hold onto. Whatever happens to Cook, the jobs number moved the committee more than any personnel change would have this month.

The second story is what Wall Street is doing with artificial intelligence data center debt.

A group of banks led by Morgan Stanley, with JPMorgan, Royal Bank of Canada, T D Securities and Mizuho, arranged roughly fifteen billion dollars of construction financing for a data center campus in Hubbard, Texas. The developer is Nexus Data Centers. The package is about fourteen billion in bridge loan plus a revolving credit facility. The campus will be filled with Google's own chips, and the computing power is leased to Anthropic.

Those banks are now trying to move that fifteen billion into the bond market. And they are not alone. Reporting over the past few months has banks working to distribute more than fifty billion dollars of construction debt tied to data centers leased to Oracle, some through risk transfer structures where the bank keeps a slice and sells the riskiest layer to investors.

The structure is the interesting part. Anthropic is the tenant, but Anthropic is not the credit. Alphabet agreed to backstop Anthropic's obligations using Google's investment-grade rating, and to cover lease and power costs if Anthropic defaults. In exchange Google takes roughly a twenty percent equity stake in the project. Google already owns something like fourteen percent of Anthropic itself. So the money is lent against a construction asset, the rent is owed by a startup, and the rating that lets it clear the bond market belongs to the startup's largest investor, who also owns a fifth of the building.

The debt will be carved into a sequence of bond sales rather than one, mirroring a delayed-draw loan, so the developer pulls funds as construction hits milestones. That means the market has to absorb this repeatedly rather than once.

The shape here is a single-tenant, build-to-suit credit deal. The value is in the lease, and the lease is only as good as the covenant behind it. What is unfamiliar is the concentration. A commercial mortgage-backed securities pool spreads risk across dozens of borrowers. These structures carry the credit of one very large counterparty each, and the counterparties overlap: Oracle, CoreWeave, the OpenAI vehicles, now Google standing behind Anthropic. The banks are selling because their internal limits on those names are filling up. That is the signal. Not that the projects are bad, but that the lenders who know them best would like to hold less.

There is a second version of the same trade, and it closed this week. SoftBank borrowed ten billion dollars from a group of banks using its stake in OpenAI as collateral, and the cash funds another ten billion dollar purchase of OpenAI shares by October, completing a thirty billion dollar investment announced earlier this year. A margin loan against public stock is routine. You pledge shares, you borrow, and if the price falls through a trigger you post more cash or the lender takes the collateral. What is unusual is that OpenAI is private, loss-making, and has no continuous market price. There is no closing print to test a trigger against each night. SoftBank has said the deal does require additional cash if the valuation falls, which means the parties agreed on some other mechanism for establishing that value.

Matt Levine's framing at Bloomberg is the useful one. A large share of the data center financing announced over the past year is, underneath, credit extended against OpenAI's and Anthropic's future payments, with more creditworthy public companies standing in between to make the paper investment grade. Borrowing against OpenAI to buy more OpenAI is just the most literal version of it.

The thing to watch in both cases is a mark. On the data center side, pricing on the first tranche: if it clears near comparable investment-grade paper, the backstop is doing its job and the pipeline behind it moves. If investors demand a premium for the tenant rather than the guarantor, every deal behind it gets more expensive. On the SoftBank side, a down round or a large secondary printing below the trigger is what turns a financing story into a margin call story.

The third story is rent-stabilized New York.

There is a five hundred six million dollar commercial mortgage-backed securities loan secured by fifty-three rent-stabilized buildings in New York, roughly thirty-five hundred apartments, owned by A and E Real Estate. The loan was floating rate and interest only. When rates rose and the interest rate cap expired, the borrower could not refinance, and defaulted in twenty twenty-four. It has since missed more than five and a half million dollars of interest owed to junior bondholders.

The ratings firm K B R A now values the collateral at about four hundred sixty million dollars. When the bonds were sold five years ago the appraisal was seven hundred seventeen million. That gap implies losses above eighty million dollars.

The operating math explains most of it. Expenses across the portfolio are running twenty-two percent above the original projections, driven by insurance premiums and utilities. Revenue is up six percent. On rent-stabilized buildings the owner cannot close that gap through the rent roll, because the rent roll is set by the Rent Guidelines Board. And the board, under Mayor Mamdani, has frozen rents on the city's roughly one million regulated apartments.

Fitch says the freeze will strain building finances further and erode valuations. Moody's estimates a prolonged freeze could push about six percent of the city's multifamily commercial mortgage-backed securities loans toward default. K B R A says at least two other large deals backed by New York regulated properties are already projecting losses above a hundred million dollars each.

What makes this loan the one to watch is that it is not a story about a bad building. Occupancy is not the problem. The buildings are full. A floating-rate, interest-only structure was underwritten against an assumption of rent growth that policy has now removed, in a cost environment that keeps rising. Nothing about the asset has to deteriorate for the bondholders to take the loss. The gap between a twenty-two percent expense increase and a zero percent rent increase does it on its own. On regulated stock in this city, the number that decides the deal is no longer the cap rate. It is the expense growth assumption, and whether the loan survives the rent line staying flat for several years.
