One story worth more time this morning. Anthropic is going public this fall, and the number attached to it is two trillion dollars.

That would be the largest listing in history, ahead of SpaceX's one point seven seven trillion earlier this year, and double where Anthropic is valued privately today.

The number rests on one figure. Investors project that by the end of this year the company will be running at as much as a hundred twenty billion dollars of annual revenue, roughly ten times where it started the year. At two trillion, that is about sixteen times revenue. Some backers argue three trillion is defensible, which would be thirty times. For context, Palantir and Nebius trade around fifty-five times.

Here is the mechanism worth understanding. Multiples like that are not statements about profit. They are statements about the durability of a growth rate. A company growing tenfold in a year gets valued on where revenue will be in three years, not where it is. That works exactly as long as the growth rate holds, and it unravels violently when it does not, because the multiple and the growth compress at the same time.

So the things that could break the growth rate are the whole analysis. Three are visible now.

The first is price. Anthropic's top model runs roughly two and a half times the cost of the closest comparable from OpenAI. It benchmarks better. The open question is what happens when corporate customers move from experimenting to budgeting, and someone in finance asks whether the better answer is worth the premium on every call.

The second is the government. Anthropic is in court with the federal government over the Pentagon designating its products a supply chain risk, and in June the administration put temporary export controls on its most powerful models, which alarmed customers and, by the company's own account, slowed revenue growth.

The third is what it costs to keep going. Anthropic is reportedly in talks to buy a startup called Decart for six billion dollars, for technology that would cut training costs and eventually let it design its own chips. That is a company buying its way out of dependence on Nvidia before it has even listed.

What would make this concrete is the filing. A prospectus has to show the revenue, the customer concentration, and the cost of compute in one place. Until then, two trillion is a projection wearing a valuation's clothes.

The second story. The United States is about to try to finish the Iran war with money rather than ordnance, and the oil market has quietly changed hands in a way that complicates it.

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said new measures come next week, and described them as, in his words, measures like have never been seen in the history of economic isolation on a country. The design is a one-two punch: financial restrictions layered on the continuing naval blockade of Iran's ports. Iran's foreign ministry spokesman, Esmaeil Baqaei, responded that Iran has demonstrated over decades that it will not be strangled by what he called these exhausted refrains.

Iran's economy has already absorbed enormous damage. Much of the industrial base is destroyed and crude exports are severely curtailed. So the question is what marginal pressure is left to apply, and the answer runs through the oil market.

The great surprise of this war is that oil never did what everyone assumed it would. Five months in, with the Strait of Hormuz mostly shut, Brent sits roughly forty dollars below the hundred twenty-six dollar intraday high it touched on April thirtieth. There was even a stretch this summer traders called a mini-glut.

The Economist's argument this week is that the reason is China, and that it reflects a real structural shift. OPEC's power has always come from controlling supply. What China has built is power on the other side of the trade: enough concentrated purchasing, storage and refining capacity that it can decide when to buy and when to sit out. A buyer at that scale sets the price at the margin as surely as a seller does. When it steps back, the price falls even when barrels are scarce.

That is the tension inside next week's announcement. Economic isolation works by finding the buyers and making it painful to keep buying. If the dominant buyer is a country that will not take the call, the tool is weaker than it looks, and the pressure has to come from the blockade instead, which is a military instrument with military risks.

What to watch is narrow: whether the measures name Chinese refiners directly or route around them, and what Brent does in the two sessions afterward. A plan the market believes will cut supply shows up in the price immediately. If crude barely moves, that is the market telling you it does not think this will bind.
