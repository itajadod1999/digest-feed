One idea before we finish.

In nineteen sixty-seven, the psychologist Stanley Milgram, then at Harvard, ran an experiment by mail. He gave packets to people in Nebraska and Kansas, addressed to a stranger in the Boston area. The rule was that you could only send it on to someone you knew on a first-name basis, who you thought was closer to the target. Among the packets that arrived, the typical chain had about five intermediaries. That result became the phrase six degrees of separation, though Milgram never used it; it came from a John Guare play in nineteen ninety.

The circumstance is that Milgram was in his early thirties and already notorious for his obedience experiments at Yale. The small-world question wasn't his. Two researchers, Ithiel de Sola Pool and Manfred Kochen, had circulated it for years in an unpublished paper. Milgram turned it into something you could measure with postage.

The complication is the packets that didn't arrive. In two thousand two, the psychologist Judith Kleinfeld went through Milgram's papers at Yale and found that in an early Kansas run, only three of sixty arrived. In the published Nebraska study, sixty-four of about three hundred did. So the short chains described the minority that finished. In two thousand three, a team at Columbia repeated it by email, with more than twenty-four thousand chains. Only three hundred and eighty-four reached their targets, mostly in five to seven steps. Whether the world is small, or only looks small when you count the chains that finish, hasn't been settled.
