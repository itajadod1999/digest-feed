The animal in the phrase is a duck, and the picture is the hunter waiting in a frozen marsh at dawn. The trap is building it from an English adjective. Don't say this:

One story worth more time this morning: why a chief executive's affair is a problem for the whole artificial intelligence industry.

Start with what Nvidia has become. It sells the chips, but it also finances the people who buy them. Morning Brew counts about five hundred billion dollars of investments across the industry, and roughly a hundred and sixty-five billion dollars lent to neoclouds, the companies that rent chips to the big labs. DealBook calls Nvidia the central bank of A I. When a supplier lends its customers money to buy its products, its revenue and their solvency become the same bet.

Now the people. Huang founded the company and runs it with something like sixty direct reports. He owns about three and a half percent of the shares, without supervoting stock, so the board can remove him if it chooses. Bernstein's Stacy Rasgon says he is Nvidia. Nobody has named a successor. The board says it has no reason to believe national security or company secrets were compromised.

Then the policy. Huang has been the loudest voice against export controls on China. The woman at the center of this worked for a customer that American officials already suspected of moving chips into China. And now there is the report that a Commerce inquiry into that customer was moved after an Nvidia executive called. Elizabeth Warren wants tighter export laws, and as of January her party runs both chambers.

Put that together and you get Monday's market. The rest of the industry rallied because last week's selling had gone too far. Nvidia fell because the risk is specific to it: a key man, an investigation, and a political argument it may now lose.

What would make this matter more? Three things to watch: what the board's independent committee concludes, whether Huang gives the closed-door briefing House committees have asked for, and whether the new Congress writes chip rules he can no longer lobby against. CoreWeave, which Nvidia has backed, reports today. Its results are the first test of how much of this has reached the customers.

One idea before we finish.

In June of nineteen seventy-eight, a NASA scientist named Donald Kessler and his colleague Burton Cour-Palais published a paper in the Journal of Geophysical Research with an uncomfortable prediction. If enough satellites and spent rocket stages piled up in low orbit, collisions between them would create fragments faster than the thin upper atmosphere could drag them down. Each collision would make more debris, which would make more collisions. Past a certain density, the process would sustain itself, and parts of orbit could become unusable for generations.

The circumstance is what made him see it. Kessler had spent years at the Johnson Space Center working on meteoroids, and on whether spacecraft could cross the asteroid belt safely. The mathematics of the asteroid belt is the mathematics of rocks grinding each other into smaller rocks over millions of years. He realized that low Earth orbit, with thousands of objects moving at roughly seventeen thousand miles an hour, was a young asteroid belt we had built ourselves. A military analyst named John Gabbard gave it the name people still use, the Kessler syndrome.

In two thousand seven, China destroyed one of its own weather satellites with a missile and created more than three thousand trackable pieces. In February two thousand nine, an active Iridium communications satellite and a dead Russian one collided over Siberia, the first accidental collision of two intact satellites.

The complication is that nobody agrees on where the threshold is, or whether we've crossed it. Kessler himself has argued that some altitude bands, around five to six hundred miles up, may already be unstable even if nothing else is launched. Others note the big new constellations sit lower, where drag clears debris within a few years. A runaway cascade, if it is happening, would be slow enough to look like nothing for decades.
