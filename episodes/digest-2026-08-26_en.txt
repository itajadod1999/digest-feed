One story worth more time this morning.

Bloomberg reported yesterday that in twenty eighteen, months before he signed with the Lakers, a company controlled by LeBron James borrowed almost three hundred million dollars from two Midwestern life insurers advised by an arm of Guggenheim Partners. The bonds are due in twenty forty-nine and are secured by his earnings off the court, chiefly a lifetime Nike deal. Nobody has alleged anything improper about them.

Strip the name off and this is ordinary structured finance. A person with large, predictable future cash flows sells a senior claim on them. He gets three hundred million now; the buyers get it back with interest over thirty years. The interesting question is who the buyer should be.

Not a bank: banks are funded by deposits, and a thirty-year illiquid commitment is a poor use of them. Not a mutual fund: its customers can withdraw daily, and there is no secondary market in LeBron bonds. What you want is money that cannot leave, which used to mean pension funds. America has largely stopped offering those, so the industry rebuilt the thing under another name. Annuity companies sell what is functionally a private pension, hand the money to an alternative asset manager, and the manager buys long, illiquid assets that pay more than public bonds. That is most of what private credit is.

Which brings you to the part under investigation. Mark Walter runs Guggenheim and separately owns two life insurers, along with stakes in the Dodgers, the Lakers and Chelsea. Regulators and federal prosecutors are examining whether those insurers lent to his other businesses without disclosing it. After subpoenas, they disclosed more than twenty billion dollars of loans that should have been labelled affiliated and weren't. More than a billion went to new companies under a small trading firm, which lent it on to Walter's businesses at a higher rate and kept the spread. Walter has agreed to sell the Lakers.

Lending to yourself is not illegal. Mislabelling it is, because capital rules make insurers hold more against affiliated loans. So the disclosed number was wrong and the cushion was thin. Bloomberg's Matt Levine draws the line between the two halves, and it is the sharpest thing I read yesterday. Insurance money should finance strange illiquid assets, because it is best suited to hold that risk. But if one person can direct billions into whatever he likes, and nobody is watching, he may pick the assets he finds coolest rather than the ones that pay best.

The second story, shorter, and it lands tonight.

Nvidia's stock has badly lagged its own sector this year despite growth close to a hundred percent, and the reason has a name: circular financing. On the tenth of August, Jensen Huang announced a partnership with six Wall Street firms to raise five hundred billion dollars for artificial-intelligence infrastructure. A week later, Nvidia agreed to provide up to a hundred and five billion dollars of credit support so OpenAI could lease a data centre in Ohio.

The mechanism states simply. Nvidia's customers cannot afford as many chips as Nvidia wants to sell, so Nvidia helps arrange the money they buy them with. Revenue arrives immediately; the credit risk arrives later if at all. Not fraud, and not new. Vendor financing built the telecom boom, then helped unbuild it.

So what would make tonight matter is not the ninety-two billion. It is whether the customer base is widening. If the growth comes from buyers Nvidia is not also financing, the worry deflates. If not, the discount was right.

One thing to sit with this morning.

The fishing net exists because of the fish; when the fish are got, the net is forgotten. Words exist because of meaning; when the meaning is got, the words are forgotten. Where shall I find a man who has forgotten words, that I may have a word with him?

That is Zhuangzi, from the chapter called External Things, in James Legge's translation. Zhuang Zhou lived in the fourth century before the common era in the state of Song and held a minor post at a lacquer garden. The one story that survives about him is that envoys came offering him the office of prime minister of Chu, and he asked whether a sacred tortoise wrapped in cloth in a temple would rather be honoured dead, or alive and dragging its tail in the mud.

The observation I'd make is that the last line is a joke at his own expense. He wants the man who is finished with words, and the only way to ask is in words, and the request has been kept in words for twenty-three hundred years by people who did not forget them. The net got saved. The fish didn't.
