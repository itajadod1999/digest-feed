Good morning. Saturday, October third, and the week in review. Nothing new this morning, just the five days we had, sorted by what they turned out to mean.

If the week has one sentence, it's this: the inflation and jobs data both came in soft, and the bond market finished the week higher anyway.

Start there.

Monday opened with the ten-year at its highest since two thousand and seven, and by Thursday morning it had touched five point three four percent, the highest since April of two thousand and two. Britain's thirty-year gilt went above six percent for the first time since nineteen ninety-eight, and France's ten-year hit a twenty-four-year high. It wasn't just an American problem.

Then the data turned. Wednesday's inflation gauge came in at three point four percent against three point seven expected. Friday's jobs report showed just twenty-nine thousand jobs in September, against roughly ninety thousand forecast. July was revised to a loss of ten thousand, and August's strong number was trimmed from a hundred and sixty-two thousand to a hundred and thirty-three. Unemployment ticked up to four point two percent, and wages grew three percent, slower than prices.

The October rate hike, which traders treated as the likely case on Monday, now looks unlikely. Most measures put the odds below one in five. And yet the ten-year rallied on the jobs number, then reversed and closed Friday about five basis points higher. More on that in the deep dive.

The second thread was Iran, and it hardened as the week went on.

On Monday, the President rejected Tehran's seven-day plan for reopening the Strait of Hormuz. By midweek, the talks in Doha had become a fight over sequencing: Iran wants the strait reopened before nuclear talks, Washington wants the nuclear file first. On Friday, the President rejected Iran's latest proposal, said strikes could resume after the November third midterms if there's no deal, and the Pentagon ordered a third carrier group to the region.

The pressure on Iran shows. The Economist put its September oil exports under a tenth of pre-war levels, and on Friday an Iranian court jailed Hamid Rasaei, a hardline lawmaker opposed to a deal.

Britain became a second front for the story. The five men arrested near R A F Fairford are out on bail, a British-Iranian man was arrested in London on Thursday, and on Friday police charged two Iranian men over an alleged plot against Manchester's Jewish community. Police say they were in contact with someone abroad who may be in Iran.

And oil got its answer on Friday. The G seven agreed to release a hundred million barrels of crude and diesel over four months, front-loaded toward diesel, an idea France had pushed. The President dropped his threat to restrict American diesel exports in response.

Back in English, for the story worth more time this week: why soft data didn't save the bond market.

Tuesday's deep dive covered the mechanics, so briefly: a long-term yield is the market's guess at future short-term rates, plus a term premium, the extra return investors want for lending for ten or thirty years.

This week tested which one is in charge. The first should have fallen. Inflation came in below forecasts, hiring nearly stalled, and the odds of another Fed hike collapsed. On Friday morning the two-year yield, which tracks Fed expectations most closely, fell about six basis points, and the ten-year followed. By the close, both had reversed and finished higher.

So the term premium is doing the work. Three things are pushing it up.

The first is supply, and it's global. France will borrow a record three hundred and forty billion euros next year, Britain's long gilts are above six percent, and American deficits haven't narrowed. When every big government is borrowing more at once, investors want more to hold all of it.

The second is corporate borrowing, mostly for artificial intelligence. Finimize reports that four of the biggest tech spenders sold nearly eighty percent more bonds in the first half of this year than in all of last year. SoftBank sold a record eleven point one billion dollars of junk bonds. This week Broadcom agreed to lend Anthropic up to forty-two billion. All of it competes for the same savings.

The third is inflation that won't settle. Three point four percent is a miss on the downside, but it's still well above target, wages are now growing more slowly than prices, and oil is above a hundred. A weak labour market with sticky inflation is the combination where the Fed can't easily cut either.

The strain is spreading. Corporate bond spreads widened the most since March, to a six-month high. Some of the bonds Paramount sold to finance the Warner deal fell about four percent on their first day of trading. And Finimize says a third of smaller companies are struggling to cover interest bills. Mortgage rates had their biggest weekly jump in four years.

The counterargument: one week isn't a regime, some of Friday's reversal was probably positioning, and the G seven release, if it brings diesel down, would take some pressure off inflation expectations.

What would settle it? Watch whether long yields keep rising on bad economic news. If they do, it's no longer about the Fed. It's about how much debt the world is trying to sell, and at what price buyers will take it.

One idea before we finish.

In nineteen seventy, George Akerlof, a young economist at Berkeley, published a paper called The Market for Lemons. It argued that when sellers know more about quality than buyers, markets can unravel. Buyers can't tell a good used car from a bad one, so they offer an average price. Owners of good cars won't sell at that price and leave. The average falls, the price falls, and in the extreme only lemons are left.

The circumstance is the good part. Three leading journals rejected it. The American Economic Review called it trivial. The Journal of Political Economy turned it down because, as Akerlof later recalled, if it were correct, economics would be different. It finally ran in the Quarterly Journal of Economics, after he'd spent a year in India watching moneylenders charge high rates partly because they couldn't tell which borrowers would repay.

The complication is that real used-car markets didn't behave as the model said. In nineteen eighty-two, an economist named Eric Bond compared used pickup trucks with ones of the same age that had never changed hands, and found no sign that the traded ones needed more repairs. Warranties, dealer reputations and inspections may patch the problem, or the effect may only bite in particular corners. How often markets actually unravel, and how often they quietly fix themselves, is still argued over.
