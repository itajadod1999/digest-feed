Now two stories worth more time, and the first is that ten-year yield.

Five percent is not a magic number, but thresholds work on people. And this is not really a Federal Reserve story. The Fed sets the overnight rate. The ten-year is the market's guess at the average of those overnight rates for a decade, plus a cushion investors demand for tying money up that long. That cushion is the term premium, and it is what has been moving.

Three things are inflating it. Oil above a hundred dollars makes the inflation path genuinely uncertain rather than merely debatable. New government debt keeps arriving, and Tuesday's buyback came in at six billion dollars against a market that treated it as a rounding error. And confidence in the central bank is itself in question, with a President demanding cuts and a chairman who reads the data the other way.

Here is the tell that this is a term-premium story rather than an American fiscal one. Australian yields hit a fifteen-year high the same day, and Japanese yields surged. Neither country announced anything. Long-dated government bonds are close substitutes, and when investors decide the compensation for holding duration is too thin, they mark it up everywhere at once. Buyers in Sydney are not punishing Canberra. They are repricing the same risk.

What breaks the move is a soft inflation number this morning and a Fed that raises anyway, because that says the path is contained and the institution will still act. What extends it is a hot number and a Fed that flinches.

The second story is a town of forty thousand people.

Mocha sits on Yemen's Red Sea coast, just north of the Bab el-Mandeb Strait, the twenty-mile gap between Yemen and Djibouti through which everything bound for the Suez Canal must pass. Houthi forces took it on Thursday, and the geography is the point. There are two doors out of the Gulf region: Hormuz at the top, which Iran has spent six months contesting with mines, missiles and now paperwork, and Bab el-Mandeb at the bottom, which is how anything that avoids Hormuz by pipeline across Saudi Arabia reaches Europe. Mocha gives the Houthis coastline, radar lines of sight and launch positions on the Red Sea side.

The mechanism by which this gets expensive is insurance, not sinkings. No vessel needs to be hit for the route to close. Underwriters widen the war-risk zone, the premium on a single transit goes from tens of thousands of dollars to hundreds, and past some threshold the ten extra days round the Cape of Good Hope is simply cheaper. The cargo still arrives, later and dearer, and freight rates register it first.

Set that beside yesterday's figure, Saudi oil exports at a thirteen-year low because there is no safe way to ship — and that was with one door contested. What would make this matter beyond a week of headlines is a confirmed attack launched from the newly held coast.

One idea before we finish.

In nineteen seventy-nine, Charles Perrow, a sociologist at Yale who studied organisations, was asked to contribute to the presidential commission on the accident at Three Mile Island. He expected to find what commissions find: somebody made a mistake. What he argued instead, in a book five years later, was that in certain systems nobody has to. If the parts interact in ways the designers did not anticipate, and if the system is tightly coupled so there is no slack to absorb a fault before it propagates, then serious accidents are a property of the design rather than a failure of the operators. He called them normal accidents, and the implication was blunt: some technologies cannot be made safe by trying harder.

The circumstance is what makes it interesting. Perrow had no nuclear expertise and no engineering background. He got the invitation because he had written about organisational structure, and he read the reactor transcripts as an organisational document rather than a technical one. The operators had done reasonable things given what their instruments told them, and the instruments were saying something untrue about a valve.

The complication is that a serious school thinks he is wrong. Researchers at Berkeley spent the nineteen eighties aboard aircraft carriers and inside air traffic control, and reported that these organisations run genuinely hazardous technology with far fewer accidents than his framework permits. Scott Sagan tested both arguments against the near-miss record of American nuclear weapons and came down closer to Perrow. Perrow's reply was that a carrier deck is not tightly coupled in the sense he meant, which either resolves the dispute or defines it away depending on who you ask.
