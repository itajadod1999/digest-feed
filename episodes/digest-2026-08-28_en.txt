Now a minute of French, and then the European news in it.

How would you say you can't wait for something? Not that you're waiting patiently. That you want it to be here already.

Here it is.

J'ai hâte.

English gives you a phrasal verb, looking forward, and French has nothing that maps onto it, so you reach for j'attends avec impatience and it comes out sounding like the last line of a cover letter. French makes it a noun you possess. La hâte is haste, and you have it, the way you have hunger. Extend it and it stays natural. J'ai hâte d'y être.

One story worth more time this morning.

The commercial mortgage maturity wall, because the phrase has been used so loosely for three years that it stopped meaning anything, and this is the year it stops being a forecast.

The mechanism first. A commercial mortgage is usually interest-only over five or ten years, with the principal due in a lump at the end. Nobody expects to pay that lump. You refinance it, which works as long as the new loan is at least the size of the old one. That depends on the building's net operating income and the rate a lender will write against it. Move either and somebody writes a cheque for the difference.

The numbers. Roughly nine hundred thirty-six billion dollars of commercial mortgages mature this calendar year. Of the securitized slice, more than a hundred billion comes due in twenty twenty-six, and over half of that is expected to default rather than refinance cleanly. Office delinquency in commercial mortgage-backed securities passed twelve percent early this year.

Here is the part the aggregate hides. Office loans in five of America's three hundred eighty-seven metropolitan areas account for a little over a third of all office delinquencies in the securitized market. This is not a national condition. It is five cities, and inside them a specific vintage of building.

And capital has noticed. Banks originated four hundred fifty-five billion dollars of commercial property loans in the first quarter, up eighty percent year over year. Prime assets are being refinanced enthusiastically. The rest are being handed back.

So the wall is real, and it is also not a wall. It is a sieve, and what gets through has been sorted by quality rather than by date. What would make the next year worse is the thing that hasn't happened: a repricing of the good buildings. If that stops holding, the sorting stops working.

The second story, and it is a serious argument told through a silly gesture.

The President signed an executive order yesterday renaming Lake Ontario as Lake America, effective immediately. Federal agencies and mapping systems will use it. Canada will not, and legally nobody else has to.

The reaction was bipartisan and unkind. Debbie Dingell, a Michigan Democrat, called it idiotic and will introduce a bill Monday titled the Hands Off Our Great Lakes Act. Vermont's Republican governor called it petty. Officials in Wisconsin said Lake Wisconsin sounded good to them.

Underneath the joke is a trade war on a timetable. American tariffs of fifteen to fifty percent on twenty billion dollars of Canadian goods are already in force. Canada's matching retaliation, plus a doubling of steel and aluminium tariffs to fifty percent, lands on the eighth of September. Fifty percent on Canadian cars, parts and steel arrives on the first of January. Ontario's premier has threatened to cut off electricity and critical minerals. The American measures rest on Section three thirty-eight, a provision nearly a century old and largely untested, which is where this goes to court.

Single-sourced so far. But it is the first condition either side has named for restarting, and it is about the language the documents are written in.

One thing to sit with this morning.

I do not wish them to have power over men; but over themselves.

That is Mary Wollstonecraft, from A Vindication of the Rights of Woman, written in roughly six weeks in the autumn of seventeen ninety-one and published in London that January. She addressed it by name to Talleyrand, who had just proposed to the French National Assembly that girls' state education end at the age of eight. She was thirty-two, supporting herself entirely by writing and translating, which almost no woman in England did.

The observation I'd make is about the shape of the sentence, which gives something away before it asks for anything. She concedes the frame first, and the request that follows is deliberately the smaller of the two. Self-command is a thing you can ask for without frightening anyone, because it costs the people holding power nothing. It is a tactical sentence, read for two centuries as a modest one.
