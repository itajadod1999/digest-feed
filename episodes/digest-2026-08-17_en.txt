Over to technology, where the interesting number is one nobody has to report.

The quarterly capital expenditure figures everyone quotes are the part already spent. The commitments behind them are far larger, and far less visible.

Which runs straight into the grid. Today is the deadline the federal regulator gave six big grid operators to defend their rules for what is called a large load, a single site asking for more than fifty megawatts, or write new ones. Peak demand is closer to seven hundred fifty. And roughly a thousand gigawatts of large-load requests are reportedly queued for the difference, many speculative.

One storm before the deep dives.

Hurricane Lala never made landfall in Hawaii and did most of the damage anyway. One person dead, more than two hundred thousand customers without power, nearly seventy percent of the Big Island dark, and enough damage in the island's southeast to cut road access to the town with the only hospital. Behind it sits an El Niño on track to be the strongest in nearly eighty years of records, and doing damage before it has finished growing.

One story worth more time this morning. The truce between the United States and Iran expires today, and nothing has replaced it.

The sequence is straightforward. The war started on the twenty-eighth of February. In June, the President signed a memorandum of understanding with Tehran, and his officials fanned out to build support for a deal that would reopen the Strait of Hormuz and wind the thing down. That understanding ran sixty days. It ends today, with no agreement and no extension. A senior White House official calls the negotiations static. Iran's foreign minister has written that his government has not decided to restart talks at all.

The sticking point is the strait, and the numbers show how completely it has closed. Five vessels went through over the weekend, according to the data firm Kpler. The weekend before, thirty-one. About a fifth of the world's oil and liquefied natural gas moved through there before the war. Brent crude is at eighty-eight dollars, sixteen above its pre-war price, which remains the most surprising fact of the conflict.

Iran's price for reopening it, set out by a senior official, is less a negotiating position than a list of everything: an end to the war and to what it calls aggression against Iran and its allies in Lebanon, Palestine, Yemen and Iraq, an end to sanctions, frozen assets released, and compensation for wartime damage.

Here is what the Journal adds, and it changes the shape of today. Iranian and Arab officials told its correspondents that the hard-liners in Tehran never believed the June understanding was real. Their reading was that Washington and Israel wanted to take pressure off the global economy and buy time for a larger attack. So rather than wait on the talks, they spent the two months rebuilding missile infrastructure and mobilising forces, with the stated aim of making the last round of strikes too costly to repeat.

If that account holds, the truce was not a step toward a settlement. It was a rearmament window, and both sides used it.

The other constraint is American, and physical. The Energy Department said last week that the Strategic Petroleum Reserve has fallen below three hundred million barrels for the first time since nineteen eighty-three. Amos Hochstein, who advised the last administration on energy, warns that the salt caverns holding it are damaged as they empty, and that at around seventy million barrels the reserve reaches, in his words, the point of never resurrecting it. That is a hard floor under how long the oil price can be managed out of storage.

What to watch is narrow, and it is not rhetoric. Whether the vessel count through the strait moves off single digits. Whether the promised sanctions name Chinese refiners directly. And what Brent does in a session with no headline attached to it.

The second story. New York is converting office towers into apartments faster than it ever has, and one building in Midtown has become the argument about whether it knows how.

The building is the old Pfizer headquarters on East Forty-Second Street. Pfizer left in twenty twenty-three. The plan is about sixteen hundred apartments across two structures, one from nineteen oh nine and one from the nineteen sixties, with more than a dozen new storeys going on top of the older one. On the seventh of July, crews found beams buckling, and several Midtown blocks were evacuated on the expectation of a partial collapse. Emergency jacks went in at the weakest points, then new steel. An engineer on the project told Gothamist that workers had failed to reinforce columns as designed.

The mechanism is worth understanding, because this failure mode belongs to conversions specifically. A new tower is designed as one system, and every load is known before anything is poured. A conversion inherits a frame built for another purpose, usually by people who are dead, often to drawings that do not match what was actually built. So the work becomes a long sequence of temporary conditions. You cut an opening, the load travelling through it has to go somewhere else, and the steel that receives it has to already be there. The design can be entirely correct and the sequence still fail. That appears to be what happened here.

The scale is why this is not one bad site. Nineteen Manhattan office buildings began conversions this year and another seventeen are planned, according to Cushman and Wakefield. Cities everywhere have settled on conversions as the answer to two problems at once, a housing shortage and an office glut, and the abatements now in place are designed to produce many more.

What would make this concrete is the buildings department. If inspectors start requiring engineered sequencing plans and outside review for temporary conditions, the cost and timeline of every conversion in the pipeline move, and the marginal ones stop working.

Now a minute of French.

How would you say you can't be bothered? Not that you don't want to, not that you're tired. That specific shrug.

J'ai la flemme.

The textbook reach is for this one, which only manages to say you don't feel like it.

Je n'ai pas envie.

The word doing the work in the first version is a noun, so you have it rather than are it, and it means a settled disinclination to move rather than tiredness. Assembling the phrase out of the English gives you a sentence about not being disturbed, which is something else.

One thing to sit with this morning.

It is almost impossible to carry the torch of truth through a crowd without singeing somebody's beard.

That is Georg Christoph Lichtenberg, in one of the notebooks he kept from the seventeen sixties until his death in seventeen ninety-nine. He was a professor of experimental physics at Göttingen, and he took the format from English shopkeepers, who kept a waste book for rough entries before copying them into the ledger. He never copied them over and never published them, which is part of why they read as they do.

The observation I would make is that the line quietly flatters whoever holds the torch. It assumes the thing being carried is truth, and the burnt beards incidental. In most actual arguments, whether it is truth is exactly what is in dispute, and the singeing is the only part everybody agrees on.
