Now the one story worth real time this morning, and it started as a footnote in a peace deal.

The Journal reported Friday that when talks between Armenia and Azerbaijan looked like collapsing last year, American negotiators kept them alive by offering something no previous mediator has had to offer. Not aid, not security guarantees, not a seat at a table. Access to Nvidia chips.

Here is the object at the center of it. Firebird is an artificial-intelligence cloud company backed by the Afeyan Foundation for Armenia — Noubar Afeyan, the Flagship Pioneering chief executive, is a founding partner — together with the Armenian government, in a public-private partnership of about five hundred million dollars. Washington cleared the chips for export to Armenia last November. The campus is meant to reach three hundred megawatts and more than seventy thousand Blackwell and Vera Rubin processors by the end of twenty twenty-seven, with servers from Dell, and about five billion dollars invested across all the phases.

Why does that work as leverage at all? Because of a set of properties that almost nothing else in the modern economy has. The most advanced artificial-intelligence processors are designed by one company, fabricated by one foundry in Taiwan, and cannot be legally exported without a licence from the American government. There is no grey market at scale and no substitute at the frontier. So a licence is a scarce good, in the gift of a single state, that a country cannot buy its way around. That is precisely the structure that made oil an instrument in the nineteen-seventies, and enriched uranium and jet engines before that. Officials call it chip diplomacy.

It has been used before, but commercially. Last November the Commerce Department authorized exports to G42 in the United Arab Emirates and Humain in Saudi Arabia — up to thirty-five thousand Blackwell chips, conditioned on security and reporting requirements — days after the Saudi crown prince pledged a trillion dollars of investment in the United States. The longer ambition discussed for the Emirates ran to five hundred thousand chips a year and a five-gigawatt campus in Abu Dhabi. Those were trades: capital for compute. Armenia is the first documented case of the same currency being spent to stop a shooting war.

So what does each party actually want. Washington wants Russia out of the South Caucasus, where it has been Armenia's security guarantor for thirty years, and the peace framework does that structurally: it hands the United States exclusive development rights for ninety-nine years over a transit route of roughly twenty-five miles across southern Armenia, connecting Azerbaijan to its exclave of Nakhchivan. The land stays Armenian. The corridor is called the Trump Route for International Peace and Prosperity, which tells you something about how the deal was assembled. Armenia wants a hedge and a growth story after losing Nagorno-Karabakh. Azerbaijan wants the road. And Nvidia wants markets that its own government's export rules would otherwise close, which is the quiet part: chip diplomacy is also chip sales.

Now the numbers in context, because they are smaller than they sound. Seventy thousand processors is a serious cluster and nothing like a hyperscaler. Three hundred megawatts against a proposed five gigawatts in Abu Dhabi is about six percent. Armenia is not being made into a compute power. It is being given a strategically placed installation that it cannot run without American permission, which is the entire point. The compute is the hostage, not the prize.

What would have to happen for this to matter beyond one clever negotiation. Three things, and none of them has happened yet. The peace agreement has been initialed, not ratified; full ratification is tied to Armenia amending its constitution to drop territorial claims, which points at a referendum in twenty twenty-seven. The chips have to actually arrive and the buildings have to actually go up; approval was ten months ago and the first Blackwell deployment is this year. And the model has to survive its own logic. A licence that can be granted can be withdrawn, so every recipient of one now has a standing reason to fund somebody else's silicon. That is the same incentive that has China pouring money into domestic accelerators, and the reason a policy that looks like leverage today can look like a subsidy for competitors in five years.

One more caution. The Armenia negotiation detail rests on the Journal's reporting and has not been confirmed by anyone at the table. The export approval and the data centre are on the record. The claim that chips saved the talks is, so far, single-sourced.

Back in English. One thing to sit with this morning.

I have laboured carefully, not to mock, lament, or execrate, but to understand human actions.

That is Spinoza, opening the Political Treatise, left unfinished when he died in sixteen seventy-seven. He was forty-four and had spent his adult life outside every institution that might have kept him. Amsterdam's Jewish community expelled him at twenty-three with a writ so violent it forbade anyone to speak with him or read a word he wrote. He ground optical lenses for a living, and the glass dust probably killed him. Three years before the end he turned down a chair at Heidelberg, on the grounds that a salary would come with limits on what he could say.

The observation I'd make is that the sentence is a promise the book does not entirely keep. He says he will not mock, and then he is quite funny at the expense of monarchs and theologians. But the deeper problem is the assumption underneath it, which is that understanding and condemnation are alternatives — that if you traced a human action back far enough you would lose the appetite to judge it. That is not a method. It is a claim about what happens to people who look closely, and it is not obviously true.
