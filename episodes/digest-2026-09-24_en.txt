One story worth more time this morning, and it's the bond market.

Start with a statistic from Finimize. Over the past three months, the correlation between oil and the ten-year yield has reached sixty-five percent, the highest in thirty-five years. The only time it's been higher was the start of the Gulf War in nineteen ninety.

Why that matters. For most of two decades, bonds and stocks insured each other. When the economy wobbled, stocks fell, investors bought Treasuries, and yields dropped. That works when the main risk is growth.

When the main risk is inflation, the cushion disappears. An oil shock lifts expected inflation, which lifts the yield bond buyers demand, which hurts stocks, all at once. That's what Wednesday looked like: oil up, yields up, stocks down, and nothing in a portfolio going the other way.

Then add the Fed. It raised rates this month for the first time since twenty twenty-three, and Wednesday's survey said the economy is running hot, not cooling. That's exactly the case where a central bank keeps going. So the short end rose on expected hikes, and the long end rose on something else: investors wanting more to hold long-dated debt from governments that borrow enormously.

The long end is what reaches everything else. The ten-year anchors mortgages and most fixed-rate commercial property debt. It's why borrowers in the real estate section chose floating loans and short terms. They're betting today's yields are a peak, not a plateau.

What would change it is oil. If the Hormuz talks go anywhere and tankers move, the inflation premium drains out of bonds quickly and the old relationship could return. If the strait stays shut into winter, with diesel already at records, the Fed has reason to hike in October and the long end has little reason to fall. Next week's jobs and inflation data will show which way the market leans.

One idea before we finish.

In nineteen sixty-eight a German mathematician, Dietrich Braess, published a short paper with an uncomfortable result. In a road network where every driver picks the fastest route for themselves, adding a road can make everyone's journey slower. Nobody does anything wrong. Each rational choice adds up to a worse outcome than before the road existed.

The circumstance is why it stuck. West Germany was building roads at a furious pace, and planners had already noticed that some new links seemed to make congestion worse. In Stuttgart, traffic reportedly improved after a newly built stretch was closed. On Earth Day in nineteen ninety, New York shut Forty-Second Street, gridlock was predicted, and The Times reported traffic flowed better. In Seoul, the elevated Cheonggyecheon highway came down in the mid two thousands, and speeds around it rose.

The complication is that almost none of those cases cleanly proves Braess. Seoul redesigned its whole bus network at the same moment. And a British study in the late nineties, led by Sally Cairns and Phil Goodwin, found that when roads close, on average about a quarter of the displaced traffic simply vanishes. People change routes, times, or don't travel. That's demand responding, not the geometry of the network. Mathematicians say the paradox is common in randomly generated networks. Whether it's common in real ones, where people can also just stay home, is something traffic engineers still argue about.
