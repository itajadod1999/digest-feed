One story worth more time this morning: who is actually lending the money for the artificial intelligence buildout.

What happened. DealBook reports that American life insurers held about eighty-five billion dollars of artificial intelligence related debt at the end of June, up twenty-four percent in six months, on Moody's numbers. Moody's thinks that could reach a hundred and eighty-five billion by the end of next year.

The mechanism. Data centers cost so much that even the richest tech companies would rather not carry the debt themselves, so they build them in separate vehicles. The best-known example is Meta's Hyperion campus in Richland Parish, Louisiana. Meta owns twenty percent of the venture, Blue Owl the rest. The venture borrowed about twenty-seven billion dollars, and Meta leases the campus back. When the deal was struck last year, the leases were reported as short initial terms with renewal options, backed by a Meta guarantee on the buildings' residual value if it walks away.

That structure is why S and P rated the debt just one notch below Meta itself. Moody's says insurers funded about three point four billion dollars of Hyperion.

The disagreement. A recent Ares Management newsletter, your shop, for what it's worth, calls these two-faced transactions: they look nearly as safe as the tech company itself, but carry fewer guarantees of repayment than if the company had borrowed directly. Meta's own statement to the Times is blunt: Meta has not guaranteed repayment of the bonds.

What the numbers mean. Eighty-five billion is small against the trillions life insurers hold. The concern is concentration. Most of this debt traces back to a handful of borrowers, Alphabet, Meta, Nvidia, OpenAI and Anthropic among them, all tied to the same trade that lost two and a half percent in a day on Monday. And it sits with state-regulated insurers whose regulators, the Times notes, may not be keeping up.

What would have to happen next. Not a default, not soon. The test is the renewal date. If a tenant decides it needs less capacity when an initial lease term runs out, the question becomes what a single-purpose campus in rural Louisiana is worth to anyone else, and how much of the gap the guarantee actually covers. That's when the rating and the reality get compared.

One idea before we finish.

In nineteen fifty-one, a young economist named Kenneth Arrow published a short book called Social Choice and Individual Values, with a proof that bothered people for decades. Suppose you want a fair rule for turning individual rankings into one group ranking. You'd ask for modest things. If everyone prefers A to B, the group should too. The group's choice between two options shouldn't depend on how people feel about some third one. And no single person should decide everything. Arrow showed that with three or more options, no rule satisfies all of these at once. Every voting system breaks at least one.

The circumstance is the strange part. In the summer of nineteen forty-nine, Arrow was at the RAND Corporation, and a colleague there, the philosopher Olaf Helmer, asked him a Cold War question: game theory treats countries as single players with clear preferences, but a country is millions of people, so what does it even mean to say what the Soviet Union wants? Arrow expected to write down the obvious answer. Within a few weeks he'd proved there wasn't one.

The complication is how much it actually matters. Amartya Sen showed that if people can express how strongly they feel, not just their rankings, some of the impossibility loosens. And political scientists still argue over whether the cycles Arrow's theorem allows, where A beats B, B beats C and C beats A, show up in real legislatures often enough to matter. William Riker thought they explained a good deal of American history. His critics thought he found them because he went looking.
