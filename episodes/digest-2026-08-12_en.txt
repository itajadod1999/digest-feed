Before we get to deals, a small thing I liked, and it is technically a credit story.

Credito Emiliano, in Emilia-Romagna, has lent against wheels of Parmigiano Reggiano since nineteen fifty-three. The vault holds more than half a million wheels, worth well over three hundred million euros, and a dairy can borrow sixty to eighty percent of a wheel's value while the cheese sits there aging. This summer's heat has pushed the vault's daily energy use up about thirty percent. And the cows, being hot, are lying down and eating less, which has cut milk output by as much as ten percent a year. Italy's cheese economy is worth roughly four point seven billion dollars, and it is now, in the most literal sense, a climate exposure.

One story worth more time this morning. Nvidia has built a financing machine, and it is worth understanding what it actually is.

On Monday, Nvidia announced it had brought together six of the largest firms in private capital, Apollo, Blackstone, BlackRock, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs and KKR, to source five hundred billion dollars of financing for artificial intelligence infrastructure. Jensen Huang said he approached only those six, and none of them said no. Larry Fink of BlackRock said, and this is a direct quote, we need to raise this money as fast as possible. He also said he foresaw a next future for financial engineering.

Here is the structure, as far as anyone has described it. The money is not equity in Nvidia and it is not equity in the data centers. It is debt. The firms will create dedicated pools of capital, in Nvidia's words at attractive rates, for Nvidia's largest customers, so those customers can buy chips and build the halls to put them in. Executives referred to yield-based products and to securitization.

Then the part that tells you what Nvidia really thinks. Huang posted that in some cases Nvidia may provide a residual-value support mechanism for up to twenty-five percent of an opportunity. Translate that. If you lend against a pile of chips, your risk is what the chips are worth in four years if the borrower stops paying. Nvidia is offering to eat a quarter of that. Which lowers the cost of capital for anyone buying its hardware, and it is, functionally, a price cut, paid for with Nvidia's own investment-grade balance sheet.

That solves a problem for both sides. Nvidia has been criticized for months for being the de facto financial backstop of its own customers, arranging circular financing. This moves the risk onto Wall Street investors while keeping the sales. And the six firms have the opposite problem: they have raised enormous amounts of capital, increasingly from insurance books and retail retirement accounts, and they need somewhere to put it. Apollo's Jim Zelter called modern compute a scarce, mission-critical asset class. That is the language of someone describing collateral, not a technology.

For scale, Morgan Stanley expects hyperscalers to spend three and a half trillion dollars on this infrastructure between now and twenty twenty-eight.

Two things to watch. The plumbing is already being laid: the Securities and Exchange Commission has exempted data-center bonds from key securitization rules. And the binding constraint may not be financial at all. Data centers have become politically toxic in a lot of places over water and power. Money is clearly available. Sites and substations are the scarce thing.

Investors were not thrilled. Nvidia closed down almost three percent on the announcement.

One more worth understanding, and it is smaller but it has been running for six years. Apple and Epic Games.

The history in short. Epic sued in twenty twenty, Apple largely won, but the court ordered it to stop preventing developers from linking out to payment options outside the App Store. Apple complied with the letter of that and started charging twelve to twenty-seven percent on those outside purchases, at which point almost no developer bothered using the option. Epic said that violated the order. Judge Yvonne Gonzalez Rogers agreed, and held Apple in contempt.

Apple has asked the Supreme Court to review the contempt ruling, and the court hears it in October. In the meantime Apple asked the district court to pause the separate proceeding over what those fees should actually be. Yesterday the district court said no. Apple has twenty-four hours to file its proposed fee schedule, and Epic gets sixty days to respond before a hearing.

So the fee fight runs on one track while the Supreme Court considers the contempt finding on another. If the fee proceeding lands first, Apple could be operating under a court-set number in the largest software storefront in the world before the justices have said a word about whether the contempt finding was right. Procedural rather than dramatic, which is usually where these are decided.
