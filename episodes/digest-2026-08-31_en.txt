How would you say someone is wearing you out? Not that he made you angry. That he has been talking at you for forty minutes and you would like to lie down.

Here it is.

Il me soûle.

The textbook hands you il m'énerve, which is correct and lands like a complaint filed in triplicate. Soûler literally means to get someone drunk. Spoken French moved it sideways, to the particular exhaustion of being talked at. It bends: ça me soûle for a situation rather than a person.

Back in English, and to Iran, where six months of blockade turned back into a shooting war overnight.

One story worth more time this morning.

Aon is buying USI for seventeen billion dollars. Here is why that is a story and not a headline.

USI is the tenth-largest insurance broker in America: about three billion dollars of annual revenue, ten and a half thousand employees, roughly two hundred offices. Kohlberg Kravis Roberts and other investors owned it, and this is their exit. The net price is sixteen point seven billion, about fourteen and a half times trailing operating earnings once you credit the savings Aon expects to find.

Now the mechanism, because brokerage is one of the best businesses in finance and almost nobody outside it knows why. A broker takes no underwriting risk. It places your coverage with carriers and collects a commission, usually a percentage of premium. No capital requirement to speak of, revenue that renews every year because businesses do not stop buying insurance, and when premiums rise the commission rises with them for free. That is why these companies trade at multiples that look like software.

The strategic point is the phrase middle market. Aon has historically served very large corporations. USI sells to mid-sized American businesses, and that segment is more than a third of all commercial property and casualty premium written in this country. It also brings the excess and surplus market, where risks go when standard carriers won't write them: another twenty-six percent of premium, and the fastest-growing slice, because the world has more uninsurable-looking risk in it than it used to.

This follows Aon's purchase of NFP two years ago, so it is the second leg of one strategy rather than a change of direction. What would make it matter is integration, which is what brokerage roll-ups habitually get wrong. The asset is producer relationships, and producers leave. Watch retention of USI's brokers over the next eighteen months, not the synergy slide.

The second story, and it is about money you cannot see.

A Times analysis puts roughly one billion dollars of untraceable spending into this year's races, and the reporters say plainly it is a low-end estimate, because the money is designed not to be counted. Nearly every major primary and battleground race this autumn has been shaped by it, and in some the anonymous spending exceeds what the candidates themselves are spending.

The machinery has three parts. First, issue advertising: a nonprofit need not name its donors when it runs commercials about a legislative debate or a societal problem, and in practice those commercials name candidates and attack them. Second, a nonprofit hands money to a super political action committee, which does disclose donors, and duly discloses the nonprofit rather than the people behind it. No limit on that, and some committees are funded entirely that way. Third, and this is the cleanest, pop-up committees: the last disclosure deadline falls three weeks before the election, so a committee formed after it spends freely and files its donors once the votes are counted.

The four largest players are registered as social welfare organisations and account for more than four hundred sixty million dollars from hidden sources. Two back Republicans, two back Democrats, and in every case the nonprofit and its allied committee share an office and the same strategists run both.

The billion-dollar figure is the Times' own estimate, not a government finding. What is not in dispute is the structure, because the structure sits in the tax code and the disclosure calendar, and neither is a secret.

One thing to sit with this morning.

I shall consider human actions and appetites just as if it were an investigation into lines, planes, or bodies.

That is Baruch Spinoza, opening the third part of the Ethics, in Elwes' translation. He finished it around sixteen seventy-five and declined to publish. Three years earlier a mob in The Hague had torn apart Johan and Cornelis de Witt, the closest thing the Dutch Republic had to protectors; the story is that Spinoza tried to go out with a sign reading ultimi barbarorum and his landlord locked the door. He had been expelled from Amsterdam's Portuguese-Jewish congregation at twenty-three, ground lenses for a living, and died at forty-four, the year the Ethics finally appeared.

The observation I'd make is that the sentence is a bet, and not obviously a winning one. Lines and planes compose; you can add them. The claim underneath the method is that hatred and love and fear behave the same way, with magnitudes and directions that combine predictably. Most of what is interesting about a person's motives is that they don't combine, that two can be held at full strength facing opposite ways, and the holder often cannot say which one is operating.
