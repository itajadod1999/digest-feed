And one story worth more time: the forecasts for tomorrow, and why they disagree.

What happened. The Economist's model gives Democrats a ninety-four percent chance of taking the House, with an average majority of two hundred and thirty-six seats to a hundred and ninety-nine. The Senate is where they split. Betting markets give Democrats about a seventy-two percent chance of flipping it. The Economist's model gives them fifty-four.

The mechanism. Polling averages show Democrats narrowly ahead in more than enough states to win the chamber. The Economist's model doesn't take them at face value. It applies a historical pattern: when a party leads the national generic ballot by a lot, its candidates tend to win by less than the polls imply. And it weights past results in each state, which favour Republicans in places like Iowa, Ohio and Texas. Narrow leads in red states get discounted twice.

The players. Republicans are defending states that weren't supposed to be competitive, which is why the President spent Sunday in Iowa and Kansas. Democrats, by the Journal's reporting, are already trading blame between the leadership and an insurgent left over where the money went.

What would make it matter. If the polls are right, Democrats take both chambers. If the model's historical discount holds, the Senate comes down to a handful of states decided by a point or two, and possibly to Alaska, where a federal judge on Sunday threw out the administration's attempt to block certification in advance. The Justice Department appealed within hours.

One idea before we finish.

In nineteen fifty-one, Maurice Duverger, a French political scientist at Bordeaux, published a book called Political Parties. His claim: elections decided by a single round, in single-member districts, with the plurality winner taking the seat, tend to produce two parties. Proportional representation tends to produce many.

He offered two reasons. The mechanical one: third parties win votes everywhere but seats almost nowhere. The psychological one: voters, knowing that, desert them so as not to waste a ballot.

The circumstance is the interesting part. Duverger was writing in the Fourth Republic, which used a form of proportional representation and went through government after government, coalitions forming and collapsing, often within months. In nineteen fifty-eight the Fifth Republic switched to single-member districts, though with two rounds, which Duverger had said would not produce two parties, and it hasn't.

The complication is that the world keeps refusing to obey. Britain, Canada and India all use plurality voting and all have more than two serious parties. Gary Cox argued in nineteen ninety-seven that it holds district by district: two real contenders in each seat, just not the same two everywhere. And a group of political scientists argue the causation runs backwards: that countries with many parties choose proportional rules, rather than the rules creating the parties. Whether it's a law about voting systems, or about the people who write them, is still argued over.
