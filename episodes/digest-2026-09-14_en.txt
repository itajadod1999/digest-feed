Now one story worth more time this morning.

Over the past year American mall values rose thirteen percent — more than double the gain across commercial property as a whole, and the best of any property type in the country. Simon Property, the largest mall owner in America, is at record highs. That is the Journal's reporting. After twenty years of the mall being the standing example of terminal decline, it is the most counterintuitive figure I have read this month. Part of the answer is real and part of it is arithmetic.

Start with the arithmetic. Property values are measured against a basket, and the composition of that basket changes. Over the past decade an enormous number of weak American malls did not recover. They were demolished, converted to warehouses or medical offices, or handed back to lenders and written down to very little. When a property stops being a mall, it stops being counted as one. The surviving population is therefore systematically better than the original population was, and the index measures survivors. That is not fraud, it is what survivorship does to an average. But it means thirteen percent describes the malls that are left, not the mall business as it existed in twenty fifteen.

Now the part that is real, because it is substantial. The malls that survived did it by changing what they sell. The anchor department store was the structural weak point — enormous square footage, low rent per foot, a tenant in secular decline, and usually a co-tenancy clause that let every other tenant cut its rent when the anchor left. In many centres that box has been carved up and replaced with restaurants, gyms, medical tenants, entertainment and luxury retail. Those tenants pay more per foot and keep people on site longer. Luxury and grocery-anchored centres now behave like a different asset class, and are underwritten as one.

Simon at record highs matters here, because listed prices move daily while appraisals move slowly. When the listed owner and the appraisal index agree, the appraisal is usually telling the truth.

What would make this more than a good year. Retail sales land Wednesday, alongside the Federal Reserve. The spending holding these values up belongs to the same household paying six dollars a gallon for diesel. A thirteen percent gain built on resilient consumers is exactly as durable as the consumers.

One idea before we finish.

In nineteen thirty-one, Ronald Coase was a twenty-one-year-old student at the London School of Economics who had won a travelling scholarship to the United States. He spent the year visiting factories — Ford, General Motors, whatever would let him in — and came back with a question that sounds childish and is not. If markets allocate resources efficiently through prices, why do firms exist at all? A firm is a zone where the price mechanism has been switched off. Inside it nobody bids for the next task; somebody is simply told to do it. His answer, published in nineteen thirty-seven, was that using the market is not free — you have to find the counterparty, negotiate, write the contract, enforce it — and a firm expands up to the point where doing something internally costs less than those frictions. He called them transaction costs.

The circumstance is the part worth having. Coase was a young socialist, and the question came out of the argument of the moment: whether Soviet central planning could work. Lenin had said an economy could be run like one big factory, and the standard reply was that this was absurd. Coase noticed that Western economies were already full of very large organisations run in exactly that way, and wanted to know why nobody could say where the line fell.

The complication is that economists who admire the paper mostly think its mechanism is wrong. Sanford Grossman and Oliver Hart argued in the nineteen eighties that transaction costs alone cannot locate a firm's boundary, because you could write the same contract between two firms as inside one. What matters, on their account, is who owns the physical assets, and therefore who decides in the situations the contract failed to anticipate. Coase complained late in life that his paper was much cited and little used.
