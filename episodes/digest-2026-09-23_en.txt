Two stories worth more time this morning. The first is about turning an office building into apartments, and what that costs.

Five hundred North Michigan Avenue in Chicago is twenty-five storeys of nineteen sixties office on the Magnificent Mile. Commonwealth Development Partners bought it last year and is converting it into three hundred and twenty apartments, sixty-four of them affordable, finishing in spring of twenty twenty-eight. This week the capital stack closed: a seventy-one and a half million dollar non-recourse construction loan from Santander and forty-one point eight million of joint venture equity from Washington Capital Management, arranged by JLL.

Here is the mechanism, because conversions get discussed as though the obstacle were willingness.

An office floor plate is deep. Apartments need windows, so every unit has to reach an exterior wall, which leaves the middle of the building as corridor, mechanical space, or waste. Office plumbing runs to a handful of core bathrooms; apartments need a kitchen and a bathroom in every unit, so the whole stack of risers has to be rebuilt vertically through the structure. A conversion ends up costing close to new construction while inheriting a floor plan somebody else designed for another purpose.

So the arithmetic only closes if something outside the rent roll fills the gap. Here there are three such things. The building keeps its nineteen sixties exterior, which qualifies it for federal historic tax credits. The affordable units bring it into an Illinois programme that cuts the property tax assessment. And the debt is a construction loan, sized against a finished building two years out rather than the empty one standing there now.

Which tells you the conversion wave is not a market solving itself. It is a market being subsidised into a particular answer, building by building, because the alternative is an empty tower paying full taxes. Strip out the historic credit, or the state programme, and the same building stays an office.

The second is shorter, and it is about where private credit goes next.

JPMorgan Chase has been sounding out private credit firms about taking a piece of its credit card business. The idea is what the industry calls second-look lending. You apply for a co-branded card, an airline card or a retailer's, and the bank declines you; the retailer has just lost a customer it wanted. Under a second-look arrangement, a different lender takes that declined application and can approve it on its own book, at its own price, carrying its own risk. Blue Owl, Blackstone, KKR and Sixth Street have all been approached. That's the Journal's reporting, sourced to unnamed people so far.

The direction is what matters. Private credit grew up lending to companies, moved into asset-backed finance, then equipment and aircraft, and now consumers. A card receivable is about as far from a leveraged loan to a mid-market software firm as you can get and still call it credit.

What would make it matter is scale. A pilot with one retail partner is a curiosity. If it becomes the standard structure across co-branded cards at the largest card issuer in the country, a meaningful slice of American consumer credit has moved off bank balance sheets and into funds, with different disclosure and different regulators. Nothing has been announced.

One idea before we finish.

In the nineteen thirties Wassily Leontief built a table of the American economy. Rows for what each industry produced, columns for what each industry consumed, so you could trace a car back through steel, through coal, through the machinery that mined it. He called it input-output analysis, and it earned him a Nobel.

The circumstance is the war. Leontief spent it at the Office of Strategic Services, and the method fed into the work of the Enemy Objectives Unit, the economists asked which German factories to bomb. The reasoning was elegant. Don't bomb tanks. Bomb the input the largest number of finished weapons cannot be built without. Follow the columns far enough and one answer kept appearing: ball bearings, which go into aircraft, tanks, trucks and submarines alike, and which were concentrated at Schweinfurt. The raids there in nineteen forty-three cost more than sixty bombers in one mission.

The complication is that German bearing production was back to its previous level within a year. Manufacturers dispersed into smaller plants, drew down stockpiles, and redesigned equipment to use plain or sleeve bearings wherever the tolerances allowed. Mançur Olson later made the general argument: a table of fixed coefficients tells you what an economy does use, not what it can use. Substitution is precisely what the method cannot see, and an economy under attack substitutes furiously. Whether that's a flaw in Leontief's tables or in the people who read them as a targeting list, nobody has settled.
