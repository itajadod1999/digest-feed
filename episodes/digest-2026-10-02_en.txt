One story worth more time this morning: Washington wants to let ordinary savers into private markets.

What happened. The Securities and Exchange Commission has put out proposals it calls the responsible retailization of private markets. Three pieces. Registered funds sold to the public could charge performance fees of up to twenty percent of returns. Interval funds, which hold hard-to-sell assets, could offer redemptions monthly instead of quarterly. And the agency is asking for comment on an exam, developed by the industry regulator FINRA, that would let people qualify as accredited investors by showing knowledge rather than wealth.

The mechanism. Today, most private funds can only be sold to accredited investors: people with two hundred thousand dollars of income, three hundred thousand for couples, or a million dollars of net worth outside their home. Matt Levine notes that's about eighteen and a half percent of American households. Performance fees, meanwhile, have long been limited to wealthier clients. An interval fund is a compromise: it's always open to new money, but it only promises to buy back a slice of its shares, typically five to twenty-five percent, at set intervals. That's how it can own illiquid loans without a run.

The players. The managers want this badly. PitchBook says American private equity fundraising has fallen every year since twenty twenty-three, with about a hundred and sixty billion dollars raised in the first half of this year. Institutions are fully allocated; individuals are the next pool. The skeptics point to the plumbing. Money Stuff notes that some non-traded business development companies, the retail private credit vehicles, have run into trouble this year with redemption requests. And Apollo has just started publishing daily valuations for a private credit fund, as the agency urges vigilance on how these assets are marked.

What the numbers mean. Monthly liquidity sounds friendlier, but it means more frequent cash demands on assets that can't be sold quickly. Performance fees reward risk-taking. And an exam swaps a wealth test, which at least measures the ability to absorb a loss, for a knowledge test, which doesn't.

What would have to happen next for this to matter. These are proposals with a comment period, not rules. Watch whether the exam idea survives, and how the existing retail vehicles handle redemptions while yields sit at twenty-year highs. A bad quarter for one of them would shape the final rules more than any comment letter.

One idea before we finish.

In September of eighteen fifty-four, a London doctor named John Snow persuaded the local parish board in Soho to take the handle off a water pump on Broad Street. Cholera had killed more than five hundred people in the surrounding streets in about ten days, and Snow was convinced the pump's water was carrying it.

The circumstance is the good part. Almost every authority believed cholera spread through bad air, miasma. Snow went door to door. The brewery workers on the street, who drank beer, were mostly spared. The workhouse nearby had its own well, and few of its inmates died. A widow out in Hampstead, who liked the taste of the Broad Street water and had it carted to her, died of cholera. A local curate who set out to disprove Snow ended up finding the likely source: a baby's soiled nappies rinsed into a cesspool beside the well.

The complication is that the story is tidier than what happened. Snow himself admitted the outbreak was already fading when the handle came off, partly because so many people had fled. The famous dot map was drawn afterwards, to illustrate the case, not to make it. The government's own inquiry rejected his conclusion, and the handle was put back. And London's great sewer system, which did end its cholera outbreaks, was built after the Great Stink of eighteen fifty-eight by engineers who mostly believed in miasma. The right infrastructure, built for the wrong reason. Historians still argue over how much credit Snow's evidence deserves for what came next.
