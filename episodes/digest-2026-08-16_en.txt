Before the deals, something from the auction block.

At Pebble Beach on Friday, a nineteen sixty-four Shelby Cobra Daytona Coupe sold for about thirty-nine million dollars. That is the highest price ever paid at public auction for an American car, and it is not close. The old record was twenty-two million, for a Duesenberg, at the same sale eight years ago. Six of these coupes were built, to beat Ferrari, and they did. This was the one Carroll Shelby kept for himself for twenty-three years.

Moving to deals and credit.

Sixth Street Partners bought the Pier House Resort in Key West for a hundred ninety million dollars. Key West is about as supply-constrained as American real estate gets.

And a number for anyone paying a fee for stock picking. Morningstar counted the share of actively managed American large-cap funds that beat their passive equivalents over the twelve months to June thirtieth, after fees. Twenty-seven percent. The industry has spent two years arguing that artificial intelligence has split the market into winners and losers and created a stock picker's paradise. If it has, the stock pickers have not found it.

Now real estate.

The largest story is an office tower in Midtown, and that is the second deep dive, so I'll hold it.

Trepp has published something clarifying about distressed mall debt. Nearly every troubled retail loan in the securitized market was written in twenty sixteen or earlier. Loans from twenty seventeen onward are performing at close to a hundred percent. So the mall problem, discussed for a decade as a permanent condition of the asset class, increasingly looks like a vintage problem working its way out.

In San Francisco, artificial intelligence companies now occupy roughly ten percent of the office market. Four years ago that number was effectively zero.

Now Iran and the Middle East.

The story of the weekend is a dispute over three men. Iran says three of its military pilots, whose aircraft went down in the opening days of the war in March, were taken alive by Qatari forces and have been held for six months without any contact with their families. The claim came in a letter to the president of the International Committee of the Red Cross from the officer who runs Iran's committee for missing personnel, and it names them: Javad Salehi, Abdolmajid Dashtian and Omran Behraveshian.

Qatar categorically denies it. Its foreign ministry spokesman, Majed Al Ansari, called the claims misleading, and said Qatar had made contact with the pilots after they breached Qatari airspace. Doha has separately said it recovered the remains of one pilot. Both sides agree on the underlying event: on March second, Qatari air defenses shot down two Iranian bombers, the first such interception in the Gulf during this war. What happened to the crews afterward is entirely contested, and there is no independent account of it.

On the oil side, the Journal reports that the Chinese petrochemical group Hengli has become a major buyer of Iranian crude moving outside the sanctions regime, citing industry analysts, shipping brokers and the American Treasury. And Le Monde ran a column from the historian Jean-Pierre Filiu arguing that the Revolutionary Guard is not only running the military campaign but dictating terms to Iran's own president and his negotiators, which is his explanation for why the talks keep stalling.

Meanwhile the admiral running the war visited the USS Abraham Lincoln, the carrier whose conditions I mentioned Friday.

Something from a workshop in Queens before we get to the city proper.

There are more than a million street signs in New York, and every one of them is made by forty-six people in a single building in Queens. Cornelia Street, the Brooklyn-Queens Expressway, every arrow and every prohibition. Forty-six people.

Now the city.

The Health Department has published the first real evaluation of what congestion pricing did to the air, and the answer is far less dramatic than either side promised. Air quality was stable or slightly improved in the first year of tolling, continuing a twenty-year trend already underway. Crucially, the neighborhoods along the highway corridors that the environmental assessment flagged as at risk of absorbing displaced traffic did not see pollution rise. Researchers used a full year of data before tolling and a full year after, plus a control site outside the zone, to separate the program from weather, seasonality and wildfire smoke. The honest summary is that the air did not change much either way.

On Friday night a man struck a congregant at Central Synagogue on the Upper East Side. The Times reports it landing hard on a Jewish population in the city already navigating threats and its own internal divisions.

One story worth more time this morning. In Mason County, Kentucky, a mother and daughter turned down twenty-six and a half million dollars, and the reason they gave has become the organizing argument of a national fight.

Ida Huddleston is eighty-two. Her daughter, Delsia Bare, farms alongside her on land the family has worked for generations. A company assembling a site offered Huddleston sixty thousand dollars an acre for her seventy-one acres, and Bare forty-eight thousand an acre for her four hundred sixty-three. Together, more than twenty-six million dollars, for land whose agricultural value is a fraction of that. A neighbor, Tim Grosser, was offered thirty-five thousand an acre, more than four times local market value, and said no as well.

What the buyer wants is two thousand acres for a two point two gigawatt data center. That number is the whole story. Two point two gigawatts is roughly the output of two large nuclear reactors, committed to one campus. It is why the offers run at ten times farm value, and it is why the land has to be contiguous, which is what makes refusing to sell such effective leverage. A hyperscale campus needs a connected footprint next to specific transmission capacity. One holdout in the middle of the map is not an inconvenience. It can be fatal to the site.

That is the mechanism worth understanding here. These projects look like they are competing on price, and at the parcel level they are. But they are really competing for a very short list of places where enough contiguous land, enough water, and enough grid interconnection exist in the same spot. There are not many. Which is why buyers move quietly through shell entities, and why the local politics turn so fast once residents work out what is being built.

In Mason County they did work it out. A group calling itself We Are Mason County has sued over the zoning vote, arguing the approval was rushed, and making a hard-to-dismiss point: the county already has land zoned industrial that could host this, without converting farmland.

And the other half of the story is what the Journal reported the same week. All that construction is producing a real American manufacturing boom, with Caterpillar, Eaton and Ford pivoting capacity toward the equipment these campuses consume. Both things are true at once. The build-out is creating industrial demand of a kind the country has not seen in a generation, and it is running into people who do not want it on their road.

What would make this concrete is the zoning litigation. If courts start finding these approvals procedurally defective, the constraint on data center development stops being power and starts being process, and process is much slower to fix than megawatts.

The second story. Worldwide Plaza has slipped out of its owners' hands.

The tower on Eighth Avenue, one of the more recognizable roofs on the Midtown skyline, is in foreclosure. Goldman Sachs, Deutsche Bank and a trustee for holders of the building's nine hundred forty million dollar senior mortgage filed in January. A receiver was appointed in March, and as of the first of July, SL Green is no longer the property manager. Cushman and Wakefield has taken over. Losing the management contract is the moment ownership becomes nominal.

The numbers explain the rest. SL Green reported occupancy at sixty-one percent at the end of June. The receiver's own June report put it at fifty-one. That ten-point gap is not necessarily anyone lying; occupancy can be counted as leased space or as space actually being paid for, and in a distressed building those diverge fast. The receiver also reported monthly net operating income of negative four hundred eighty-four thousand dollars. The building is not underperforming. It is consuming cash. And the broadcaster WNET leaves ninety-five thousand square feet when its lease expires at the end of this month.

Here is the structure underneath it. That nine hundred forty million dollars sits in commercial mortgage-backed securities, which means the lender is not a bank that can pick up the phone and restructure. It is a trust holding the loan for bondholders in different tranches with opposing interests. The senior bondholders get paid first and want the asset sold now at whatever it fetches. The junior bondholders are wiped out at that price and want time. Because nobody can simply negotiate, the loan goes to a special servicer bound by contract, and when that fails you get exactly this: a receiver, a new manager, and a court.

Which connects to the Trepp figure from earlier this week. Office special servicing at seventeen percent is an abstraction until you can point at the building.

What to watch is the sale. Whatever Worldwide Plaza fetches becomes a public, unarguable mark for large Midtown office towers, and every lender holding similar paper will be asked to explain their own valuation against it.
