One story worth more time this morning. The politics of data centres has flipped, and it happened fast.

Eighteen months ago a hyperscale campus was something a governor announced with a ribbon. This week the Journal reported that politicians in both parties are running against them. Greg Abbott in Texas and Josh Shapiro in Pennsylvania have halted approvals or tightened oversight. Kathy Hochul imposed the country's first statewide moratorium on Monday, a year long, covering any site drawing more than fifty megawatts. And the Times went to Loudoun County, Virginia, which hosts more than two hundred fifty and whose officials now describe themselves as dependent on building more.

The polling explains the speed. Gallup has seventy-one percent of Americans opposed to a data centre in their own area. A Politico survey in New York put opposition to one within three miles of home at forty-one percent, up from twenty-four in January.

The mechanism underneath is electricity and who pays for it. A hyperscale campus is a very large, very steady load bolted onto a grid built for something else. Upgrading transmission to serve it is paid for through rates, and rates are billed to everyone. So the tax revenue lands in one county budget while the bill lands on every meter in the region, which is a fairly reliable formula for a revolt.

What makes this concrete is that a data centre lease is a fifteen or twenty-year commitment signed years before the building opens. The financings announced this summer, Nvidia guaranteeing a hundred five billion dollars in Ohio, Anthropic's nine billion in Texas, all assume the sites get permitted. Permitting is now a political question in states that were recently competing to give the land away.

The second story. America's squeeze on Iran runs through Beijing, and Beijing has said no.

Bessent said this week that China, which buys more than eighty percent of Iran's exported oil, needed to get with the programme. China's foreign ministry replied that economic warfare will not work and called for diplomatic solutions instead. That exchange is the whole policy in miniature.

Secondary sanctions work by making a choice expensive: deal with Iran, or deal with the American financial system, not both. That works beautifully against a Dubai trading house or a Greek shipowner, because the American side of the ledger is worth vastly more. It works much less well against the second largest economy on earth, whose refiners buying Iranian crude are mostly small independents with little exposure to dollar clearing in the first place. The buyers who would feel the threat are not the ones doing the business.

Xi Jinping is also due in Washington next month, and an administration that wants a summit has limited appetite for sanctioning Chinese banks in the weeks before it.

What would make this real is a designation rather than a statement: a named Chinese refiner or port operator added to the Treasury list. Until then, it is a threat about a threat.

One thing to sit with this morning.

No great thing is created suddenly, any more than a bunch of grapes or a fig. If you tell me that you desire a fig, I answer you that there must be time. Let it first blossom, then bear fruit, then ripen.

That is Epictetus, from the Discourses, spoken in Nicopolis in western Greece around the year one hundred. He was born into slavery in Phrygia, owned by a freedman who had been a secretary to Nero, and he was lame. When Domitian expelled the philosophers from Rome he moved to the Adriatic coast and taught there. He wrote nothing at all. What survives is lecture notes taken by his student Arrian, later the standard historian of Alexander's campaigns.

The observation I would make is that the analogy is doing the arguing, and it may be arguing the other side. A fig ripens without attending to the matter. That is the entire nature of a fig. If becoming good works the way ripening works, the daily effort Epictetus demanded of his students is beside the point. If it does not, the image proves nothing.
