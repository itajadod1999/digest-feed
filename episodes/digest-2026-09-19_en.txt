Three things I got wrong this week.

The first is the five percent close. On Thursday I led with the ten-year finishing a session above five percent for the first time in nineteen years and treated it as a threshold crossed, a regime marker. It lasted one session. The ten-year ended the week four basis points from where it began. I made a round number into an event, and it was a round number.

The second is the French budget. On Thursday I told you the figure was thirty billion euros of savings and said we finally knew the number. We didn't. Thirty billion was a component of a fifty-four billion euro total, which I had to correct the next morning. I reported a part as the whole because it arrived first.

The third is the sanctions bill. Thursday's deep dive told you to watch whether the President signs it promptly, which framed the congressional vote as the decisive act. By Friday the reporting had him weighing business deals with Moscow as the route to ending the war instead. Clearing Congress was the easy part, and I gave it the weight of the hard part.

Now the week's biggest thread, properly.

The Federal Reserve raised rates and the bond market barely moved. That is the story, and you can read it off the curve.

Start with the mechanism. A long-term yield is, roughly, the average of expected short-term rates over the life of the bond, plus a premium for the risk of holding it that long. So when a central bank raises the front end and the long end sits still, the market is saying something quite specific. Yes, rates are higher now. No, we don't think they stay there.

That is exactly what happened. Eleven basis points on the two-year, four on the ten-year, essentially nothing on the thirty. The gap between the two and the ten narrowed from thirty-three basis points to about twenty-five. The curve flattened, which is the shape you get when a market thinks tightening now buys weakness later.

And there is good reason to think that, because most of the inflation on the page is oil, and oil is expensive because of a war and a closed strait. Raising rates does not produce more crude. It reduces demand for everything else until the price of everything else comes down. Tightening into a supply shock is the standard way to get a recession with the inflation still in it.

Here is the complication I underplayed all week. The long end was already near five before the Fed moved at all, up from four point six five in mid-August. That climb was not about policy rates. It was about supply. Gross federal debt is around forty trillion dollars, and the Treasury's answer has been buybacks of long-dated debt in tranches of up to four billion. That is roughly ten thousand to one. The bond market is not broken; it is doing arithmetic.

The same week gave us two other central banks and the same lesson. The Bank of Japan raised to one and a quarter percent, the highest since nineteen ninety-five, and the yen fell more than one percent anyway, to about a hundred and fifty-eight. The Bank of England held at three point seven five and quietly paused auctions of its own government debt. In both cases the policy rate was the headline and the balance sheet was the news.

So what would change the picture. Futures now put about ninety percent odds on another increase by the ninth of December, and fifty-five percent on the twenty-eighth of October. If that repricing happens and the long end rises with the front end, the market has decided this inflation is durable. If the curve keeps flattening, it is pricing the recession instead. And the honest counterargument is that oil fell about a percent this week on the Saudi pipeline news. If crude keeps sliding, this entire argument quietly dissolves.

One idea before we finish.

In nineteen twelve a German meteorologist named Alfred Wegener proposed that the continents had once been joined and had since moved apart. He had the coastlines that fit, matching rock formations on either side of the Atlantic, and identical fossils of animals that could not possibly have swum it. He published the full argument in nineteen fifteen and spent the next fifteen years being treated as a crank.

The circumstance is that he was not a geologist. He was a weather scientist and a balloonist who had set a world endurance record, and he came at the problem the way an outsider does, by noticing a pattern nobody in the field considered his to notice. He also proposed a mechanism, which was his mistake. He suggested the continents plowed through the ocean floor. Geophysicists ran the numbers and showed that rock is far too strong for that. They were right.

So the complication is uncomfortable. Wegener was correct about the phenomenon and wrong about the cause, and the men who dismissed him were applying sound physics to the argument he had actually made. He died on the Greenland ice sheet in nineteen thirty, aged fifty, on a supply run to a weather station. Vindication came in the nineteen sixties with seafloor spreading, which supplied a completely different mechanism. Whether that makes him right, or makes him lucky, is a question historians of science have never settled.
